# 13. Modeling Non-Parallel Term Structure Shifts and Hedging

Duration and DV01 assume the whole curve moves together. Real curves steepen, flatten, and twist. This chapter replaces the single factor with several: key rates, partial '01s, and forward-bucket '01s — and shows how to hedge each exposure with a system of equations.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the limitations of single-factor metrics (DV01, duration, convexity) when shifts are non-parallel.
- Define key rate exposures (KR01s) and key-rate durations and compute the change in portfolio value from a set of key-rate shifts.
- Show that the sum of key-rate durations approximately equals the effective duration (for parallel shifts).
- Construct an appropriate multi-bond hedge for a portfolio's key-rate exposures.
- Describe partial '01s and forward-bucket '01s and how they relate to key-rate exposures.
- Explain volatility-weighted (regression-based) hedging in outline.

## Why one factor is not enough

A DV01-neutral position can still lose money. Classic case: long a 10-year bullet, short a duration-matched 2s/30s barbell. Parallel shifts net to zero — but a steepening, flattening, or curvature change moves the 2-, 10-, and 30-year legs by different amounts and produces P&L despite "zero duration." Single-factor metrics:

- assume all rates move in lockstep (perfect correlation along the curve);
- hide exposures that net to zero in aggregate but are large at individual maturities;
- give no guidance on *which* instruments to hedge with — any DV01 match "works" on paper.

Multi-factor metrics fix this by measuring sensitivity to each segment of the curve separately.

## Key rates, KR01s, and key-rate durations

Choose a small set of **key rates** — commonly the 2-, 5-, 10-, and 30-year par yields. Each key-rate shift moves its own rate by 1bp, tapering **linearly to zero at the adjacent key rates** (and staying flat beyond the first and last). The sum of the four shifted curves reproduces a parallel 1bp shift.

For each key rate k:

$$\text{KR01}_k = -\frac{\Delta P_k}{10{,}000 \cdot \Delta y_k} \qquad D_k = -\frac{1}{P}\frac{\Delta P_k}{\Delta y_k}$$

where $$\Delta P_k$$ is the repricing impact of shift k alone. The price change for any combination of key-rate moves:

$$\Delta P \approx -\sum_k \text{KR01}_k \cdot \Delta y_k^{(bp)} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -\sum_k D_k \, \Delta y_k$$

Because the shifts sum to a parallel shift:

$$\sum_k D_k \approx D_{Eff} \qquad \sum_k \text{KR01}_k \approx \text{DV01}$$

{: .note }
A coupon bond can have a **negative** key-rate duration. A 30-year par bond hedged… e.g., a long-maturity zero discounted off a curve fitted through par key rates can gain when an intermediate key rate rises. Don't panic if a KRD prints negative — it is a fitting artifact, and GARP has tested exactly this.

### Worked example: key-rate durations of a barbell

A \$100M barbell holds 2-year and 30-year zeros and has effective duration 7.0. Its key-rate profile (2y, 5y, 10y, 30y) is:

$$D_2 = 1.24,\quad D_5 \approx 0,\quad D_{10} \approx 0,\quad D_{30} = 5.76 \qquad \sum_k D_k = 7.0$$

Compare a 10-year bullet with the same total duration: its profile is concentrated, roughly (0, 0, 7.0, 0). Now suppose only the 30-year key rate rises 50bp:

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -5.76 \times 0.005 = -2.88\% \;\; (\text{barbell}) \qquad 0\% \;\; (\text{bullet})$$

Identical "duration," radically different outcomes — this is the steepener risk that single-factor analysis cannot see.

## Hedging key-rate exposures

To neutralize n key-rate exposures you need (at least) **n hedging instruments**, chosen so each is concentrated near one key rate (e.g., on-the-run 2-, 5-, 10-, 30-year bonds). Set the hedge face amounts so that the total KR01 at every key rate is zero — a system of n linear equations in n unknowns. Hedges constructed this way are robust to *any* combination of key-rate moves, not just parallel ones.

### Worked example: two-bond key-rate hedge

A portfolio has KR01s of \$500 (5-year) and \$400 (10-year) — it loses \$500 per bp rise in the 5-year key rate, \$400 per bp rise in the 10-year. Hedge bonds (KR01 per \$1M face):

| | 5-year KR01 | 10-year KR01 |
|---|---|---|
| Bond A (5-yr note) | \$40 | \$5 |
| Bond B (10-yr note) | \$0 | \$80 |

Short face amounts $$F_A, F_B$$ (in \$M) so hedge KR01s offset the portfolio's:

$$40 F_A + 0 \cdot F_B = 500 \qquad 5 F_A + 80 F_B = 400$$

From the first equation $$F_A = 12.5$$. Substitute: $$5(12.5) + 80 F_B = 400 \Rightarrow F_B = \frac{337.5}{80} = 4.22$$.

Short **\$12.5M of Bond A** and **\$4.22M of Bond B**. Note the order of solution: start with the equation containing only one unknown, then back-substitute — exam systems are usually triangular like this.

## Partial '01s and forward-bucket '01s

Swap desks use finer grids than four key rates:

- **Partial '01s:** shift one *fitting instrument's* rate (e.g., each par swap rate used to build the curve) by 1bp, refit the curve, reprice. One '01 per curve input — often 10–20 buckets. The sum of partial '01s ≈ total '01.
- **Forward-bucket '01s:** shift all **forward rates** within a bucket (e.g., years 0–2, 2–5, 5–10, 10–30) by 1bp and reprice. This expresses risk in terms of forward-curve segments — natural for instruments priced off forwards — and again the bucket '01s sum to the total.

All three approaches (key rates, partial '01s, forward buckets) are the same idea at different resolutions: decompose the curve into pieces, measure a '01 per piece, hedge piece by piece.

### Choosing the resolution

| Approach | What gets shifted | Typical user |
|---|---|---|
| Key rates (2/5/10/30) | Par yields, tent-shaped | Treasury/government portfolios |
| Partial '01s | Each curve-fitting instrument's rate | Swap desks |
| Forward-bucket '01s | All forwards in a maturity bucket | Forward-sensitive books |

More buckets means more faithful risk capture but more hedge instruments, more transaction costs, and noisier individual exposures. The exam tests the *concepts and the sums-to-total property*, not curve fitting.

{: .tip }
If a question gives a table of KR01s and a set of key-rate moves, the answer is a dot product: $$\Delta P = -\sum_k \text{KR01}_k \times \Delta y_k^{(bp)}$$. Watch for one rate falling while another rises — the signs do the work.

## Volatility-weighted hedging

A DV01 hedge implicitly assumes the position's yield and the hedge's yield move **one-for-one**. If empirically the 20-year moves 0.9bp when the 30-year moves 1bp, scale the hedge:

$$N = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{position}}{\text{DV01}_{hedge}} \times \beta$$

where β is the regression slope (change in position yield per unit change in hedge yield) — a **volatility-weighted** or regression-based hedge. It minimizes P&L variance on average but introduces model risk: β is estimated from history and drifts.

{: .warning }
Sign discipline is where these problems are lost. A *positive* KR01 means you **lose** when that rate rises (long-bond convention), so the hedge must be a *short* position with matching KR01s — or equivalently, solve for hedge KR01s equal in magnitude and opposite in sign. Decide the convention in line one and carry it through; flipping mid-solution turns a correct \$12.5M short into a \$12.5M long.

## Quick quiz

**1.** A portfolio's key-rate durations are 0.8 (2y), 1.5 (5y), 3.2 (10y), and 1.5 (30y). Its effective duration is approximately:
- A. 3.2
- B. 7.0
- C. 1.75
- D. Cannot be determined from key-rate durations

**2.** A long 10-year bullet hedged to zero DV01 with a short 2s/30s barbell loses money when:
- A. The curve shifts up in parallel
- B. The curve shifts down in parallel
- C. The curve's curvature changes (2y and 30y fall relative to 10y)
- D. Nothing — zero DV01 means fully hedged

**3.** In the standard key-rate framework, the 5-year key-rate shift is:
- A. A 1bp parallel move in all rates from 0 to 5 years
- B. A 1bp move at 5 years, declining linearly to zero at the 2- and 10-year points
- C. A 1bp move at 5 years only, with all other maturities unchanged
- D. A 1bp move in all forward rates between years 2 and 10

**4.** A portfolio has a 10-year KR01 of +\$2,400. Hedge Bond X has a 10-year KR01 of \$60 per \$1M face (other KR01s ≈ 0). The hedge is:
- A. Buy \$40M face of X
- B. Sell \$40M face of X
- C. Sell \$4M face of X
- D. Buy \$24M face of X

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B.** Key-rate durations sum to the effective (parallel-shift) duration: 0.8 + 1.5 + 3.2 + 1.5 = 7.0.

**2. C.** The position is flat to parallel moves by construction; it is short convexity/curvature — it loses when the wings rally relative to the belly (or equivalently when the butterfly moves against it).

**3. B.** Key-rate shifts are tent-shaped: peak of 1bp at the key maturity, linear decay to zero at adjacent key rates, flat extension beyond the end key rates.

**4. B.** Need an offsetting −\$2,400 KR01: 2,400 / 60 = 40 → short \$40M face. Buying doubles the exposure.

</details>
