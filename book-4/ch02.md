# 2. Calculating and Applying VaR

GARP tests the three estimation methods (delta-normal, historical simulation, Monte Carlo), when each works, the square-root-of-time rule, and the logic of backtesting. Expect at least one historical-simulation rank question and one scaling question.

## Learning objectives

- Explain and calculate VaR using parametric (delta-normal), historical simulation, and Monte Carlo approaches.
- Compute portfolio VaR for linear positions using volatilities and correlations.
- Describe the limitations of the delta-normal method for non-linear (option) portfolios.
- Apply the square-root-of-time rule to scale VaR across horizons and state its assumptions.
- Describe how VaR models are backtested by comparing exceptions to expected exceedances.
- Explain how ES is estimated under historical simulation.

## Linear vs non-linear portfolios

- **Linear:** portfolio value changes proportionally with risk-factor changes (stocks, FX spot, forwards, roughly bonds for small moves). If risk factors are jointly normal, portfolio P&L is normal → delta-normal VaR is exact.
- **Non-linear:** options and anything with convexity. Delta approximation breaks down for large moves; the P&L distribution is skewed even with normal underlying returns. Use full revaluation (historical sim or Monte Carlo).

## Delta-normal (parametric) VaR

Map each position to dollar exposure × risk-factor volatility, then aggregate with correlations. For two positions with dollar standard deviations $$\sigma_1, \sigma_2$$ and correlation ρ:

$$\sigma_P = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + 2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}$$

$$\text{VaR}_\alpha = z_\alpha \,\sigma_P$$

For an option, the linear mapping uses delta: dollar exposure ≈ $$\Delta \times S \times$$ (number of underlying units), so $$\sigma_{\$} = \Delta \cdot S \cdot \sigma_S$$. This ignores gamma — fine for small moves only.

**Pros:** fast, analytic, easy aggregation. **Cons:** assumes normality (thin tails), linearizes options, correlations unstable in stress.

### Worked example: two-asset delta-normal VaR

Positions: \$60M in asset 1 (daily σ = 2%), \$40M in asset 2 (daily σ = 1%), correlation 0.3.

Dollar σs: $$\sigma_1 = 60 \times 0.02 = \$1.2\text{M}, \quad \sigma_2 = 40 \times 0.01 = \$0.4\text{M}$$

$$\sigma_P = \sqrt{1.2^2 + 0.4^2 + 2(0.3)(1.2)(0.4)} = \sqrt{1.44 + 0.16 + 0.288} = \sqrt{1.888} = \$1.374\text{M}$$

**99% one-day VaR:** $$2.326 \times 1.374 = \$3.196\text{M}$$

Undiversified VaR = 2.326 × (1.2 + 0.4) = \$3.722M → **diversification benefit** = \$0.526M. (With ρ = 1 the portfolio VaR equals the sum; with ρ < 1 it is strictly smaller — delta-normal VaR is subadditive.)

## Historical simulation

Revalue today's portfolio under each of the last *n* days' risk-factor changes, producing *n* simulated P&Ls. Sort losses; VaR is the loss at the appropriate rank. With *n* scenarios at confidence α, a common convention (Hull/GARP) takes the $$n(1-\alpha)$$-th worst loss; ES is the average of the worst $$n(1-\alpha)$$ losses.

**Pros:** no distributional assumption, captures fat tails and non-linearity (full revaluation), easy to explain. **Cons:** limited by sample (the future must look like the recent past), ghost effects as big days drop out of the window, poor at extreme confidence levels with short windows.

### Worked example: historical-simulation VaR and ES

100 daily simulated losses, the six worst (in \$K): 1,420; 1,180; 990; 870; 760; 700.

**95% VaR:** rank = 100 × 0.05 = 5th worst loss = **\$760K**.

**95% ES:** average of the worst five = $$\frac{1{,}420 + 1{,}180 + 990 + 870 + 760}{5} = \frac{5{,}220}{5} = \$1{,}044\text{K}$$

{: .note }
Conventions differ (5th vs 6th worst, interpolation). GARP questions state or imply one — with $$n(1-\alpha)$$ an integer, use that rank, and average that many worst losses for ES.

## Monte Carlo simulation

Specify stochastic processes for risk factors (with estimated parameters and correlations), simulate thousands of paths, fully revalue the portfolio on each, read VaR/ES off the simulated distribution.

**Pros:** handles any instrument, any distribution, any horizon; no data-window limit. **Cons:** computationally heavy, exposed to model risk (you get out what you put in), sampling error unless many trials.

| | Delta-normal | Historical sim | Monte Carlo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distributional assumption | Normal | None (empirical) | Whatever you specify |
| Non-linearity | Poor (delta only) | Good (full reval) | Good (full reval) |
| Speed | Fastest | Moderate | Slowest |
| Main risk | Thin tails | Unrepresentative window | Model misspecification |

## Scaling VaR over time: the square-root rule

If daily returns are i.i.d. with zero mean, variances add, so:

$$\text{VaR}_{T\text{-day}} = \text{VaR}_{1\text{-day}} \times \sqrt{T}$$

Assumptions: **i.i.d.** returns (no autocorrelation, constant volatility) and zero drift. Positive autocorrelation (trending) → √t **understates** true risk; mean reversion → overstates. Basel's traditional market-risk charge scaled 1-day 99% VaR by √10.

### Worked example: time scaling

One-day 99% VaR is \$1.2M. The 10-day 99% VaR under the square-root rule:

$$1.2 \times \sqrt{10} = 1.2 \times 3.1623 = \$3.79\text{M}$$

Going the other way: an annual VaR computed from σ_annual = 25% on \$50M at 95% is 1.645 × 0.25 × 50 = \$20.56M; the corresponding 1-day VaR (252 trading days) is $$20.56/\sqrt{252} = \$1.30\text{M}$$.

{: .warning }
Scale by $$\sqrt{T}$$, **never by T**. And the rule applies to σ and VaR, not to variance (variance scales by T). Mixing these up — multiplying VaR by 10 instead of √10, or σ by 252 instead of √252 — is the most common distractor in scaling questions.

## Backtesting intuition

Compare realized daily losses against the VaR forecast over, say, 250 days and count **exceptions** (days the loss exceeded VaR).

- Expected exceptions = $$n(1-\alpha)$$: at 99% over 250 days, expect **2.5**.
- Too many exceptions → model understates risk; far too few → overly conservative (also a problem: wasted capital).
- The number of exceptions is binomial $$(n, 1-\alpha)$$; statistical tests (e.g., Kupiec) judge whether the observed count is plausible. Basel's traffic-light approach: green ≤ 4 exceptions, yellow 5–9 (capital multiplier rises), red ≥ 10.
- With high confidence levels, exceptions are rare → **low power**: hard to distinguish a good 99% model from a bad one quickly. Backtesting at 95% gives more observations.

{: .tip }
Also check **independence** of exceptions: clustered exceptions signal the model misses volatility dynamics even if the total count looks fine.

## Quick quiz

**1.** A desk holds \$25M in an index with daily volatility 1.6%. Its 95% one-day VaR is closest to:
- A. \$0.658M
- B. \$0.784M
- C. \$0.930M
- D. \$0.400M

**2.** From 500 historical-simulation losses, the 99% VaR is best estimated by the:
- A. Worst loss
- B. 5th worst loss
- C. 10th worst loss
- D. Average of all losses

**3.** A one-day 99% VaR of \$2M corresponds to a 25-day VaR (i.i.d. returns) of:
- A. \$50M
- B. \$10M
- C. \$31.6M
- D. \$4M

**4.** Which method is *least* suitable for a large portfolio of exotic options?
- A. Delta-normal
- B. Historical simulation with full revaluation
- C. Monte Carlo with full revaluation
- D. They are equally suitable

**1. A.** 1.645 × 0.016 × 25M = \$0.658M. (\$0.930M uses z = 2.326; \$0.784M uses 1.96.)

**2. B.** 500 × 0.01 = 5th worst loss under the standard convention.

**3. B.** 2 × √25 = 2 × 5 = \$10M. (\$50M multiplies by 25 — the classic trap.)

**4. A.** Delta-normal linearizes payoffs; option convexity and skewed P&L are lost.
