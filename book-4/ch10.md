# 10. Interest Rates

Spot, forward, and par rates and the compounding conventions that connect them. GARP tests rate conversions between compounding frequencies and the spot→forward→par chain — pure calculator territory.

## Learning objectives

- Calculate and compare interest rates under annual, semiannual, monthly, and continuous compounding; convert between conventions.
- Define spot (zero) rates and relate them to discount factors.
- Define forward rates, derive them from spot rates, and interpret them.
- Define par rates and compute them from discount factors.
- Explain the relationships among spot, forward, and par curves (upward/downward-sloping cases).
- Apply a flat term structure and value cash flows under different conventions.
- Describe maturity-matching and the use of forwards to lock in future borrowing/lending rates.

## Compounding conventions

\$1 invested at quoted rate r for T years grows to:

- m-times-per-year compounding: $$\left(1 + \frac{r}{m}\right)^{mT}$$
- Continuous compounding: $$e^{rT}$$

**Conversions.** Equate terminal wealth. From m-compounded $$r_m$$ to continuous $$r_c$$ and back:

$$r_c = m \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right), \qquad r_m = m\left(e^{r_c/m} - 1\right)$$

Between frequencies m and k: $$\left(1+\frac{r_m}{m}\right)^{m} = \left(1+\frac{r_k}{k}\right)^{k}$$.

More frequent compounding → lower quoted rate for the same effective return: $$r_{annual} > r_{semi} > r_{monthly} > r_{cont}$$ for the same wealth outcome.

### Worked example: conversions

8% with semiannual compounding. (a) Continuous equivalent:

$$r_c = 2\ln(1.04) = 2 \times 0.039221 = 7.844\%$$

(b) Annual-compounding (effective) equivalent: $$(1.04)^2 - 1 = 8.16\%$$.

(c) Monthly equivalent: $$r_{12} = 12\left[(1.04)^{2/12} - 1\right] = 12(1.006558 - 1) = 7.870\%$$.

Sanity check the ordering: 8.16% (annual) > 8.00% (semi) > 7.87% (monthly) > 7.84% (continuous). 

## Spot (zero) rates

The spot rate $$z(t)$$ is the rate on a zero-coupon investment maturing at t — the rate embedded in the discount factor. Semiannual convention (standard for US fixed income):

$$d(t) = \frac{1}{\left(1 + \frac{z(t)}{2}\right)^{2t}}, \qquad \text{continuous: } d(t) = e^{-z(t)\,t}$$

Bond price = sum of cash flows discounted at *each* cash flow's own spot rate.

## Forward rates

The forward rate $$f(t_1, t_2)$$ is the rate agreed today for the period $$t_1 \to t_2$$. No-arbitrage: investing to $$t_2$$ directly must equal investing to $$t_1$$ then rolling at the forward:

$$\left(1+\frac{z_2}{2}\right)^{2t_2} = \left(1+\frac{z_1}{2}\right)^{2t_1}\left(1+\frac{f}{2}\right)^{2(t_2-t_1)}$$

Continuous compounding gives the clean version:

$$f(t_1,t_2) = \frac{z_2 t_2 - z_1 t_1}{t_2 - t_1}$$

Interpretation: the forward is the **marginal** rate for extending maturity. Spot rates are *averages* of forwards, so: forwards above spots ⟺ spot curve rising; the forward curve amplifies the spot curve's slope.

### Worked example: spot → forward

1-year spot 3.00%, 2-year spot 3.50% (annual compounding). Forward rate for year 2:

$$1 + f = \frac{(1.035)^2}{1.03} = \frac{1.071225}{1.03} = 1.040024 \;\Rightarrow\; f = 4.00\%$$

With continuous compounding the shortcut gives $$f = (0.035 \times 2 - 0.03 \times 1)/1 = 4.00\%$$ as well (small convention differences appear at more decimals). The forward (4.00%) sits above both spots — consistent with an upward-sloping curve.

## Par rates

The par rate $$c(T)$$ is the coupon rate making a T-maturity bond price exactly 100. With semiannual coupons and discount-factor sum $$A(T) = \sum_{i} d(t_i)$$ (the annuity factor):

$$\frac{c(T)}{2} \cdot A(T) \cdot 100 + 100 \cdot d(T) = 100 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c(T) = \frac{2\left(1 - d(T)\right)}{A(T)}$$

### Worked example: par rate from discount factors

d(0.5) = 0.9852, d(1.0) = 0.9690.

$$c(1) = \frac{2(1 - 0.9690)}{0.9852 + 0.9690} = \frac{0.0620}{1.9542} = 3.17\%$$

A 1-year bond paying a 3.17% semiannual coupon prices at par: check 1.586(0.9852) + 101.586(0.9690) = 1.563 + 98.437 = 100.00. ✓

## Curve relationships

| Curve shape | Ordering |
|---|---|
| Upward-sloping | forward > spot > par |
| Downward-sloping | forward < spot < par |
| Flat | forward = spot = par |

Why par < spot when rising: a coupon bond's yield-like par rate is a blend that weights *earlier* (lower) spot rates through its coupons; a zero feels only the final (highest) rate.

{: .warning }
Convention traps: (1) match the compounding convention of the *question* — plugging a semiannual rate into $$e^{rT}$$ silently overstates growth; (2) in semiannual problems, always divide rates by 2 and double the periods — using $$(1+z)^t$$ on a semiannual-quoted rate is the most common error in the spot/forward chain; (3) forward rates are *break-even* rates, not forecasts — realized future spots need not equal them.

## Locking in rates with forwards

A borrower needing funds from $$t_1$$ to $$t_2$$ can lock today's forward by going long the $$t_1$$ zero and short the $$t_2$$ zero (or via an FRA/futures). Maturity matching: choosing asset maturities to match liability dates removes reinvestment/rollover rate risk — the term-structure analog of immunization.

- A. 2.8%
- B. 3.2%

- D. 4.0%

**3.** With an upward-sloping spot curve, the par rate at maturity T is:
- A. Above the T-year spot rate
- B. Below the T-year spot rate
- C. Equal to the T-year spot rate
- D. Above the T-year forward rate

**4.** \$100 grows for 2 years at 5% continuously compounded. Terminal value:
- A. \$110.25
- B. \$110.52
- C. \$110.00
- D. \$110.38
