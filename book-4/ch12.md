# 12. Applying Duration, Convexity, and DV01

The one-factor toolkit: DV01, the duration family, convexity, and how to hedge with them. This is among the most heavily calculated chapters in Part I — expect a price-change estimate, a hedge ratio, and a barbell-vs-bullet question.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the one-factor interest rate model assumption underlying DV01, duration, and convexity.
- Define, compute, and apply DV01 and DV01-based hedge ratios.
- Distinguish Macaulay, modified, and effective duration; state the relationships and when each applies.
- Define dollar duration and use it for portfolio risk.
- Compute convexity and effective convexity and use the duration-plus-convexity price change estimate.
- Compute portfolio duration and convexity as value-weighted averages.
- Compare barbell and bullet portfolios with equal duration and explain why the barbell has more convexity.

## The one-factor assumption

DV01, duration, and convexity all assume rate changes are driven by **one factor**: every yield along the curve moves by the same (usually parallel) shift. The metrics answer "what happens if *the* rate moves Δy" — they say nothing about steepeners, flatteners, or twists. Chapter 13 relaxes this.

## DV01

**DV01** (dollar value of an '01) is the price change for a one-basis-point decline in yield:

$$\text{DV01} = -\frac{\Delta P}{10{,}000 \cdot \Delta y}$$

with Δy in decimal (so 10,000·Δy converts to basis points). The sign convention makes DV01 positive for ordinary long bond positions: yields down → price up. In practice DV01 is estimated by repricing the bond at y ± 0.5bp (or ± 1bp) and taking the difference.

**DV01 hedge ratio.** To neutralize a position with a hedging instrument:

$$N = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{position}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{hedge}}}$$

The minus sign means the hedge takes the opposite side: long the position → short the hedge. After hedging, the combined DV01 is zero — for a parallel one-basis-point move. The hedge does **not** protect against curve reshaping or large moves (convexity mismatch).

### Worked example: DV01 hedge contract count

A trader is long a bond portfolio with DV01 = \$12,500. Treasury note futures have a DV01 of \$85 per contract. The hedge:

$$N = -\frac{12{,}500}{85} = -147.06$$

Sell **147 contracts**. If the curve shifts up 5bp in parallel, the portfolio loses about 5 × \$12,500 = \$62,500 while the short futures gain about 5 × 147 × \$85 = \$62,475 — flat to within rounding.

## The duration family

**Macaulay duration** — value-weighted average time (in years) to the bond's cash flows:

$$D_{Mac} = \frac{\sum_t t \cdot PV(CF_t)}{P}$$

It is the holding horizon at which price risk and reinvestment risk offset (immunization). For a zero-coupon bond, $$D_{Mac} = T$$.

**Modified duration** — percentage price sensitivity to yield:

$$D_{Mod} = \frac{D_{Mac}}{1 + y/m} \qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{Mod}\,\Delta y$$

where m is the compounding frequency. With continuous compounding, Macaulay and modified duration coincide.

**Effective duration** — numerical, computed by shifting the *curve* and repricing:

$$D_{Eff} = \frac{P_{-} - P_{+}}{2 \cdot P \cdot \Delta y}$$

where $$P_{-}$$ and $$P_{+}$$ are prices after a downward and upward shift. Use effective duration for bonds whose **cash flows change with rates** — callables, putables, MBS — where the yield-based formulas break down. For option-free bonds, effective ≈ modified.

**Dollar duration** scales by price: $$DD = D_{Mod} \cdot P$$, so $$\Delta P \approx -DD \cdot \Delta y$$. DV01 is just dollar duration per basis point:

$$\text{DV01} = \frac{D_{Mod} \cdot P}{10{,}000}$$

{: .note }
Duration ranking intuition: longer maturity → higher duration; lower coupon → higher duration; lower yield → higher duration. A zero is the maximum-duration bond for its maturity.

## Convexity

Duration is a linear (first-order) approximation; **convexity** captures curvature:

$$C = \frac{1}{P}\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} \qquad \text{Effective: } C_{Eff} = \frac{P_{+} + P_{-} - 2P}{P \cdot (\Delta y)^2}$$

The second-order price change estimate:

$$\Delta P \approx -D \cdot P \cdot \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C \cdot P \cdot (\Delta y)^2$$

For an option-free bond C > 0, so the convexity term **adds** value in both directions: it cushions losses when rates rise and amplifies gains when rates fall. Duration alone therefore *overstates* losses and *understates* gains. Convexity matters more for large Δy (the term is quadratic).

### Worked example: price change with duration and convexity

A bond has P = \$100, modified duration 7.0, convexity 60. Rates rise 100bp (Δy = +0.01):

$$\Delta P \approx -7.0 \times 100 \times 0.01 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 60 \times 100 \times (0.01)^2 = -7.00 + 0.30 = -\$6.70$$

Duration alone predicts −\$7.00; convexity claws back \$0.30. For a 100bp *fall*: +7.00 + 0.30 = +\$7.30. Note the asymmetry — gain exceeds loss, the signature of positive convexity.

{: .warning }
Units are the classic trap. Δy must be in **decimal** (100bp = 0.01), and the convexity term needs the **½**. If convexity is quoted "per 100" or in per-period terms, rescale before plugging in. Also: a duration of 7 means a 1% yield change moves price about 7% — not 7 dollars unless P = 100.

## Portfolio duration and convexity

For a portfolio of bonds with values $$P_i$$ and total value P, duration and convexity are **value-weighted averages**:

$$D_{port} = \sum_i \frac{P_i}{P} D_i \qquad C_{port} = \sum_i \frac{P_i}{P} C_i$$

Equivalently, portfolio DV01s simply add. This holds exactly only under the one-factor (parallel shift) assumption — if the 2-year and 30-year yields move differently, a single portfolio duration misleads.

## Barbell vs bullet

A **bullet** concentrates in one intermediate maturity; a **barbell** splits between short and long maturities. Choose barbell weights so durations match:

$$w \cdot D_{short} + (1-w) \cdot D_{long} = D_{bullet}$$

With equal duration and equal value, the **barbell has more convexity** — convexity rises roughly with maturity squared (dispersion of cash flows), and the barbell's cash flows are more dispersed. Consequences:

- Large parallel moves (either direction): barbell outperforms.
- The market prices this in: the barbell typically has a **lower yield** — convexity isn't free.
- Curve steepening hurts the barbell's long leg; the comparison is only clean for parallel shifts.

### Worked example: building a duration-matched barbell

A trader holds \$50 million of a bullet 10-year bond with duration 7.0 and wants the same duration from a barbell of a 2-year (duration 1.9) and a 30-year (duration 16.5):

$$1.9w + 16.5(1-w) = 7.0 \;\Rightarrow\; 16.5 - 14.6w = 7.0 \;\Rightarrow\; w = \frac{9.5}{14.6} = 0.6507$$

Invest 65.07% (\$32.53M) in the 2-year and 34.93% (\$17.47M) in the 30-year. Durations match, so small parallel shifts produce the same P&L — but the barbell's higher convexity means it outperforms for a ±100bp jump and likely carries a lower portfolio yield.

{: .tip }
Exam shortcut: "same duration, which has more convexity / which wins in a big parallel move?" → barbell, both times. "Which has the higher yield?" → usually the bullet.

## Quick quiz

**1.** A bond has price \$98, modified duration 5.2, convexity 40. The estimated price change for a 150bp yield increase is closest to:
- A. −\$7.64
- B. −\$7.19
- C. −\$6.96
- D. −\$8.10

**2.** A portfolio has DV01 of \$9,000. A hedging bond has DV01 of \$0.072 per \$100 face. The face amount of the hedge bond to short is closest to:
- A. \$1.25 million
- B. \$12.5 million
- C. \$6.48 million
- D. \$125 million

**3.** Effective duration rather than modified duration must be used for:
- A. Zero-coupon Treasuries
- B. Callable bonds
- C. Par bonds
- D. Premium bonds

**4.** A barbell and a bullet have identical market value and duration. Following a 200bp parallel downward shift, the barbell will:
- A. Underperform, because of its longer cash flows
- B. Outperform, because it has greater convexity
- C. Match the bullet exactly
- D. Outperform only if the curve also steepens

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B.** ΔP = −5.2 × 98 × 0.015 + ½ × 40 × 98 × 0.015² = −7.644 + 0.441 = −\$7.20. Duration alone gives A; forgetting the ½ gives a too-large cushion.

**2. B.** Hedge DV01 per \$100 face is \$0.072, so per \$1 face it is 0.00072. Face = 9,000 / 0.00072 × … more directly: 9,000 / 0.072 = 125,000 "hundreds" of face = \$12.5 million.

**3. B.** Embedded options make cash flows rate-dependent; only repricing-based (effective) measures capture that. Option-free bonds are fine with modified duration.

**4. B.** Same duration → same first-order P&L; the barbell's extra convexity adds value for any large parallel move, up or down.
