# 5. Country Risk: Determinants, Measures, and Implications

GARP tests the sources of country risk, why sovereigns default (including on local-currency debt), and how sovereign ratings, default spreads, and CDS spreads compare as risk measures. This is a conceptual chapter — expect "which statement is correct" questions.

## Learning objectives

- Identify sources of country risk: economic structure, political risk, legal system, and economic maturity.
- Describe how country risk is measured: risk services, rating agencies, and market-based measures.
- Describe the history and patterns of sovereign default, both foreign-currency and local-currency.
- Explain the consequences and causes of sovereign default.
- Distinguish foreign-currency vs local-currency sovereign ratings; describe the rating process and its criticisms.
- Compare sovereign ratings with sovereign default spreads and CDS spreads as measures of sovereign risk.

## Sources of country risk

| Source | What to look at |
|---|---|
| **Economic structure** | Growth stage and volatility; commodity/sector concentration (oil exporters live and die with prices); diversification |
| **Political risk** | Continuity vs discontinuity: democracies deliver gradual policy change, autocracies stability-then-rupture; corruption (a stealth tax); expropriation/nationalization risk; war and internal conflict |
| **Legal system** | Property-right protection and contract enforcement; speed and predictability of courts; weak systems depress investment and raise risk premia |
| **Economic maturity** | Early-stage, fast-growing economies are inherently more volatile; reliance on a few exports or external capital amplifies shocks |

{: .note }
Country risk is broader than sovereign default risk: it includes the risk to *any* investment in the country (equity, FDI, contracts) from these factors.

## Measuring country risk

1. **Risk services** (PRS/ICRG, Economist Intelligence Unit, World Bank governance indicators): composite scores blending political, economic, financial indicators. Useful for ranking; scores are not PDs, scales differ across providers, and weightings are judgmental.
2. **Sovereign ratings** (Moody's, S&P, Fitch): explicit credit opinions on government debt.
3. **Market-based measures:** sovereign bond default spreads and CDS spreads — continuous, forward-looking, but noisy and contaminated by liquidity and risk appetite.

## Sovereign default: history and causes

- Defaults have occurred in **waves** tied to global cycles: 1820s–30s (Latin America), 1930s (Depression), 1980s (Latin American bank-loan crisis), 1998 (Russia), 2001 (Argentina), 2012 (Greece — largest restructuring), 2020s (Sri Lanka, Zambia, Ghana).
- Most defaults are on **foreign-currency debt** — the sovereign cannot print the foreign currency, and FX reserves run out.
- **Local-currency defaults happen too** (e.g., Russia 1998, plus numerous historical cases). Why default when you can print? Because printing causes inflation/currency collapse that may be judged *more* costly than restructuring; some sovereigns also have institutional constraints (independent central bank, currency board, euro membership — Greece could not print euros).
- Common causes: high debt/GDP and interest burden relative to revenues, revenue volatility (commodity dependence), short-maturity and foreign-currency-denominated debt structure, banking-system feedback loops, political unwillingness to pay (default is partly a *choice*).

**Consequences of default:** output losses and recessions, banking crises (banks hold government bonds), loss of (or costlier) market access for a period, trade and capital-flow disruption, political fallout. Empirically, reputational exclusion is real but often shorter-lived than feared; the domestic economic damage is the larger cost.

## Sovereign ratings

- Assigned separately for **foreign-currency** and **local-currency** obligations; local-currency ratings are typically equal or 1–2 notches *higher* (printing press, taxing domestic holders is easier).
- Inputs: debt ratios, fiscal and external balances, growth, institutional strength, monetary flexibility, banking-sector contingent liabilities, political stability.
- Criticisms: **ratings lag markets** (downgrades follow spread widening), herd behavior and pro-cyclicality (clustered multi-notch downgrades in crises — Asia 1997, eurozone 2010–12), home/regional bias debates, and the same issuer-pays conflicts as corporates.
- Upgrades are slow and incremental; downgrades cluster and cascade (sovereign ceilings drag down banks and corporates rated above the sovereign).

## Market-based measures

**Sovereign default spread** = sovereign's USD (or EUR) bond yield − matched-maturity risk-free (Treasury/Bund) yield.

**CDS spread** = annual premium for default protection; roughly, for small spreads,

$$s \approx PD \times LGD$$

so an annual PD estimate is $$PD \approx s / LGD$$ (e.g., 240 bp spread with 60% LGD → ≈ 4% annual risk-neutral PD).

| | Ratings | Bond default spreads | CDS spreads |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Episodic | Continuous | Continuous |
| Timeliness | Lag | Lead ratings | Lead ratings; often lead bonds |
| Noise | Low (too smooth) | Liquidity premia, currency mismatch | Counterparty/liquidity effects, but cleanest pure-default read |
| Interpretation | Ordinal grade | bp spread | bp spread; risk-neutral PD ≈ s/LGD |

Market measures are **risk-neutral** — they embed risk premia and overshoot in panics; ratings are smoother but stale. Best practice uses both: ratings for the level, market spreads for the direction and early warning.

{: .warning }
Don't equate a CDS-implied PD with a real-world PD: CDS spreads price risk aversion and liquidity on top of expected loss, so risk-neutral PDs systematically **exceed** historical default frequencies — most visibly for investment-grade sovereigns. Also remember local-currency default is possible: "a sovereign that prints its own currency cannot default" is a false statement GARP loves to offer.

{: .tip }
Linkage to valuation: analysts often add a country risk premium (scaled default spread) to discount rates for emerging-market investments — know the concept, not the mechanics, for Part I.

## Quick quiz

**1.** Which is the *best* explanation for a sovereign defaulting on local-currency debt?
- A. It is impossible — local-currency debt is risk-free
- B. Printing money to pay may be judged more costly (inflation, currency collapse) than restructuring
- C. Local-currency debt always has shorter maturity
- D. Rating agencies require it

**2.** Compared with sovereign credit ratings, sovereign CDS spreads are generally:
- A. Slower to react and less noisy
- B. Faster to react and noisier
- C. Identical in information content
- D. Available for fewer sovereigns than bonds in all cases

**3.** A sovereign's 5-year CDS trades at 300 bp. With an assumed LGD of 75%, the implied (risk-neutral) annual default probability is closest to:
- A. 2.25%
- B. 4.00%
- C. 3.00%
- D. 0.75%

**4.** Local-currency sovereign ratings are usually:
- A. Lower than foreign-currency ratings
- B. Equal to or higher than foreign-currency ratings
- C. Not assigned by major agencies
- D. Set equal to the country's largest bank's rating

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B.** Default can be a choice when monetization's inflation cost exceeds restructuring's cost (or printing is institutionally barred).

**2. B.** Market measures lead ratings but carry liquidity/risk-premium noise.

**3. B.** PD ≈ s/LGD = 0.03/0.75 = 4%. (2.25% multiplies instead of divides.)

**4. B.** The printing press and domestic taxation make local-currency capacity at least as strong.
