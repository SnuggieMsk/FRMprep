# 15. The Black-Scholes-Merton Model

The closed-form benchmark for European options. GARP tests the lognormal setup, a full d₁/d₂/N(d) computation, the dividend adjustments, implied volatility as a concept, and the American-option caveats.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the lognormal property of stock prices and compute the mean and variance of continuously compounded returns.
- Distinguish realized (historical) volatility from implied volatility and estimate volatility from returns data.
- List the BSM assumptions.
- Compute European call and put prices with the BSM formulas.
- Adjust BSM for dividends: continuous yield substitution and discrete PV-of-dividends adjustment.
- Define implied volatility and explain how it is backed out of market prices.
- Discuss American options in the BSM context (Black's approximation) and value warrants with dilution.

## Lognormal stock prices

BSM assumes the stock follows geometric Brownian motion, so the **price is lognormal** and the **continuously compounded return is normal**:

$$\ln S_T \sim N\!\left(\ln S_0 + \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T,\; \sigma^2 T\right)$$

The annualized continuously compounded return over [0, T] is normal with mean $$\mu - \sigma^2/2$$ and variance $$\sigma^2/T$$. The σ²/2 drag is why the expected continuously compounded return is *less* than μ (the expected simple-return growth rate): volatility erodes compound growth. Prices can't go negative (lognormal support is positive), and the distribution is right-skewed.

## Realized vs implied volatility

**Realized (historical) volatility:** compute log returns $$u_i = \ln(S_i/S_{i-1})$$, take their standard deviation s, and annualize:

$$\hat{\sigma} = s\sqrt{252} \;\; (\text{daily data; } \sqrt{52} \text{ weekly, } \sqrt{12} \text{ monthly})$$

**Implied volatility** is the σ that makes the BSM price equal the observed market price — found by iteration, since BSM can't be inverted analytically. Realized looks backward; implied is the market's forward-looking consensus (plus risk premia). Quoting options in "vol" terms is standard practice.

## BSM assumptions

- Stock follows GBM with constant μ and σ; no jumps.
- No dividends during the option's life (relaxed below).
- No transaction costs or taxes; perfectly divisible securities; continuous trading.
- Short selling allowed with full use of proceeds.
- Constant risk-free rate r; borrowing = lending rate.
- No riskless arbitrage opportunities.

The pricing argument mirrors the binomial one: a continuously rebalanced portfolio of the stock and the option is instantaneously riskless, must earn r, and μ drops out — only σ matters.

## The formulas

$$c = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \qquad p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1)$$

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$$

Interpretation: $$N(d_2)$$ is the risk-neutral probability the call finishes in the money; $$S_0 N(d_1) e^{rT}$$ is the risk-neutral expected stock price *given* exercise, times that probability. The two prices satisfy put–call parity $$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$ by construction.

### Worked example: full BSM call

$$S_0 = 100$$, K = 95, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 0.5 years.

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/95) + (0.05 + 0.02)(0.5)}{0.20\sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{0.05129 + 0.03500}{0.14142} = 0.6102$$

$$d_2 = 0.6102 - 0.1414 = 0.4688$$

From the normal table: N(0.61) = 0.7291, N(0.47) = 0.6808 (interpolating d₂ ≈ 0.47).

$$c = 100(0.7291) - 95e^{-0.025}(0.6808) = 72.91 - 92.654 \times 0.6808 = 72.91 - 63.08 = \mathbf{\$9.83}$$

Checks worth a few seconds: c exceeds intrinsic value (\$5) and is below $$S_0$$; lower bound $$S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 7.35$$ is respected.

### Worked example: put via put–call parity

Same inputs. Rather than rerunning the formula with N(−d₁) and N(−d₂):

$$p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 9.83 + 92.65 - 100 = \mathbf{\$2.48}$$

Direct check: $$p = 92.654 \times N(-0.47) - 100 \times N(-0.61) = 92.654(0.3192) - 100(0.2709) = 29.58 - 27.09 = 2.49$$ — equal to rounding. Parity is the fastest route to the second price and a free error check on the first.

{: .warning }
The two highest-frequency slips: (1) using $$r - \sigma^2/2$$ instead of $$r + \sigma^2/2$$ in d₁ — the *minus* version belongs to the lognormal mean of ln S_T, not to d₁; (2) forgetting to discount K (using K instead of $$Ke^{-rT}$$). Both errors appear verbatim among the answer choices.

{: .note }
Normal-table mechanics: N(−x) = 1 − N(x), so one table serves both tails — N(−0.61) = 1 − 0.7291 = 0.2709. When d falls between table rows, interpolate linearly; GARP typically supplies the N(d) values or makes the rounding immaterial.

## Dividends

**Discrete dividends:** replace $$S_0$$ with $$S_0 - PV(\text{divs})$$, the PV of dividends paid during the option's life (discounted at r), then apply BSM unchanged.

**Continuous dividend yield q** (indices, FX where q = foreign rate): replace $$S_0$$ with $$S_0 e^{-qT}$$:

$$c = S_0 e^{-qT} N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

Dividends lower call values and raise put values: the holder of an option doesn't receive dividends, and the stock price drops on ex-dividend dates.

## American options and Black's approximation

BSM prices **European** options only. An American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early, so its value equals the European call's — BSM applies directly. With dividends, early exercise of a call can be optimal **just before an ex-dividend date**. **Black's approximation:** value (1) a European call to final maturity T with $$S_0$$ reduced by PV of all dividends, and (2) a European call expiring just before the last ex-dividend date; take the larger. American puts may be exercised early even without dividends, so BSM understates them — use a binomial tree.

## Warrants and dilution

Warrants are call options issued by the company itself; exercise creates **new shares** and brings in cash, diluting existing holders. With N shares outstanding and M warrants:

$$\text{Warrant value} \approx \frac{N}{N + M} \times c$$

where c is the BSM value of a regular call on the (post-announcement) stock. The market typically impounds dilution into the stock price when the issue is announced, so the cost to shareholders is incurred at announcement, not exercise.

{: .tip }
Implied-volatility logic questions: price and implied vol move together (vega > 0). If a quoted option price is *above* your BSM value computed with historical σ, the market's implied σ is *higher* than historical — no computation needed.

## Quick quiz

**1.** A stock has expected return 12% and volatility 30%. The mean of the continuously compounded annual return is:
- A. 12.0%
- B. 7.5%
- C. 16.5%
- D. 10.2%

**2.** In BSM, N(d₂) is best interpreted as:
- A. The option's delta
- B. The risk-neutral probability that a call expires in the money
- C. The real-world probability of exercise
- D. The hedge ratio for the put

**3.** A European call on an index paying a 3% continuous dividend yield is valued by:
- A. Replacing r with r + q
- B. Replacing S₀ with S₀e^{−qT}
- C. Replacing K with Ke^{−qT}
- D. Adding PV of dividends to S₀

**4.** With S₀ = 50, c = 4.20, K = 50, r = 4%, T = 1: the European put price by parity is closest to:
- A. \$4.20
- B. \$2.24
- C. \$6.16
- D. \$2.50

<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B.** Mean cc return = μ − σ²/2 = 0.12 − 0.09/2 = 0.12 − 0.045 = 7.5%.

**2. B.** N(d₂) = risk-neutral exercise probability; N(d₁) is the call's delta (and is *not* the exercise probability). It is risk-neutral, not real-world.

**3. B.** Continuous-yield adjustment substitutes S₀e^{−qT} for S₀ (q also enters d₁ through r − q). Option D describes the wrong direction — discrete dividends are *subtracted*.

**4. B.** p = c + Ke^{−rT} − S₀ = 4.20 + 50e^{−0.04} − 50 = 4.20 + 48.039 − 50 = \$2.24. Choice A is the result of forgetting to discount the strike (parity then gives p = c at the money).
