# 16. Option Sensitivity Measures: The Greeks

Delta, gamma, theta, vega, and rho: what they measure, their signs, where they peak, and how dealers use them to neutralize books. Expect a delta-hedge share count, a delta–gamma construction, and at least one sign question.

## Learning objectives

- Define and compute delta for calls, puts, and portfolios; describe delta hedging and dynamic rebalancing.
- Define theta, gamma, vega, and rho; describe their signs, peaks, and behavior as expiry approaches.
- Use the delta–gamma approximation for the price change of an option position.
- Construct delta-neutral and delta–gamma-neutral portfolios using traded options.
- State the relationship among theta, delta, and gamma for a delta-neutral portfolio.
- Describe portfolio insurance as synthetic put replication and its limitations.

## Delta

Delta is the sensitivity of the option price to the stock price, $$\delta = \partial f / \partial S$$. Under BSM (no dividends):

$$\delta_{call} = N(d_1) \in (0,1) \qquad \delta_{put} = N(d_1) - 1 \in (-1,0)$$

ATM calls have delta near 0.5 (slightly above, since d₁ > 0 at the money). As expiry nears, call delta migrates to 1 (ITM) or 0 (OTM). Portfolio delta is the position-weighted sum: $$\delta_{port} = \sum_i n_i \delta_i$$ — and one share has delta 1.

**Delta hedging.** A short call (delta −N(d₁) per option to the writer) is hedged by holding $$N(d_1)$$ shares per option. Because delta changes with S and t, the hedge must be **rebalanced** — *dynamic hedging*. The cost of rebalancing (buy high after rallies, sell low after declines, for a short-gamma hedger) is exactly what the option premium pays for.

### Worked example: delta hedging a short call, then rebalancing

A dealer writes 1,000 calls (10 contracts on 100 shares) with delta 0.62. Position delta = −1,000 × 0.62 = −620. **Buy 620 shares** → portfolio delta zero.

The stock rallies and the calls' delta rises to 0.70. Position delta = −700 + 620 = −80. **Buy 80 more shares** (total 700). The hedger bought *after* the price rose — repeated, this "buy high, sell low" pattern is the rebalancing cost of being short gamma.

{: .note }
Deltas of the linear instruments, for portfolio arithmetic: a share has delta +1; a forward on a non-dividend stock has delta exactly 1; a futures contract has delta $$e^{rT}$$ (daily settlement). Hedging with futures therefore needs slightly *fewer* contracts than the share count suggests.

## Theta

Theta is the time decay $$\Theta = \partial f / \partial t$$ — usually quoted per calendar day (annual θ / 365). Theta is **negative for most long options**: with everything else fixed, value erodes as expiry approaches. Exceptions: a **deep ITM European put** can have positive theta (its price sits below intrinsic value, $$Ke^{-rT} - S$$, and pulls *up* toward K − S), and deep ITM European calls on high-dividend-yield assets similarly. Theta is most negative for ATM options close to expiry. Theta is not a hedgeable risk — time passes deterministically — but it is the flip side of gamma.

## Gamma

Gamma is the rate of change of delta: $$\Gamma = \partial^2 f / \partial S^2 = \frac{N'(d_1)}{S_0\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$. It is **positive for long calls and long puts** alike, and **largest for ATM options near expiry** (delta snaps from 0 to 1 across the strike). Gamma measures how fast a delta hedge goes stale.

The **delta–gamma approximation** for a position's value change:

$$\Delta P \approx \delta\, \Delta S + \frac{1}{2}\Gamma\, (\Delta S)^2$$

— the option-market twin of duration-plus-convexity. For a delta-neutral long-gamma book, P&L ≈ ½Γ(ΔS)² > 0 for any large move; the short-gamma book bleeds on large moves and collects theta in between.

## Vega and rho

**Vega** $$= \partial f / \partial \sigma$$: positive for long calls and puts, **highest for ATM options** and for *longer* maturities (unlike gamma, which peaks at short maturities). Quoted per 1% vol change.

**Rho** $$= \partial f / \partial r$$: **positive for calls** (higher r → lower PV of strike paid), **negative for puts**. For European options, rho_call $$= KTe^{-rT}N(d_2)$$. Usually the least important Greek for short-dated equity options.

## Sign table

| Position | Delta | Gamma | Theta | Vega | Rho |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long call | + | + | − | + | + |
| Long put | − | + | − (usually) | + | − |
| Short call | − | − | + | − | − |
| Short put | + | − | + (usually) | − | + |

Gamma and vega always share a sign (long options: both +); theta generally takes the opposite sign to gamma.

## Delta–gamma-neutral portfolios

Shares have gamma zero, so gamma can only be fixed with **other options**. Procedure (order matters):

1. Add $$w = -\Gamma_{port}/\Gamma_{option}$$ units of a traded option to zero the gamma.
2. The new option position changes delta — re-neutralize delta with shares (delta of shares is 1, gamma 0, so step 2 doesn't undo step 1).

The same two-step logic extends to vega neutrality with a second traded option (two instruments, two equations).

### Worked example: delta–gamma neutralization

A book has delta −450 and gamma −6,000. A traded call has delta 0.55 and gamma 1.8.

Step 1 — gamma: buy $$w = 6{,}000 / 1.8 = 3{,}333$$ calls. Gamma: −6,000 + 3,333 × 1.8 ≈ 0.

Step 2 — delta: new delta = −450 + 3,333 × 0.55 = −450 + 1,833 = +1,383. **Sell 1,383 shares.** Final book: delta ≈ 0, gamma ≈ 0; small moves and the *change* in delta are both hedged until the option's own Greeks drift.

{: .warning }
Two traps live in this calculation. First, hedge **gamma before delta** — adding options to fix gamma wrecks any delta hedge done first, while shares never disturb gamma. Second, watch position signs: a *written* (short) call contributes −delta and −gamma to the book. Exam answers that "buy" where you should sell, or that delta-hedge first and stop, are always on the menu.

## The theta–gamma trade-off

Substituting a delta-hedged portfolio into the BSM partial differential equation gives:

$$\Theta + rS\delta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi$$

where Π is the portfolio value. For a **delta-neutral** portfolio, $$\Theta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi$$: large positive gamma forces large negative theta and vice versa. You cannot own convexity without paying time decay — theta is the rent on gamma. This is why theta can serve as a rough proxy for gamma on a delta-neutral book.

### Quick reference: where each Greek peaks

- **Delta:** call delta ≈ 0.5 ATM; → 1 deep ITM, → 0 deep OTM (puts: −0.5, −1, 0).
- **Gamma:** ATM, short maturity — explosive just before expiry at the strike.
- **Vega:** ATM, *long* maturity — the opposite maturity pattern from gamma.
- **Theta:** most negative ATM near expiry (mirrors gamma, opposite sign).
- **Rho:** larger for ITM and long-dated options (more strike value at risk to discounting).

{: .tip }
"Which Greek is identical for a European call and put with the same strike and expiry?" — gamma and vega (differentiate put–call parity twice or once with respect to σ; the S₀ and K terms drop out). Delta, theta, and rho all differ between the pair.

## Portfolio insurance

Synthetic protective put: rather than buying puts, replicate one by **selling stock (or index futures) as the market falls and buying as it rises**, keeping the equity exposure equal to the put-adjusted delta. Cheap in calm markets, but it demands liquidity exactly when markets gap: in October 1987, programmatic insurance selling amplified the crash. Key limitation: replication assumes continuous trading and stable volatility — the conditions that fail in a crash.

## Quick quiz

**1.** A trader writes 500 puts with delta −0.38. To delta-hedge, the trader should:
- A. Buy 190 shares
- B. Sell 190 shares
- C. Buy 310 shares
- D. Sell 310 shares

**2.** Gamma is greatest for options that are:
- A. Deep in the money with long maturity
- B. At the money close to expiry
- C. Deep out of the money close to expiry
- D. At the money with long maturity

**3.** A delta-neutral portfolio has gamma +8,000. The stock jumps \$3 (either direction). The approximate P&L is:
- A. +\$24,000
- B. −\$36,000
- C. +\$36,000
- D. Zero — the portfolio is delta-neutral

**4.** For a delta-neutral portfolio with large positive theta, gamma is most likely:
- A. Large and positive
- B. Near zero
- C. Large and negative
- D. Unrelated to theta

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B.** Short 500 puts → position delta = −500 × (−0.38) = +190. Offset by **selling 190 shares**. (Buying shares — choice A — doubles the exposure.)

**2. B.** Delta flips fastest across the strike when little time remains, so gamma peaks ATM near expiry. Vega, by contrast, peaks ATM at *long* maturity (choice D is the vega answer).

**3. C.** ΔP ≈ δΔS + ½Γ(ΔS)² = 0 + ½ × 8,000 × 3² = +\$36,000, regardless of direction — the payoff of long gamma. Choice A is Γ × ΔS (no square, no ½); choice D ignores the gamma term entirely.

**4. C.** From Θ + ½σ²S²Γ = rΠ, big positive theta pairs with big negative gamma (a short-options book that earns decay but loses on large moves).

</details>
