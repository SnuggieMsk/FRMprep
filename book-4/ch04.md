# 4. External and Internal Credit Ratings

GARP tests the rating scales, through-the-cycle vs point-in-time philosophy, how to read and chain transition matrices, and how internal rating systems and statistical scoring models (Altman Z) complement agency ratings.

## Learning objectives

- Describe external rating scales (Moody's, S&P, Fitch), the rating process, and what ratings measure.
- Distinguish investment grade from non-investment grade and describe outlooks and watchlists.
- Explain through-the-cycle (TTC) vs point-in-time (PIT) rating philosophies and rating stability.
- Define and interpret rating transition (migration) matrices and compute multi-period transition probabilities.
- Describe historical default and recovery evidence by rating, and rating performance (accuracy ratios, in outline).
- Explain internal rating systems at banks, their uses, and how they differ from external ratings.
- Describe statistical credit scoring (Altman's Z-score) and the use of market-based measures alongside ratings.
- Explain rating-agency conflicts of interest (issuer-pays) and their role in the 2007–09 structured-products debacle.

## External ratings

| Grade | Moody's | S&P/Fitch | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investment grade | Aaa, Aa, A, Baa | AAA, AA, A, BBB | Low default risk |
| Speculative ("junk") | Ba, B, Caa, Ca, C | BB, B, CCC, CC, C | Material default risk |
| Default | C/D | D | In default |

Modifiers (1/2/3 or +/−) create notches. The IG/HY boundary — **Baa3/BBB−** — matters because many mandates and collateral rules key off it; a downgrade across it ("fallen angel") triggers forced selling.

- Ratings target **default probability** primarily (S&P) or **expected loss** = PD × LGD (Moody's, especially for structured products).
- **Outlooks** (positive/stable/negative, 1–2 year view) and **watchlists** (likely action within ~90 days) signal direction without changing the rating.
- Ratings are **ordinal** rankings of relative creditworthiness, not cardinal PDs; realized default rates per rating vary over the cycle.

## Through-the-cycle vs point-in-time

| | Through-the-cycle (agencies) | Point-in-time (many bank models) |
|---|---|---|
| Conditioning | Average/stressed conditions over a full cycle | Current conditions |
| Stability | High — few reversals, ratings "smoothed" | Volatile — moves with the cycle |
| Default-prediction accuracy short-term | Lower | Higher |
| Procyclicality | Low | High (capital needs spike in recessions) |

Agencies deliberately trade short-term accuracy for stability (investors dislike rating churn); they act when a change is unlikely to be reversed. PIT measures (KMV-style EDFs from equity markets) lead agency actions but generate noise.

## Transition matrices

A one-year transition matrix gives probabilities of migrating between ratings (rows = start, columns = end; rows sum to 100%, including a default column and possibly withdrawn). Properties: heavy diagonal (ratings are sticky), default probability rises monotonically as rating falls, and **rating momentum** exists empirically (a recently downgraded issuer is more likely to be downgraded again — a Markov-chain violation).

### Worked example: two-year default probability

One-year transitions for a BB issuer: stay BB 80%, up to BBB 8%, down to B 10%, default 2%. For B: default probability 6%; for BBB: 0.5%. Assuming the Markov property, the two-year cumulative default probability for the BB issuer:

$$PD_{2yr} = \underbrace{0.02}_{\text{yr 1}} + \underbrace{0.80 \times 0.02}_{\text{stay BB, default yr 2}} + \underbrace{0.10 \times 0.06}_{\text{to B, default}} + \underbrace{0.08 \times 0.005}_{\text{to BBB, default}}$$

$$= 0.02 + 0.016 + 0.006 + 0.0004 = 4.24\%$$

{: .warning }
Multi-year default probabilities are **not** one-year PD × years. You must walk the matrix: condition on each surviving state. And remember cumulative PD over n years < n × annual PD for good credits only at first — the chaining handles it correctly either way.

### Worked example: marginal vs cumulative PD

Cumulative PDs for a B-rated issuer: 5% (year 1), 11% (by year 2). The **marginal (conditional) PD in year 2** given survival through year 1:

$$PD_{2|1} = \frac{0.11 - 0.05}{1 - 0.05} = \frac{0.06}{0.95} = 6.32\%$$

Low-rated issuers' marginal PDs typically **decline** with time (survivors improve); high-rated issuers' marginal PDs **rise** (nowhere to go but down).

## Default and recovery evidence

- Default rates rise sharply down the scale and are strongly cyclical (HY default rates can exceed 10% in recessions vs ~1–2% in calm years).
- **Recovery rates** depend on seniority and collateral (senior secured loans recover most; subordinated bonds least) and are **negatively correlated with default rates** — recoveries fall exactly when defaults spike, worsening tail losses.
- Rating performance is measured by accuracy/CAP profiles: do defaults come disproportionately from the lowest ratings? Generally yes — ratings discriminate well ordinally.

## Internal ratings

Banks rate borrowers internally for underwriting, pricing, limits, provisioning (IFRS 9/CECL), and — under Basel's IRB approach — regulatory capital (estimating PD, and under advanced IRB also LGD and EAD). Versus agencies:

- Cover borrowers with no public ratings (SMEs, retail).
- Often **two-dimensional**: an obligor rating (PD) plus a facility rating (LGD/EAD).
- May be PIT, TTC, or hybrid; must be validated, replicable, and independent of the sales function.
- Risk: rating inflation pressure from relationship managers — governance and backtesting against realized defaults are essential.

## Statistical scoring: Altman's Z

A discriminant function of accounting ratios; the classic public-manufacturer form:

$$Z = 1.2X_1 + 1.4X_2 + 3.3X_3 + 0.6X_4 + 1.0X_5$$

with X₁ = working capital/assets, X₂ = retained earnings/assets, X₃ = EBIT/assets, X₄ = market equity/book liabilities, X₅ = sales/assets. Rough zones: Z > 3.0 safe, 1.8–3.0 gray, < 1.8 distress. Modern equivalents use logit/machine-learning models producing PDs directly; market-based measures (Merton/KMV) use equity prices. Know the *idea* — weighted ratios mapped to a default signal — not the coefficients.

## Conflicts of interest

The **issuer-pays** model creates pressure to rate generously; it became acute in structured finance pre-2008, where agencies rated CDO tranches AAA using flawed correlation assumptions while earning large fees and advising on structuring ("rating shopping"). Post-crisis reforms: regulation of agencies (SEC/ESMA), reduced hard-wiring of ratings into regulation, disclosure of methodologies.

{: .tip }
Exam framing: external ratings are stable TTC ordinal rankings with strong long-run discriminatory power, weak short-run timeliness, and incentive problems in issuer-pays niches. Internal ratings fill coverage and granularity gaps and feed capital models.

## Quick quiz

**1.** The lowest investment-grade rating on S&P's scale is:
- A. BB+
- B. BBB−
- C. A−
- D. BBB+

**2.** Through-the-cycle ratings, relative to point-in-time measures, are:
- A. More volatile and more accurate short-term predictors
- B. More stable but slower to reflect current conditions
- C. Based mainly on equity prices
- D. Updated daily

**3.** A one-year matrix: A-rated issuer stays A with 92%, falls to BBB with 6%, default 0.1%; BBB one-year PD is 0.3%. Ignoring other paths, the two-year cumulative PD for the A issuer is closest to:
- A. 0.21%
- B. 0.29%
- C. 0.11%
- D. 0.40%

**4.** Empirically, recovery rates tend to:
- A. Be constant across the cycle
- B. Rise when default rates rise
- C. Fall when default rates rise
- D. Depend only on the borrower's rating

**1. B.** BBB− (Baa3 at Moody's) is the IG floor; BB+ is the top of high yield.

**2. B.** TTC smooths the cycle for stability, sacrificing timeliness.

**3. A.** 0.1% + 0.92×0.1% + 0.06×0.3% = 0.1% + 0.092% + 0.018% = 0.21%. (0.11% forgets the survive-then-default paths; 0.40% naively doubles a misread 0.2%.)
