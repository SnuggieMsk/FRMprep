# 6. Measuring Credit Risk

The quantitative core of credit risk: expected and unexpected loss, credit VaR, and the single-factor (Vasicek/Gaussian copula) model behind Basel's IRB formula. GARP reliably asks an EL calculation and frequently a WCDR/credit-VaR calculation.

## Learning objectives

- Explain expected loss, unexpected loss, and the loss distribution of a credit portfolio.
- Calculate EL = PD × LGD × EAD and the UL (standard deviation of loss) for a single exposure.
- Define economic capital and explain how it relates to the loss distribution and credit VaR.
- Describe the Gaussian copula / Vasicek single-factor model and compute the worst-case default rate (WCDR).
- Explain how the Basel IRB capital requirement uses the single-factor model.
- Describe the Merton model's intuition for estimating default probability from equity and asset values.
- Explain challenges: default correlation, granularity, and the skewed nature of credit loss distributions.

## The credit loss distribution

Credit losses are **highly skewed**: most years small losses near EL, rarely a catastrophic tail (correlated defaults). Consequently:

- **Expected loss (EL):** the mean — a *cost of doing business*, covered by pricing/provisions, not capital.
- **Unexpected loss (UL):** dispersion around the mean — covered by **capital**.
- **Economic capital:** the buffer covering losses up to a chosen confidence level (e.g., 99.9%) over one year, *in excess of EL*:

$$\text{Economic capital} = \text{Credit VaR} = \text{WCL}_{99.9\%} - \text{EL}$$

## Expected loss

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$

- **PD:** probability of default over the horizon (1 year standard).
- **LGD** = 1 − recovery rate: fraction of exposure lost given default.
- **EAD:** exposure at default (drawn amount + expected drawdown of commitments).

Portfolio EL is the simple sum of individual ELs — expectation is linear, **no correlation needed**.

### Worked example: EL and UL for a single loan

\$10M loan: PD = 2%, recovery rate = 40% (LGD = 60%), σ_LGD = 25%.

**Expected loss:**

$$EL = 0.02 \times 0.60 \times 10\text{M} = \$120{,}000$$

**Unexpected loss** (standard deviation of credit loss, with default as a Bernoulli so $$\sigma_{PD}^2 = PD(1-PD) = 0.02 \times 0.98 = 0.0196$$):

$$UL = EAD \times \sqrt{PD \cdot \sigma_{LGD}^2 + LGD^2 \cdot \sigma_{PD}^2}$$

$$= 10\text{M} \times \sqrt{0.02 \times 0.25^2 + 0.60^2 \times 0.0196} = 10\text{M} \times \sqrt{0.00125 + 0.007056}$$

$$= 10\text{M} \times \sqrt{0.008306} = 10\text{M} \times 0.09114 = \$911{,}400$$

UL is ~7.6× EL — credit risk lives in the variance, not the mean.

{: .note }
Portfolio UL is **not** the sum of ULs: it aggregates with default correlations, $$UL_P = \sqrt{\sum_i\sum_j \rho_{ij} UL_i UL_j}$$, and is far below the sum for low correlations. Each loan's *risk contribution* depends on its correlation with the portfolio.

## The single-factor (Vasicek / Gaussian copula) model

Each borrower i defaults when a standardized latent variable falls below a threshold. The latent variable loads on one **common systematic factor** F (the economy) plus an idiosyncratic shock:

$$X_i = \sqrt{\rho}\, F + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,\epsilon_i$$

Conditional on a bad realization of F at the X percentile, the default rate across a large, granular portfolio converges to the **worst-case default rate**:

$$WCDR = N\!\left(\frac{N^{-1}(PD) + \sqrt{\rho}\,N^{-1}(X)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)$$

- ρ = copula (asset) correlation between borrowers; higher ρ → fatter loss tail.
- X = confidence level (Basel uses 99.9%, $$N^{-1}(0.999) = 3.090$$).
- With ρ = 0, WCDR = PD (idiosyncratic risk diversifies away entirely).

**Credit VaR** for a granular portfolio:

$$\text{Credit VaR} = EAD \times LGD \times (WCDR - PD)$$

Basel II/III IRB capital is exactly this (with a maturity adjustment, and ρ set by formula per asset class).

### Worked example: WCDR and credit VaR

A \$100M well-diversified loan portfolio: PD = 1%, LGD = 60%, ρ = 0.1, confidence 99.9%.

$$N^{-1}(0.01) = -2.326, \quad N^{-1}(0.999) = 3.090$$

$$WCDR = N\!\left(\frac{-2.326 + \sqrt{0.1}\times 3.090}{\sqrt{0.9}}\right) = N\!\left(\frac{-2.326 + 0.977}{0.9487}\right) = N(-1.422) = 7.75\%$$

$$\text{Credit VaR} = 100\text{M} \times 0.60 \times (0.0775 - 0.01) = 100\text{M} \times 0.60 \times 0.0675 = \$4.05\text{M}$$

EL = 100M × 0.6 × 0.01 = \$0.6M is provisioned; the \$4.05M above EL is economic capital.

{: .warning }
Two classic traps: (1) **subtract PD** — capital covers *unexpected* loss; using WCDR alone overstates it; (2) signs inside WCDR — $$N^{-1}(PD)$$ is a large **negative** number for small PDs while $$N^{-1}(0.999)$$ is +3.090. Flipping either sign gives plausible-looking wrong answers.

## The Merton model (intuition)

Equity is a **call option on the firm's assets** with strike equal to the face value of debt D maturing at T:

- At T: equity holders receive $$\max(V_T - D, 0)$$; debt holders receive $$\min(V_T, D)$$ — i.e., risky debt = risk-free debt − a put on assets.
- Default occurs if $$V_T < D$$. The risk-neutral PD is $$N(-d_2)$$ from BSM applied to asset value V, asset volatility σ_V.
- Practical use (KMV/EDF): back out V and σ_V from observed equity value and equity volatility, compute **distance to default**

$$DD = \frac{\ln(V/D) + (\mu - \sigma_V^2/2)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}$$

then map DD to a PD (empirically, rather than via the normal CDF). Strengths: forward-looking, updates continuously with the stock price. Weaknesses: needs traded equity, assumes a simple debt structure and a single default date, normality understates tail PDs.

## Putting the pieces together

| Concept | Covers | Funded by |
|---|---|---|
| Expected loss | Mean of loss distribution | Pricing / loan-loss provisions |
| Unexpected loss / credit VaR | Tail above the mean at 99.9% | Economic / regulatory capital |
| Beyond 99.9% | Catastrophic residual | Shareholders' problem (default of the bank) |

Drivers of tail thickness: **default correlation** (ρ), **concentration** (granularity — single-name and sector), and **LGD uncertainty** (and its adverse correlation with default rates).
