# 8. Stress Testing

GARP tests how stress testing complements VaR/ES, how scenarios are chosen (historical, hypothetical, reverse), governance expectations, and the regulatory landscape (Basel, CCAR/DFAST). Conceptual chapter — precision on definitions wins points.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the purposes of stress testing and compare it with VaR/ES.
- Explain scenario types: historical, hypothetical, predictive/model-based; scenario selection and coherence.
- Describe reverse stress testing and its uses.
- Explain how stressed inputs produce stressed VaR/ES and the pros/cons versus scenario P&L.
- Describe governance: board/senior management roles, policies, validation, and the internal audit role.
- Describe regulatory stress testing: Basel principles, US CCAR/DFAST, EU-wide tests; capital vs liquidity stress tests.
- Explain challenges: scenario plausibility vs severity, model risk, knock-on/second-round effects, and incorporating balance-sheet responses.

## Stress testing vs VaR/ES

| | VaR / ES | Stress testing |
|---|---|---|
| Question | How bad over a typical period at probability α? | What happens in *this specific* extreme scenario? |
| Probability attached | Yes (quantile) | Usually no |
| Data basis | Recent history / estimated distribution | Crisis history or imagination |
| Horizon | Short (1–10 days market risk) | Long (quarters/years), path-dependent |
| Captures unprecedented events | No | Yes (if you can imagine them) |
| Use | Day-to-day limits, capital | Capital planning, vulnerability discovery, communication |

They are **complements**: VaR backtests well but is blind beyond the sample; stress tests probe the tail but carry no likelihood. Backward-looking VaR failed in 2007 precisely because the prior sample was calm — stress testing is the institutionalized answer.

{: .note }
**Stressed VaR/ES**: compute VaR/ES using parameters (volatilities, correlations) calibrated to a crisis window (e.g., 2008). Basel 2.5 added stressed VaR; FRTB's ES is calibrated to the worst observed period. This blends the two frameworks — a probabilistic measure with stressed inputs.

## Scenario design

**Historical scenarios:** replay actual crises (1987 crash, 1998 LTCM/Russia, 2007–09 GFC, 2020 COVID). Pros: internally coherent (these co-movements actually happened), objective. Cons: the next crisis is never the last one; portfolio may have new risks (crypto, climate) absent from history.

**Hypothetical scenarios:** constructed shocks (geopolitical conflict, cyber shutdown, +300bp rate shock with property crash). Pros: forward-looking, tailored to current vulnerabilities. Cons: subjective; hard to keep **coherent** — all variables must move consistently (you can't shock equities −40% while leaving credit spreads and volatility unchanged). Approaches to coherence: shock core variables and set the rest via estimated conditional relationships (e.g., conditional expectations given the core shocks), or full macro models.

**Severity vs plausibility trade-off:** scenarios must be *severe but plausible*. Too mild → false comfort; too wild → dismissed by management. Knock-on/second-round effects (fire sales, funding runs, counterparty failures) made 2008 worse than first-round losses — good scenarios include them.

**Choosing variables:** start from the portfolio's key risk factors and concentrations; the scenario should target what would actually hurt *this* firm.

## Reverse stress testing

Standard test: scenario → loss. **Reverse:** start from the outcome — "what would make us fail / breach capital minima?" — and search backward for scenarios that produce it.

- Forces discovery of hidden vulnerabilities and concentration combinations management hasn't imagined.
- Required by UK/EU regulators for recovery and resolution planning.
- Hard part: the search space is huge; quantitative approaches search over factor combinations for the most *plausible* scenario reaching the loss threshold.

## Governance

- **Board and senior management** own the program: approve framework, scenario severity, and act on results — tests that never affect decisions are theater. The "use test": results must feed risk appetite, limits, and capital planning.
- **Policies and documentation:** scope, frequency, methodologies, remediation paths.
- **Validation and independent review:** challenge models, data, and scenario coherence; **internal audit** reviews the process end-to-end.
- Known governance failures: scenarios watered down to pass, results not escalated, infrastructure unable to aggregate exposures quickly (BCBS 239 data aggregation principles address this).

## Regulatory stress testing

- **Basel principles** (2009, updated 2018): stress testing as a key risk-governance tool; board ownership; severe-but-plausible scenarios; capture system-wide interactions and feedback.
- **US CCAR/DFAST:** annual Fed tests of large banks under supervisory scenarios (baseline/severely adverse) projecting revenues, losses, and capital ratios over a 9-quarter horizon; results gate capital distributions (stress capital buffer). The 2009 SCAP restored confidence by publishing credible results with a recapitalization backstop.
- **EU-wide (EBA) tests**, Bank of England annual scenarios — similar architecture.
- **Capital vs liquidity stress tests:** capital tests ask whether equity survives losses; liquidity tests (LCR-style, survival horizons) ask whether cash outflows can be met under funding runs. 2008 and 2023 (SVB) showed liquidity can kill before solvency does.
- Disclosure debate: publishing results aids market discipline but may encourage teaching-to-the-test and herding into identically-measured risks.

{: .warning }
Trap statements GARP likes: "stress tests assign probabilities to scenarios" — generally **false**; "stress testing replaces VaR" — false, they are complements; "historical scenarios are sufficient" — false, they miss novel risks; "a more severe scenario is always better" — false, implausible scenarios get ignored and distort capital allocation.

{: .tip }
If a question contrasts a 99% ES with a stress loss, remember the stress loss can exceed *or* fall short of ES — they answer different questions and there is no fixed ordering.

**3.** A coherent hypothetical scenario requires that:
- A. Only one risk factor is shocked at a time
- B. Shocked variables move in an internally consistent way
- C. It matches a historical episode exactly
- D. It be approved by external auditors

**4.** Under CCAR, supervisory stress results primarily constrain:
- A. Banks' trading-desk VaR limits
- B. Capital distributions via projected capital ratios under adverse scenarios
- C. Deposit interest rates
- D. Loan pricing

**4. B.** Projected post-stress capital ratios gate dividends/buybacks (stress capital buffer).
