# 9. Pricing Conventions, Discounting, and Arbitrage

The foundation of fixed income: quote conventions, discount factors, the law of one price, and replication arbitrage. GARP tests T-bill discount-rate mechanics, extracting discount factors from bond prices, and spotting/monetizing a mispricing.

## Learning objectives

- Describe Treasury quote conventions: T-bill discount rates, bond clean vs dirty (invoice) prices, accrued interest.
- Calculate the price of a T-bill from its quoted discount rate and explain why the discount rate understates the true yield.
- Define discount factors and compute them from Treasury bond/STRIP prices.
- Describe STRIPS (C-STRIPS, P-STRIPS) and how stripping/reconstitution enforces consistency.
- State the law of one price and identify arbitrage opportunities via replication.
- Construct a replicating portfolio for a bond from other bonds or STRIPS and compute the arbitrage profit.
- Explain idealized vs actual deviations: bid-ask, financing, liquidity premia (on-the-run vs off-the-run).

## Treasury quoting conventions

**T-bills** are quoted on a **discount rate** basis (actual/360):

$$P = F\left(1 - d \cdot \frac{n}{360}\right)$$

where d = quoted discount rate, n = days to maturity, F = face. The discount rate **understates** the true return because (i) the gain is divided by *face*, not price paid, and (ii) the 360-day year shortens the annualization.

**Coupon bonds** are quoted **clean** (flat); the buyer pays the **dirty (invoice) price**:

$$\text{Dirty} = \text{Clean} + \text{Accrued interest}, \qquad AI = \text{coupon payment} \times \frac{\text{days since last coupon}}{\text{days in period}}$$

(US Treasuries use actual/actual for the accrual fraction.) Prices are now quoted in decimals; legacy convention was 32nds.

### Worked example: T-bill price and true yield

A 90-day T-bill is quoted at a 2.00% discount rate, \$100 face.

$$P = 100\left(1 - 0.02 \times \frac{90}{360}\right) = 100(1 - 0.005) = \$99.50$$

True (investment) yield, annualized actual/365:

$$y = \frac{100 - 99.50}{99.50} \times \frac{365}{90} = 0.005025 \times 4.0556 = 2.038\%$$

2.038% > 2.00%: the discount rate always understates the yield.

## Discount factors

The discount factor d(t) is **the price today of \$1 delivered at t** — the cleanest, convention-free statement of time value. Any default-free cash-flow stream is then priced as:

$$P = \sum_t CF_t \cdot d(t)$$

Discount factors are extracted ("bootstrapped") from observed prices, shortest maturity first.

### Worked example: bootstrapping discount factors

- Bond A: 6-month, 4% coupon (semiannual), price 100.98. Single cash flow 102 at t = 0.5:

$$102 \cdot d(0.5) = 100.98 \;\Rightarrow\; d(0.5) = \frac{100.98}{102} = 0.9900$$

- Bond B: 1-year, 4% semiannual, price 100.97. Cash flows 2 at 0.5 and 102 at 1.0:

$$2(0.9900) + 102 \cdot d(1.0) = 100.97 \;\Rightarrow\; d(1.0) = \frac{100.97 - 1.98}{102} = \frac{98.99}{102} = 0.9705$$

d(t) must decline with maturity (positive rates) and lie below 1; a d(t) above an earlier one signals a data error or negative forward rates.

## STRIPS

Treasury STRIPS split a bond into single-payment claims: **C-STRIPS** (coupon interest) and **P-STRIPS** (principal). Each is a zero-coupon Treasury, so STRIP prices *are* discount factors (×100). Dealers can **strip** a bond into pieces or **reconstitute** pieces into the bond — this two-way arbitrage forces the bond price toward the sum of its parts. In practice small deviations persist: P-STRIPS of liquid bonds can trade rich, long C-STRIPS cheap; idiosyncratic supply/demand and liquidity matter.

## Law of one price and replication arbitrage

**Law of one price:** identical cash flows must trade at the same price — otherwise buy the cheap version, short the expensive one, and pocket the difference with zero net future obligation.

Replication recipe: match the target bond's cash flows date by date using STRIPS (trivial) or a portfolio of coupon bonds (solve backward from the longest cash flow).

### Worked example: arbitraging a mispriced bond

Using d(0.5) = 0.9900 and d(1.0) = 0.9705 from above, the fair price of a 1-year 6% semiannual-coupon Treasury (cash flows 3 and 103):

$$P_{fair} = 3(0.9900) + 103(0.9705) = 2.97 + 99.96 = 102.93$$

The bond trades in the market at 102.60 — **cheap by 0.33**.

Arbitrage: **buy** the bond at 102.60; **short** the replicating STRIPS package (sell 3 face of the 6-month STRIP and 103 face of the 1-year STRIP, receiving 102.93). Net cash today: +0.33. Future cash flows: the bond's coupons exactly cover the short STRIPS' obligations. Riskless profit = **\$0.33 per \$100 face**.

{: .warning }
Direction discipline: buy LOW, short HIGH — under exam pressure candidates reverse the legs. Also, compare like with like: replication must match the **dirty** price and every cash-flow date; comparing a clean quote against a dirty replication cost manufactures phantom arbitrage. And the T-bill trap: the discount rate is **not** the yield — never discount cash flows with a quoted discount rate.

## Why deviations survive

Real-world frictions cap the arbitrage: bid-ask spreads and financing (repo) costs; balance-sheet/capital charges; **liquidity premia** — on-the-run (most recently issued) Treasuries trade rich to off-the-run because of their liquidity and repo specialness; STRIPS market segmentation. An apparent mispricing smaller than round-trip costs is not exploitable.

- A. \$98.40
- B. \$96.80
- C. \$98.42
- D. \$98.36

**2.** A 1-year STRIP trades at 96.20 and a 6-month STRIP at 98.00. The fair price of a 1-year 5% semiannual-coupon bond is closest to:
- A. \$100.51
- B. \$101.05
- C. \$98.66
- D. \$100.00

**3.** The invoice price a bond buyer pays equals:
- A. Clean price minus accrued interest
- B. Clean price plus accrued interest
- C. Face value plus accrued interest
- D. Quoted price always

**4.** Versus the bill's true annualized yield, the quoted T-bill discount rate is:
- A. Higher, because of the 360-day year
- B. Lower, because the gain is divided by face and a 360-day year is used
- C. Identical by construction
- D. Higher for bills under 6 months, lower beyond
