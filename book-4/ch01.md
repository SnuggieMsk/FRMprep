# 1. Measures of Financial Risk

GARP tests whether you can define VaR and expected shortfall precisely, compute both under normality, and judge any risk measure against the four coherence properties. The mean-variance framework is the warm-up: know exactly when it works and when it fails.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the mean-variance framework and how it is used to evaluate risk and return.
- Explain why the mean-variance framework breaks down when returns are not elliptically (e.g., normally) distributed.
- Define value at risk (VaR), calculate it for given parameters, and describe its limitations.
- Define the properties of a coherent risk measure and explain why each matters.
- Explain why VaR is not coherent and why expected shortfall (ES) is.
- Define and calculate expected shortfall, and compare ES with VaR.
- Describe how risk measures aggregate via weighted averages of quantiles (spectral measures, in outline).

## The mean-variance framework

Investors trade off expected return $$\mu$$ against risk measured by standard deviation $$\sigma$$. If returns are normal, the entire distribution is pinned down by $$(\mu, \sigma)$$, so ranking portfolios by mean and variance is fully consistent with expected utility for any risk-averse investor.

The framework remains valid for any **elliptical** distribution (normal, Student's t with finite variance). It fails when:

- Returns are **skewed** (e.g., short option positions, credit portfolios: many small gains, rare large losses).
- Returns have **fat tails** (excess kurtosis) — σ understates tail risk.
- Payoffs are **non-linear** in underlying risk factors (options), so portfolio returns are non-normal even if the underlying is normal.

{: .note }
Two portfolios can share the same μ and σ but have wildly different tail risk. Mean-variance ranking cannot see the difference; tail-focused measures (VaR, ES) can.

## Value at risk

**Definition.** VaR at confidence level $$\alpha$$ over horizon $$T$$ is the loss that will not be exceeded with probability $$\alpha$$ — the $$\alpha$$-quantile of the loss distribution:

$$P(L > \text{VaR}_\alpha) = 1 - \alpha$$

For normally distributed P&L with mean μ and standard deviation σ:

$$\text{VaR}_\alpha = -\mu + z_\alpha \sigma$$

where $$z_{0.95} = 1.645$$ and $$z_{0.99} = 2.326$$. With μ = 0 (standard for short horizons), VaR is simply $$z_\alpha \sigma$$.

**Limitations of VaR:**

| Limitation | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Says nothing about losses *beyond* the quantile | Two books with identical VaR can have very different tail severity |
| Not subadditive in general | Merging portfolios can *raise* measured risk — penalizes diversification, invites regulatory arbitrage by splitting desks |
| Depends on α and T choices | Easy to game by tuning parameters |
| Estimated from models/history | Model risk; calm samples understate risk |

{: .warning }
VaR is a **quantile, not a worst case**. "99% one-day VaR = $10M" means losses exceed $10M on about 1 day in 100 — it puts **no ceiling** on how bad that day is. Choosing "maximum loss" as the answer to a VaR definition question is the classic trap.

## Coherent risk measures

A risk measure $$\rho$$ is **coherent** if it satisfies all four:

| Property | Statement | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| **Monotonicity** | If $$X_1 \le X_2$$ in every state, $$\rho(X_1) \ge \rho(X_2)$$ | A portfolio that always pays less is at least as risky |
| **Subadditivity** | $$\rho(X_1+X_2) \le \rho(X_1) + \rho(X_2)$$ | Diversification cannot increase risk |
| **Positive homogeneity** | $$\rho(\lambda X) = \lambda \rho(X)$$, $$\lambda > 0$$ | Doubling the position doubles the risk |
| **Translation invariance** | $$\rho(X + c) = \rho(X) - c$$ | Adding cash $c$ reduces required capital one-for-one |

VaR satisfies monotonicity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance — but **fails subadditivity** for non-elliptical distributions (it is subadditive when returns are normal). ES satisfies all four, hence is coherent.

## Expected shortfall

ES (a.k.a. conditional VaR, expected tail loss) is the **expected loss given that the loss exceeds VaR**:

$$\text{ES}_\alpha = E[L \mid L > \text{VaR}_\alpha]$$

For normal P&L with mean μ, standard deviation σ:

$$\text{ES}_\alpha = -\mu + \sigma \frac{\phi(z_\alpha)}{1-\alpha}$$

where $$\phi$$ is the standard normal pdf: $$\phi(1.645) = 0.1031$$, $$\phi(2.326) = 0.0267$$.

ES is an average of all quantiles beyond α (equal weights on the tail). More generally, **spectral risk measures** weight quantiles by a non-decreasing risk-aversion function; ES is the simplest coherent member of that family. Giving *larger* weights to *worse* outcomes is exactly what subadditivity requires.

{: .important }
Always: $$\text{ES}_\alpha \ge \text{VaR}_\alpha$$ at the same α. ES answers "how bad is bad?"; VaR answers only "how often?"

### Worked example: parametric VaR and ES at 95% and 99%

A $10 million portfolio has daily P&L that is normal with mean 0 and daily volatility 2%.

Dollar σ = 0.02 × \$10M = \$200,000.

**95% VaR:** $$1.645 \times 200{,}000 = \$329{,}000$$

**99% VaR:** $$2.326 \times 200{,}000 = \$465{,}200$$

**95% ES:** $$200{,}000 \times \frac{0.1031}{0.05} = 200{,}000 \times 2.0627 = \$412{,}540$$

**99% ES:** $$200{,}000 \times \frac{0.0267}{0.01} = 200{,}000 \times 2.665 = \$533{,}000$$

Check the ordering: ES₉₅ (\$412.5K) > VaR₉₅ (\$329K), ES₉₉ (\$533K) > VaR₉₉ (\$465K). For the normal distribution, 97.5% ES ≈ 99% VaR (multiplier 2.338 vs 2.326) — the basis of the FRTB calibration.

### Worked example: VaR fails subadditivity

Two independent corporate bonds each: 3% probability of defaulting over the year with a \$10M loss; otherwise no loss.

**Each bond alone, 95% VaR:** P(loss) = 3% < 5%, so the 95% quantile of the loss distribution is **0**. VaR = \$0 each; sum = \$0.

**Portfolio of both:** P(at least one default) = $$1 - 0.97^2 = 5.91\% > 5\%$$, so the 95% quantile loss is **\$10M**.

$$\text{VaR}(A+B) = \$10\text{M} > \text{VaR}(A) + \text{VaR}(B) = \$0$$

Subadditivity is violated — the diversified portfolio shows *more* VaR than the parts. ES would not do this: each bond's 95% ES is $$\frac{0.03}{0.05}\times 10\text{M} = \$6\text{M}$$, and the portfolio ES (≈ \$10.3M, since the 5% tail is dominated by one-default states with a sliver of double defaults) is below the \$12M sum.

{: .tip }
On the exam, "which property does VaR violate?" → **subadditivity**, and the standard counterexample is exactly this two-bond setup with default probability between the two tail levels.

## VaR vs ES summary

| | VaR | ES |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Loss quantile | Mean loss beyond the quantile |
| Coherent? | No (fails subadditivity) | Yes |
| Tail information | None beyond cutoff | Full tail average |
| Backtesting | Easy (count exceedances) | Harder (needs tail magnitudes) |
| Regulatory use | Basel II market risk (99%, 10-day) | FRTB (97.5% ES) |

## Quick quiz

**1.** A portfolio's daily P&L is normal with mean zero and σ = \$1.5M. The 99% one-day VaR is closest to:
- A. \$2.47M
- B. \$2.94M
- C. \$3.49M
- D. \$1.96M

**2.** Which property of coherence does VaR fail to satisfy in general?
- A. Monotonicity
- B. Translation invariance
- C. Subadditivity
- D. Positive homogeneity

**3.** For the same portfolio and confidence level, expected shortfall is:
- A. Always less than VaR
- B. Always at least as large as VaR
- C. Equal to VaR only for fat-tailed distributions
- D. Unrelated to VaR

**4.** The mean-variance framework gives correct risk rankings when returns are:
- A. Skewed left
- B. Elliptically distributed
- C. From short option positions
- D. Any distribution, provided σ is estimated accurately

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. C.** 2.326 × 1.5M = \$3.49M. (\$2.47M uses z = 1.645; \$2.94M uses z = 1.96.)

**2. C.** Subadditivity — merging positions can increase VaR when distributions are non-elliptical.

**3. B.** ES averages losses beyond VaR, so ES ≥ VaR always.

**4. B.** Elliptical (e.g., normal) distributions are fully described by mean and variance; skewed/fat-tailed ones are not.
