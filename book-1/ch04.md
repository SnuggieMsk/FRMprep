# 4. Credit Risk Transfer Mechanisms

How credit risk is shed without selling the loan: traditional mitigants, credit derivatives (CDS and friends), and securitization. GARP tests the mechanics, the comparison across instruments, and the role credit transfer played in the 2007–09 crisis.

## Learning objectives

- Compare different types of credit derivatives, and explain their applications and advantages.
- Explain the traditional approaches firms use to mitigate credit risk (insurance/guarantees, netting, marking to market, collateralization, termination/put features).
- Evaluate the role of credit derivatives in the 2007–2009 crisis and explain how the credit derivatives market changed afterward.
- Explain the process of securitization, describe a special purpose vehicle (SPV), and assess the risks of the originate-to-distribute versus buy-and-hold business models.

## Traditional credit risk mitigants

| Mechanism | How it transfers/reduces credit risk | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|
| **Purchasing insurance / bank guarantees / letters of credit** | A third party promises to pay if the obligor defaults | You now hold the guarantor's credit risk (wrong-way risk if guarantor is correlated with obligor) |
| **Netting** | Offsetting mutual obligations so only the net amount is at risk on default (close-out netting under an ISDA master agreement) | Legal enforceability across jurisdictions |
| **Marking to market** | Periodic settlement of gains/losses resets exposure toward zero | Requires liquidity to pay variation amounts |
| **Collateralization** | Counterparty posts assets (margin) against the exposure | Collateral value can fall with the exposure; haircuts and rehypothecation issues; margin calls create liquidity risk |
| **Termination / put options** | Right to terminate the contract or put it back at mid-market on a credit trigger (e.g., downgrade clauses) | Triggers can accelerate a counterparty's death spiral (AIG's downgrade collateral calls) |

{: .note }
These mitigants mostly *reduce* exposure to a given counterparty; credit derivatives and securitization go further — they *transfer* the credit risk itself to another investor.

## Credit derivatives

**Credit default swap (CDS).** The protection buyer pays a periodic spread (premium); on a defined **credit event** (bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring) of the reference entity, the protection seller pays par minus recovery (cash settled via auction, or physical delivery). Economically: insurance on a bond, without requiring ownership of the bond.

- **Single-name CDS** — one reference entity.
- **Index CDS / portfolio products** (e.g., CDX, iTraxx) — standardized baskets.
- **First-to-default basket** — seller covers the first default in a small basket; cheaper protection than buying CDS on every name.

**Total return swap (TRS).** One party pays the *total return* (coupons + price appreciation) of a reference asset and receives a floating rate plus spread; the receiver of total return gains the asset's full economics — **both credit and market risk** — without funding it on balance sheet.

**Credit-linked note (CLN).** A *funded* structure: the investor buys a note whose principal is written down if the reference entity defaults. Because the investor pays upfront, the protection buyer bears **no counterparty risk** on the protection.

**Collateralized debt obligation (CDO).** Securitization technology applied to a pool of credit assets (bonds, loans, CDS in synthetic CDOs) with tranched liabilities — see below.

| Instrument | Funded? | Risk transferred | Counterparty risk to protection buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| CDS | No | Credit (default) risk only | Yes — seller may fail (AIG) |
| TRS | No | Credit **and** market risk | Yes |
| CLN | Yes | Credit risk | No — cash held upfront |
| CDO tranche | Yes | Pooled, tranched credit risk | No (funded), but model/correlation risk |

**Applications/advantages:** banks can hedge concentrations while preserving client relationships (no loan sale notification), free regulatory/economic capital, take short credit positions, and customize exposures; investors gain access to credit risk they could not originate.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: a **CDS transfers credit risk but creates counterparty risk** on the protection seller — and if the seller's health is correlated with the reference credit (monoline insurers, AIG insuring mortgage CDOs), that is **wrong-way risk**: the hedge fails exactly when needed. Also, a TRS transfers *total* return (market + credit), while a CDS transfers default risk only.

## Securitization and SPVs

**Process:**
1. Originator pools assets (mortgages, auto loans, credit cards, corporate loans).
2. Sells the pool to a bankruptcy-remote **special purpose vehicle (SPV/SPE)** — a true sale isolates the assets from the originator's insolvency.
3. The SPV issues **tranches** of securities against the pool's cash flows: senior (first claim, lowest yield, highest rating), mezzanine, and equity/first-loss (last claim, highest yield).
4. Losses flow bottom-up (equity first); cash flows top-down (the **waterfall**). Credit enhancement: subordination, overcollateralization, excess spread, reserve accounts.

**Why securitize:** funding diversification, balance-sheet/capital relief, risk transfer to investors with appetite for it, liquidity for otherwise illiquid loans.

**Re-securitization:** CDOs built from mezzanine ABS tranches ("ABS CDOs," CDO-squared) concentrated and obscured subprime risk — central to Ch 10.

## Business models: originate-to-distribute vs. buy-and-hold

| | Buy-and-hold | Originate-to-distribute (OTD) |
|---|---|---|
| Who bears credit risk | Originating bank, to maturity | Investors in the securitization |
| Incentive to screen/monitor borrowers | Strong — bank eats its own losses | Weak — "skin in the game" problem; volume-driven underwriting |
| Funding/capital | Heavy balance sheet usage | Capital-light, fee-driven |
| Key risks | Concentration, credit cycle | **Pipeline/warehouse risk** (loans stuck on balance sheet when markets close), moral hazard, reputational/legal risk from bad originations |

The OTD model's eroded underwriting incentives were a core cause of the subprime debacle. Post-crisis reforms added **risk retention ("skin in the game")** rules requiring originators/sponsors to keep a slice (e.g., 5%) of securitized deals.

## Credit derivatives in the crisis — and after

**Role in the crisis:** CDS and synthetic CDOs multiplied exposure to subprime far beyond the cash mortgage market; protection sellers (AIG Financial Products, monolines) wrote massive one-way positions with little collateral; opaque bilateral OTC webs made counterparty exposures unknowable, amplifying panic after Lehman; ratings on tranches proved wildly optimistic (correlation assumptions, agency conflicts).

**Post-crisis changes:**
- **Central clearing** of standardized CDS through CCPs; trade reporting to repositories (transparency).
- **Standardization** (Big Bang protocol: fixed coupons, auction settlement, determinations committees for credit events).
- **Margin requirements** for non-cleared derivatives; higher capital for counterparty risk (CVA charges).
- Market shrank and concentrated in index products; single-name liquidity declined.

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** A bank wants to hedge default risk on a loan to a key client without selling the loan or telling the client. The most direct instrument is:
A. A total return swap paying the loan's return  B. Buying a single-name CDS on the borrower  C. Issuing a credit-linked note  D. Securitizing its entire loan book

**Q2.** Which credit risk transfer instrument leaves the protection buyer with *no* counterparty risk on the protection provider?
A. CDS  B. TRS  C. CLN  D. Bank guarantee

**Q3.** The originate-to-distribute model primarily weakens:
A. The originator's incentive to screen and monitor borrowers
B. The SPV's bankruptcy remoteness
C. Investors' access to credit risk
D. The waterfall priority of senior tranches

**Q4.** In a securitization, the first losses on the asset pool are absorbed by:
A. The senior tranche  B. The mezzanine tranche  C. The equity (first-loss) tranche  D. The originating bank's depositors

**Q1 — B.** CDS hedges default risk discreetly, relationship preserved; a TRS would also shed market risk and a CLN/securitization is heavier machinery.

**Q2 — C.** A credit-linked note is funded upfront — the buyer of protection already holds the cash.

**Q3 — A.** When risk is passed to investors, the originator's skin in the game — and underwriting discipline — disappears.

**Q4 — C.** Losses flow bottom-up: equity, then mezzanine, then senior.
