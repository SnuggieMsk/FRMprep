# 2. How Do Firms Manage Financial Risk?

This reading covers the corporate decision of *whether* and *how* to manage risk: risk appetite, the hedge-or-not debate, and the mechanics and pitfalls of implementing a hedging program. Tested mostly qualitatively, with scenario questions on choosing the right strategy.

## Learning objectives

- Compare the strategies a firm can use to manage its risk exposures (accept/retain, avoid, mitigate, transfer) and explain when each is appropriate.
- Explain the relationship between risk appetite and a firm's risk management decisions.
- Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of hedging, and explain the challenges that arise when implementing a hedging strategy.
- Apply appropriate methods to hedge operational and financial risks, including pricing risk, foreign currency risk, and interest rate risk.
- Assess the impact of risk management tools and instruments, including risk limits and derivatives.

## The four risk management strategies

| Strategy | What it means | Appropriate when |
|---|---|---|
| **Accept (retain)** | Keep the risk, price it, hold capital/reserves against it | The risk is core to the business model and the firm has an edge in bearing it (a bank retaining credit risk it can assess) |
| **Avoid** | Exit or never enter the activity | Risk is outside appetite/expertise and not compensated |
| **Mitigate** | Reduce likelihood or severity while staying in the activity | Controls, diversification, collateral, limits are cost-effective |
| **Transfer** | Shift the risk to a third party at a price | Risk is non-core and a deep market exists (insurance, derivatives, securitization) |

The right choice flows from comparing the risk to the firm's **risk appetite** and asking whether the firm is *paid* to hold it.

## Risk appetite drives the decisions

- **Risk appetite**: the amount and types of risk the firm is *willing* to take in pursuit of its objectives — set by the board (Ch 3).
- **Risk capacity**: the maximum risk the firm *can* absorb given capital, liquidity, and franchise.
- **Risk profile**: the risk actually being taken at a point in time.

Appetite should sit within capacity; the risk function's job is to keep the profile within appetite. A clear **risk appetite statement** (qualitative narrative plus quantitative limits — e.g., maximum earnings-at-risk, minimum capital ratio, no exposure to specified products) is the bridge between board strategy and desk-level limits.

{: .note }
Mapping risk appetite into actual exposures is hard in practice: appetite is firm-wide and slow-moving; exposures are granular and fast-moving. The translation layer is the **limit framework**.

## Should the firm hedge at all?

In a Modigliani–Miller world (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, frictionless markets), hedging adds **no value** — shareholders can diversify or hedge on their own account. Hedging adds value only through real-world frictions:

**Arguments for hedging**
- Reduces the probability and cost of **financial distress** and bankruptcy.
- Stabilizes earnings and cash flow → supports planning, investment, and debt capacity; avoids underinvestment when internal funds are scarce.
- Can lower expected **taxes** when the tax schedule is convex (progressive).
- Lets management focus on core business risks where it has an edge.
- May reduce the cost of capital and widen the investor base.

**Arguments against hedging**
- Shareholders can hedge themselves, often more cheaply (M&M logic).
- Hedging has direct costs (spreads, fees, collateral) and indirect costs (management time, systems).
- Hedges can **mask** underlying performance and create compliance/accounting complexity.
- A "hedge" can become a speculative position if mis-sized or mis-specified — and hedging *expected, priced* risks destroys value.

## Implementation challenges

- **Mapping the exposure**: hedge accounting-flat board statements rarely translate cleanly to hedgeable market exposures.
- **Static vs. dynamic hedging**: a static hedge is set-and-forget; a dynamic hedge requires rebalancing as prices move (cost, model risk, and liquidity risk — Metallgesellschaft, Ch 9).
- **Basis risk**: the hedge instrument does not track the exposure perfectly (different grade, location, maturity, index).
- **Rollover/maturity mismatch**: hedging a long-dated exposure with short-dated contracts exposes the firm to margin calls and roll prices.
- **Time horizon and accounting**: derivatives mark to market through earnings while the hedged item may not — creating *reported* earnings volatility even when the economic hedge is sound.
- **Governance**: who may hedge, with what instruments, against which board-approved objectives — must be written down before the first trade.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: a hedge that lowers economic risk can still **increase accounting earnings volatility** (mark-to-market on the derivative, accrual on the underlying). Don't equate "earnings got more volatile" with "the hedge failed." And remember Metallgesellschaft: economically sensible hedges can still kill a firm through **funding liquidity** (margin calls) before maturity.

## Hedging the major financial exposures

**Pricing risk (input/output prices).** Lock in commodity input costs or output prices with forwards/futures, or cap them with options. Forwards remove upside and downside; options keep the upside for a premium.

**Foreign currency risk.** Three layers:
- **Transaction risk** — known future FX cash flows: hedge with FX forwards/futures, money-market hedges, or options.
- **Translation risk** — accounting consolidation of foreign subsidiaries: often left unhedged or hedged with FX debt.
- **Economic risk** — long-run competitive exposure to FX levels: managed structurally (locating production, sourcing).

**Interest rate risk.** Swap floating for fixed (or vice versa) with **interest rate swaps**; cap floating-rate costs with **caps**; lock future borrowing rates with **FRAs/futures**. Banks manage the repricing gap between assets and liabilities (asset-liability management).

## Tools: limits and derivatives

**Risk limits** convert appetite into enforceable constraints: notional limits, stop-loss limits, VaR limits, concentration limits, tenor limits. Good limit regimes have clear ownership, monitoring, and escalation for breaches — a limit that is routinely waived is not a limit.

**Derivatives** are the workhorse transfer instruments:

| Instrument | Payoff shape | Upfront cost | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forward/futures | Linear, symmetric | None (margin for futures) | Lock a price/rate |
| Swap | Linear, symmetric, multi-period | None | Transform cash flow profile (fixed↔floating, currency) |
| Option (cap/floor) | Asymmetric — keeps favorable side | Premium | Insure against adverse moves while keeping upside |

**Exchange-traded** instruments are standardized, margined, and nearly free of counterparty risk but bring basis risk and margin liquidity demands; **OTC** instruments are tailored but carry counterparty credit risk (now mitigated by central clearing and collateral, Ch 4).

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** An airline is highly exposed to jet fuel prices, a risk it has no informational edge in bearing. Deep, liquid futures and options markets exist. The most appropriate strategy is:
A. Accept  B. Avoid  C. Transfer/hedge  D. Increase the exposure to earn the risk premium

**Q2.** Which of the following is the strongest *theoretical* argument that hedging adds shareholder value?
A. Shareholders cannot diversify firm-specific risk
B. Hedging reduces expected bankruptcy and financial distress costs
C. Hedging always increases expected cash flows
D. Hedging removes systematic risk from equity returns

**Q3.** A firm hedges a 10-year oil delivery commitment by rolling short-dated futures. Oil prices fall sharply. The firm's most immediate danger is:
A. Basis risk between crude grades
B. Counterparty default on the futures
C. Margin calls on the futures exceeding available liquidity
D. Loss of the upside on the physical commitment

**Q4.** A firm's board states it is "willing to tolerate a one-year earnings decline of up to 20% in pursuit of growth." This statement is best described as part of its:
A. Risk capacity  B. Risk appetite  C. Risk profile  D. Risk limit framework

**Q1 — C.** Non-core, uncompensated risk with a deep transfer market: hedge it.

**Q2 — B.** With M&M frictions removed, value comes from reducing distress costs (also taxes, underinvestment); A is false and C–D are not generally true.

**Q3 — C.** The stack-and-roll structure marks the futures to market daily while the long-dated commitment pays off later — the Metallgesellschaft funding squeeze.

**Q4 — B.** Willingness to take risk in pursuit of objectives = appetite; capacity is the maximum absorbable, profile is the actual position.
