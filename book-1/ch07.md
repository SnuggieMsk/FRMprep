# 7. Risk-Adjusted Return on Capital and Performance Measurement

Raw return tells you nothing until you know the risk taken to earn it. This chapter covers the CAPM-based performance ratios (Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen's alpha), benchmark-relative measures (tracking error, information ratio), downside measures (Sortino), and the banking-world workhorse RAROC. Heavily tested — calculations and "which measure for which situation."

## Learning objectives

- Calculate, compare, and interpret the Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio, and Jensen's alpha.
- Compute and interpret tracking error, the information ratio, and the Sortino ratio.
- Explain which performance measure is appropriate for a given portfolio context (total portfolio vs. sub-portfolio, benchmark-relative mandates, asymmetric return profiles).
- Define risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC), compute RAROC for a business or project, and explain its uses in capital allocation, pricing, and performance measurement.
- Explain the hurdle rate, compute adjusted RAROC (ARAROC), and apply the decision rule for accepting a project.
- Describe the challenges and limitations of risk-adjusted performance measures.

## The classic trio

**Sharpe ratio** — excess return per unit of **total risk**:

$$S_p = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\sigma_p}$$

**Treynor ratio** — excess return per unit of **systematic risk**:

$$T_p = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\beta_p}$$

**Jensen's alpha** — return above the CAPM-required return:

$$\alpha_p = E[R_p] - \left[R_f + \beta_p\left(E[R_m] - R_f\right)\right]$$

| Measure | Risk used | Use when | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sharpe | $$\sigma_p$$ (total) | Evaluating an investor's **entire** portfolio, or undiversified holdings | Slope of the line from $$R_f$$ through the portfolio in $$(\sigma, E[R])$$ space; market's Sharpe = CML slope |
| Treynor | $$\beta_p$$ (systematic) | A **well-diversified sub-portfolio** within a larger diversified whole | Idiosyncratic risk already diversified away, so only beta matters |
| Jensen's alpha | $$\beta_p$$ | Testing skill vs. the CAPM benchmark; comparing funds with **similar betas** | An absolute (percent) measure, not a ratio — can't rank funds of different beta by alpha alone |

{: .warning }
The single most-tested trap in Book 1: **Sharpe divides by total risk (σ); Treynor divides by beta.** A poorly diversified fund can look fine on Treynor (and have positive alpha) yet terrible on Sharpe, because its idiosyncratic risk inflates σ but not β. If a question says "this fund is the investor's entire wealth," use Sharpe; "one of many funds held," Treynor/alpha.

## Benchmark-relative and downside measures

**Tracking error** — volatility of active returns versus a benchmark:

$$TE = \sigma\!\left(R_p - R_b\right)$$

**Information ratio** — average active return per unit of tracking error:

$$IR = \frac{E[R_p] - E[R_b]}{TE}$$

Used to judge active managers against their mandated benchmark; an IR sustained above ~0.5 is considered strong. (Some texts put alpha rather than raw active return in the numerator — the exam convention is active return over tracking error.)

**Sortino ratio** — penalizes only **downside** volatility, measured against a minimum acceptable return $$R_{min}$$ (often the risk-free rate or zero):

$$\text{Sortino} = \frac{E[R_p] - R_{min}}{\sigma_{down}},\qquad
\sigma_{down} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{R_t < R_{min}} (R_t - R_{min})^2}$$

Preferred when return distributions are **asymmetric** (options strategies, hedge funds): upside volatility shouldn't be punished.

### Worked example 1 — ranking two funds

| | Fund A | Fund B | Market |
|---|---|---|---|
| Return | 12% | 10% | 9% |
| σ | 24% | 14% | 15% |
| β | 1.1 | 0.9 | 1.0 |

Risk-free rate 2%.

Sharpe: $$S_A = \frac{12-2}{24} = 0.417$$, $$S_B = \frac{10-2}{14} = 0.571$$, market $$= \frac{9-2}{15} = 0.467$$.

Treynor: $$T_A = \frac{12-2}{1.1} = 9.09\%$$, $$T_B = \frac{10-2}{0.9} = 8.89\%$$, market $$= 7\%$$.

Jensen: $$\alpha_A = 12 - [2 + 1.1(7)] = +2.3\%$$, $$\alpha_B = 10 - [2 + 0.9(7)] = +1.7\%$$.

Interpretation: A wins on Treynor and alpha (better per unit of *systematic* risk), but B wins on Sharpe — A carries heavy idiosyncratic risk. As a stand-alone total portfolio choose **B**; as one sleeve inside a diversified program, **A** adds more.

### Worked example 2 — information ratio and Sortino

A manager returns 11% vs. a benchmark's 8.5%, with tracking error 5%:

$$IR = \frac{11\% - 8.5\%}{5\%} = 0.50$$

Same fund: mean return 11%, minimum acceptable return 3%, downside deviation (vs. 3%) of 9%:

$$\text{Sortino} = \frac{11\% - 3\%}{9\%} = 0.89$$

## RAROC: risk-adjusted return on capital

Banks need one yardstick that works across businesses with very different risk: RAROC charges each business for the **economic capital** its risk consumes.

$$RAROC = \frac{\text{expected after-tax risk-adjusted net income}}{\text{economic capital}}$$

Expanded numerator (one period):

$$RAROC = \frac{\text{revenues} - \text{costs} - \text{expected losses} - \text{taxes} + \text{return on economic capital} \pm \text{transfer prices}}{\text{economic capital}}$$

- **Expected losses** are deducted as a cost (they are priced, not capitalized — Ch 1).
- **Economic capital** is the buffer for **unexpected** losses at the firm's target confidence level (e.g., the level consistent with an AA rating); it is risk-based, unlike book or regulatory capital.
- **Return on economic capital**: the capital itself is invested in safe assets and earns roughly the risk-free rate — added back to income.

**Uses:** comparing and allocating capital across businesses, risk-based pricing of loans/deals, identifying value-creating vs. value-destroying units, and risk-adjusted compensation.

**Decision rule:** accept activities whose RAROC exceeds the **hurdle rate** — the firm's cost of the capital backing the business (commonly the weighted cost of equity capital, e.g., across common and preferred equity).

## Adjusted RAROC

Plain RAROC ignores the *systematic* riskiness of the business's returns — a high-beta business should clear a higher bar. **ARAROC** corrects this with the business's CAPM beta:

$$ARAROC = RAROC - \beta_E\left(E[R_m] - R_f\right)$$

**Rule: accept the project if $$ARAROC > R_f$$** (equivalently, if RAROC exceeds the CAPM-consistent required return $$R_f + \beta_E(E[R_m]-R_f)$$).

### Worked example 3 — RAROC and ARAROC

A lending unit: loan book \$1 billion, revenue (interest + fees) \$90 million, operating costs \$25 million, expected losses \$20 million. Economic capital is \$75 million, invested at the risk-free rate of 4%. Ignore taxes and transfer prices.

$$RAROC = \frac{90 - 25 - 20 + 0.04 \times 75}{75} = \frac{48}{75} = 64\%\ \text{of capital} \;\Rightarrow\; 48/75 = 0.64$$

So RAROC = **64%**... which exceeds any plausible hurdle — accept. Now a riskier version: revenue \$50m, costs \$15m, expected losses \$22m, economic capital \$150m, $$R_f=4\%$$:

$$RAROC = \frac{50 - 15 - 22 + 0.04\times150}{150} = \frac{19}{150} = 12.67\%$$

If the business's equity beta is 1.3 and the market premium is 6%:

$$ARAROC = 12.67\% - 1.3 \times 6\% = 12.67\% - 7.8\% = 4.87\% > R_f = 4\%$$

Marginally acceptable: after charging for systematic risk, the unit still clears the risk-free bar — but barely, so small estimation errors flip the verdict.

## Challenges with risk-adjusted measures

- **Economic capital and beta are model outputs** — wrong confidence levels, correlations, or betas distort rankings.
- **Diversification effects**: a unit's stand-alone capital exceeds its marginal contribution to firm-wide capital; how the diversification benefit is allocated changes every unit's RAROC.
- **Horizon problems**: one-year capital for multi-year businesses; smoothing and gaming of expected-loss estimates.
- **Backward-looking inputs** (historical σ, β, TE) may not represent forward risk; ratios computed on short samples are noisy.
- Ratios mislead when returns are non-normal — a short-options book shows a beautiful Sharpe until the tail event (see Niederhoffer, Ch 9).

{: .tip }
Know the decision contexts cold: **Sharpe** = total portfolio; **Treynor/alpha** = diversified component; **IR** = benchmark mandate; **Sortino** = asymmetric returns; **RAROC vs. hurdle / ARAROC vs. risk-free** = bank capital allocation.

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** Fund X is one of forty funds inside a large, well-diversified pension plan. The most appropriate single measure for evaluating X's contribution is:
A. Sharpe ratio  B. Treynor ratio  C. Sortino ratio  D. Tracking error

**Q2.** A portfolio returns 9% with σ = 18% and β = 0.8; the market returns 8% with σ = 14%; the risk-free rate is 3%. Jensen's alpha is:
A. +1.0%  B. +2.0%  C. −1.0%  D. +4.0%

**Q3.** A business line has RAROC of 15%. Its equity beta is 1.5, the expected market return is 10%, and the risk-free rate is 4%. Under the ARAROC rule, the project should be:
A. Accepted, since ARAROC = 6% > 4%
B. Rejected, since ARAROC = 6% < 10%
C. Accepted, since RAROC > the market return
D. Rejected, since ARAROC = 0% < 4%

**Q4.** In the RAROC numerator, expected losses are:
A. Ignored — they are covered by economic capital
B. Deducted — they are a cost of doing business
C. Added back — they are non-cash
D. Multiplied by beta

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**Q1 — B.** Within a diversified whole, only systematic risk matters: Treynor (or Jensen's alpha).

**Q2 — B.** $$\alpha = 9 - [3 + 0.8(8-3)] = 9 - 7 = +2\%$$.

**Q3 — A.** $$ARAROC = 15\% - 1.5(10\%-4\%) = 15\% - 9\% = 6\% > R_f = 4\%$$ → accept.

**Q4 — B.** EL is an anticipated cost, deducted from revenue; capital covers **unexpected** losses.
