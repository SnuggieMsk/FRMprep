# 6. The Arbitrage Pricing Theory and Multifactor Models of Risk and Return

CAPM's one factor becomes many: APT's no-arbitrage logic, single- and multifactor return models, factor hedging, and Fama–French. Expect expected-return calculations from factor betas and conceptual APT-vs-CAPM comparisons.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the arbitrage pricing theory (APT), describe its assumptions, and compare APT to the CAPM.
- Describe the inputs (including factor betas) to a multifactor model.
- Calculate the expected return of an asset using a single-factor and a multifactor model.
- Explain how to construct a portfolio to hedge exposure to multiple factors, and describe the challenges of using multifactor models in hedging.
- Describe and apply the Fama–French three-factor model in estimating asset returns.

## The return-generating model

A $$K$$-factor model decomposes the realized return of asset $$i$$:

$$R_i = E[R_i] + \beta_{i1}F_1 + \beta_{i2}F_2 + \cdots + \beta_{iK}F_K + e_i$$

- $$E[R_i]$$ — expected return (compensation priced ex ante).
- $$F_k$$ — the **surprise** in factor $$k$$: actual value minus expected value. By construction $$E[F_k]=0$$.
- $$\beta_{ik}$$ — the factor beta/loading: sensitivity of asset $$i$$ to factor $$k$$.
- $$e_i$$ — firm-specific (idiosyncratic) return, uncorrelated across assets and with the factors.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: only the **unanticipated** component of a macro announcement moves returns in these models. If expected inflation was 2% and actual inflation is 2%, the inflation factor surprise is **zero** — no effect, however "high" 2% sounds. Always compute surprise = actual − expected before multiplying by beta.

## Arbitrage pricing theory

**Assumptions** (much weaker than CAPM's):

1. Returns are generated by a factor model.
2. There are enough assets to diversify away idiosyncratic risk.
3. Well-functioning markets allow no persistent arbitrage opportunities.

**Conclusion:** expected returns must be linear in factor betas, or arbitrageurs would exploit the gap:

$$E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_{i1}\lambda_1 + \beta_{i2}\lambda_2 + \cdots + \beta_{iK}\lambda_K$$

where $$\lambda_k$$ is the **risk premium** of factor $$k$$ (the excess return of a pure factor portfolio with $$\beta_k = 1$$ and zero loading on all other factors).

**APT vs. CAPM**

| | CAPM | APT |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | Equilibrium: all investors optimize mean–variance | No-arbitrage: a few arbitrageurs enforce pricing |
| Factors | One — the market portfolio | Multiple — unspecified by the theory |
| Key assumptions | Homogeneous expectations, mean–variance investors, market portfolio | Factor structure, diversification, no arbitrage |
| Market portfolio needed? | Yes (unobservable — Roll critique) | No |
| Guidance on factors | Defines the single factor | Silent — both strength (flexibility) and weakness |
| Holds for | Every asset exactly | Well-diversified portfolios; near-exactly for most single assets |

{: .note }
APT does **not** assume homogeneous expectations or that everyone holds the market — it only needs *some* investors who will arbitrage mispricing in well-diversified portfolios. The price of this generality: APT does not tell you what the factors are.

## Inputs and challenges

Inputs to a multifactor model: the chosen **factors** (macro surprises like industrial production, inflation, term spread, credit spread; or style factors like size and value), each asset's **factor betas** (estimated by regression), the **factor risk premiums**, and the **idiosyncratic variance**.

Challenges, especially for hedging:
- Betas are estimated with error and **drift over time**.
- Factor structure may be misspecified (omitted factors show up as "alpha" or unhedged risk).
- Hedging many factors requires many positions — transaction costs and shorting constraints bite.
- Correlations among factor proxies complicate clean isolation of one exposure.

## Hedging factor exposures

A portfolio's factor exposure is the weighted average of component betas. To hedge factor $$k$$, add positions (often factor portfolios or futures) so the **net beta to that factor is zero**, while leaving desired exposures intact. A **factor portfolio** (pure factor play) has beta 1 to one factor and 0 to all others — the natural hedging building block. Hedging all factor exposures leaves only idiosyncratic risk plus alpha (a "market-neutral" stance); idiosyncratic risk is then reduced by holding many names, not by hedging.

### Worked example 1 — two-factor expected return (APT)

A stock has betas of 1.2 on the GDP factor and −0.5 on the inflation factor. Factor risk premiums: $$\lambda_{GDP} = 4\%$$, $$\lambda_{INF} = 2\%$$. Risk-free rate 3%.

$$E[R] = 3\% + 1.2(4\%) + (-0.5)(2\%) = 3\% + 4.8\% - 1.0\% = 6.8\%$$

Now suppose over the next year GDP growth comes in 1% **above** expectations and inflation 0.5% **below** expectations, with no firm-specific news. Realized return:

$$R = 6.8\% + 1.2(+1\%) + (-0.5)(-0.5\%) = 6.8\% + 1.2\% + 0.25\% = 8.25\%$$

### Worked example 2 — hedging a factor exposure

A $100 million portfolio has $$\beta_{GDP} = 0.8$$ and $$\beta_{INF} = 0.4$$. The manager wants zero GDP exposure but wishes to keep the inflation exposure. Available: a pure GDP factor portfolio ($$\beta_{GDP}=1, \beta_{INF}=0$$).

Required hedge: short the GDP factor portfolio in size $$0.8 \times \$100\text{m} = \$80\text{m}$$. Net GDP beta: $$0.8 - 0.8 = 0$$; inflation beta unchanged at 0.4. If instead she wanted both factors at zero, she would also short $$0.4 \times \$100\text{m} = \$40\text{m}$$ of a pure inflation factor portfolio, leaving only alpha and idiosyncratic risk.

### Worked example 3 — Fama–French

Fund Z has loadings $$\beta_m = 1.1$$, $$\beta_{SMB} = 0.6$$, $$\beta_{HML} = -0.3$$. Premiums: market 6%, SMB 2.5%, HML 3.5%; $$R_f = 2\%$$.

$$E[R_Z] = 2\% + 1.1(6\%) + 0.6(2.5\%) + (-0.3)(3.5\%) = 2\% + 6.6\% + 1.5\% - 1.05\% = 9.05\%$$

The negative HML loading marks Z as growth-tilted; its small-cap tilt (positive SMB) adds expected return.

## The Fama–French three-factor model

$$E[R_i] - R_f = \beta_{i,m}\left(E[R_m] - R_f\right) + \beta_{i,SMB}\,E[SMB] + \beta_{i,HML}\,E[HML]$$

- **SMB** ("small minus big"): return of small-cap stocks minus large-cap stocks — the **size** premium.
- **HML** ("high minus low"): return of high book-to-market (**value**) stocks minus low book-to-market (**growth**) stocks — the **value** premium.
- Motivation: empirically, small and value stocks earned more than CAPM predicts; FF captures this with two long–short style factors. A momentum factor (UMD/WML, Carhart) is a common fourth.
- Interpretation debate: risk premia for distress-type risk vs. behavioral mispricing — the model is agnostic.

{: .tip }
Sign conventions matter: a **positive HML beta = value tilt**, negative = growth tilt; positive SMB beta = small-cap tilt. GARP questions often just test whether you can read tilts off the loadings and add up the premium terms correctly (watch the negative loading!).

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** Relative to the CAPM, the APT does NOT require:
A. A linear relation between expected return and factor betas
B. That investors hold the market portfolio under homogeneous expectations
C. Absence of arbitrage opportunities
D. A factor structure for returns

**Q2.** Expected inflation is 3%; actual inflation prints 3%. A stock has an inflation factor beta of −1.2. The effect of the announcement on its return under a factor model is:
A. −3.6%  B. +3.6%  C. 0%  D. −1.2%

**Q3.** A stock has $$\beta_1 = 0.9$$ (factor premium 5%) and $$\beta_2 = 0.2$$ (factor premium 1%); $$R_f = 4\%$$. APT expected return:
A. 8.7%  B. 4.7%  C. 9.5%  D. 10.7%

**Q4.** A portfolio with a significantly negative HML loading and near-zero SMB loading is best described as tilted toward:
A. Small-cap value  B. Large-cap value  C. Growth  D. High-dividend stocks

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**Q1 — B.** APT replaces the equilibrium/market-portfolio machinery with no-arbitrage; it keeps the factor structure and linear pricing.

**Q2 — C.** Surprise = actual − expected = 0; only surprises move returns in factor models.

**Q3 — A.** $$4\% + 0.9(5\%) + 0.2(1\%) = 4\% + 4.5\% + 0.2\% = 8.7\%$$.

**Q4 — C.** Negative HML = growth tilt; SMB ≈ 0 says nothing strong about size.
