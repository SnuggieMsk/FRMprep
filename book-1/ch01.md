# 1. The Building Blocks of Risk Management

GARP's opening reading defines what risk is, lays out the risk management process, and builds the taxonomy of risk types that the rest of the curriculum hangs on. Expect definitional questions, expected-vs-unexpected-loss distinctions, and risk-classification questions.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the concept of risk and compare risk management with risk taking.
- Describe the risk management process and identify the problems and challenges that can weaken it.
- Evaluate and apply tools and procedures used to measure and manage risk, including quantitative measures, qualitative assessment, and enterprise risk management.
- Distinguish between expected loss and unexpected loss and provide examples of each.
- Interpret the relationship between risk and reward, and explain how conflicts of interest can impact risk management.
- Describe and differentiate between the key classes of risks, explain how each can arise, and assess its potential impact.
- Explain how risk factors interact and describe the challenges in aggregating risk exposures.

## What risk is — and what risk management is not

**Risk** is the *variability* (uncertainty) of outcomes — especially adverse outcomes — not the expected cost itself. A cost that is known with certainty is an expense, not a risk.

Risk management is **not** about minimizing or eliminating risk. It is about:

1. Understanding which risks the firm is exposed to,
2. Deciding which risks to retain (because the firm is paid to bear them) and which to lay off,
3. Keeping retained risks within a deliberate **risk appetite**.

Risk management and **risk taking** are two sides of the same coin: a firm earns returns only by taking risk, so the discipline is about taking the *right* risks, in the *right* amount, knowingly.

{: .note }
Risk management can reduce the *likelihood and severity* of bad outcomes, but it cannot guarantee them away — and a firm that "never loses money" is probably not taking enough risk to earn its cost of capital.

## The risk management process

The standard loop GARP tests:

1. **Identify** the risks (including hidden, correlated, and emerging risks).
2. **Analyze and measure** — quantify exposure, likelihood, severity.
3. **Assess the impact** — on earnings, capital, liquidity, reputation.
4. **Manage** — choose among **avoid, retain (and price), mitigate, transfer**.
5. **Monitor and report** — limits, dashboards, escalation; iterate.

Challenges that weaken the process:

- **Identification failures** — unknown or newly emerging risks; risks hidden in complex products.
- **Measurement failures** — model risk, bad data, fat tails, unstable correlations.
- **Communication failures** — risk reports that the board cannot act on.
- **Incentive failures** — traders rewarded for return without a charge for risk.
- **Risk aggregation** — exposures measured in silos understate or overstate firm-wide risk because correlations shift, especially in crises.

## Tools: quantitative, qualitative, and enterprise-wide

| Tool family | Examples | Best at | Weak at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quantitative measures | VaR, expected shortfall, economic capital, scenario P&L, stress tests | Comparable, aggregable numbers | Tail behavior, model risk, "unknown unknowns" |
| Qualitative assessment | Scenario analysis, risk identification workshops, expert judgment, risk ratings | New/rare risks, narrative plausibility | Consistency, comparability |
| Enterprise risk management (ERM) | Firm-wide risk appetite, integrated reporting, unified risk culture | Interactions and concentrations across silos | Costly, organizationally hard (see Ch 8) |

**VaR** states a loss threshold not exceeded with a given confidence over a given horizon; **expected shortfall** averages the losses beyond that threshold, so it captures tail severity that VaR ignores. **Scenario analysis** and **stress testing** answer "what if?" for specific extreme events without requiring probability estimates.

## Expected loss vs. unexpected loss

**Expected loss (EL)** is the average loss anticipated over a period — a *predictable cost of doing business* that should be priced into products and covered by reserves/provisions.

For credit:

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$

where $$PD$$ = probability of default, $$LGD$$ = loss given default (1 − recovery rate), $$EAD$$ = exposure at default.

**Unexpected loss (UL)** is the *variability of loss around the expected level* — the genuine risk. It is covered not by pricing but by **capital** (the buffer that absorbs losses worse than expected). Example: a credit card book expects 4% charge-offs (EL, priced into rates); a recession year with 9% charge-offs produces a 5-point unexpected loss that capital must absorb.

{: .warning }
Classic exam trap: expected loss is **not** a risk in the economic sense — it is a cost, anticipated and priced in. Risk (and capital) is about **unexpected** loss. Also note UL rises with the *correlation* between defaults, not just with individual PDs.

## Risk and reward; conflicts of interest

Higher expected return generally requires bearing more risk — but the relationship is observable only *ex ante* in well-functioning markets and gets distorted by:

- **Asymmetric information** — managers/agents know more than principals.
- **Moral hazard** — the protected party takes more risk (e.g., traders with bonus upside and no loss clawback; banks with deposit insurance).
- **Adverse selection** — the riskiest customers are most eager to buy at a given price (insurance, lending).
- **Conflicts of interest** — e.g., rating agencies paid by issuers; originators selling loans they will not hold (originate-to-distribute, Ch 4 and Ch 10).

## The risk taxonomy

| Risk class | Definition | Typical sources / examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Market risk** | Loss from moves in market prices/rates | Equity, interest rate, FX, commodity price risk; includes basis risk |
| **Credit risk** | Loss from counterparty failure to perform | Default risk, downgrade/migration risk, counterparty risk, settlement (Herstatt) risk, sovereign risk, concentration risk |
| **Liquidity risk** | Two distinct types | **Funding liquidity**: cannot roll/raise cash to meet obligations. **Market/trading liquidity**: cannot exit a position without moving the price |
| **Operational risk** | Loss from failed people, processes, systems, or external events | Fraud, rogue trading, cyber attacks, IT failure, legal risk, model risk (often classed here) |
| **Business/strategic risk** | Loss from poor business decisions or demand/competitive shifts | Wrong product strategy, volume/margin shocks; strategic = larger, longer-horizon bets (M&A, new markets) |
| **Reputation risk** | Loss of franchise value from damaged trust | Often *consequential* — triggered by another risk event (fraud, mis-selling) |

{: .tip }
Know the two liquidity risks cold — GARP loves asking which one a scenario illustrates. A bank that can't roll commercial paper has **funding** liquidity risk; a fund forced to dump an illiquid bond at a fire-sale price faces **market liquidity** risk. Crises feature both, feeding each other.

## Risk interactions and aggregation

Risks **interact** rather than add up:

- Market losses can trigger margin calls → funding liquidity risk → fire sales → more market losses (the loss spiral of 2008).
- Credit deterioration of a counterparty raises **counterparty credit risk** on derivatives that were "market risk" trades.
- Operational failures (a mis-booked hedge) create market exposure.

Aggregation challenges: correlations are unstable and **rise toward 1 in crises**; different risk types are measured on different horizons and confidence levels; netting and diversification benefits assumed in calm markets can vanish exactly when needed.

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** A bank's credit card portfolio has historically lost 3% of balances per year, and the bank prices this into its lending rate. In a severe recession losses reach 8%. The 3% is best described as:
A. Unexpected loss  B. Expected loss  C. Economic capital  D. Stress loss

**Q2.** Which scenario best illustrates *funding* liquidity risk?
A. A hedge fund must sell a large block of small-cap stock at a steep discount
B. A bank cannot roll over its maturing commercial paper
C. A dealer widens bid-ask spreads in volatile markets
D. A pension fund's bonds fall in value as rates rise

**Q3.** Risk management is best described as:
A. Minimizing the firm's total risk
B. Eliminating exposures that could produce losses
C. Selecting and sizing the risks the firm is paid to bear, within appetite
D. Insuring all material exposures

**Q4.** A trader is paid a bonus on profits with no penalty for losses. The resulting incentive problem is best described as:
A. Adverse selection  B. Moral hazard  C. Basis risk  D. Funding liquidity risk

**Q1 — B.** Anticipated, average, priced-in losses are expected loss; the extra 5% in the recession is unexpected loss, absorbed by capital.

**Q2 — B.** Failure to roll funding is funding liquidity risk; A and C describe market/trading liquidity; D is interest rate (market) risk.

**Q3 — C.** Risk management is deliberate risk selection within appetite, not minimization or elimination.

**Q4 — B.** Asymmetric payoff after the relationship is in place induces excess risk taking — moral hazard.
