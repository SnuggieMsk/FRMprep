# 5. Modern Portfolio Theory and the Capital Asset Pricing Model

The quantitative core of Book 1: Markowitz diversification, the efficient frontier, CAPM assumptions and pricing, beta, and the CML vs. SML distinction. Expect calculations — portfolio variance, beta, CAPM expected return — plus conceptual CML/SML questions.

## Learning objectives

- Explain modern portfolio theory (MPT) and interpret the Markowitz efficient frontier.
- Understand the derivation and components of the CAPM and describe its underlying assumptions.
- Interpret and compare the capital market line (CML) and the security market line (SML).
- Apply the CAPM to calculate the expected return on an asset.
- Interpret beta and calculate the beta of a single asset or of a portfolio.
- (Performance measures built on the CAPM — Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen, IR, Sortino — are covered in depth in Chapter 7.)

## Modern portfolio theory

Markowitz (1952): investors care only about the **mean** and **variance** of portfolio returns, and diversification reduces risk because assets are imperfectly correlated.

For a portfolio of weights $$w_i$$:

$$E[R_p] = \sum_i w_i E[R_i]$$

Two-asset variance:

$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1 w_2 \rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$$

with covariance $$\mathrm{Cov}(R_1,R_2) = \rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$$.

Key implications:

- Unless $$\rho = +1$$, portfolio standard deviation is **less than** the weighted average of individual standard deviations — the diversification benefit.
- As the number of assets grows, **idiosyncratic (specific) risk diversifies away**; what remains is **systematic risk**, driven by average covariance.
- The **efficient frontier** is the set of portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk (the upper edge of the feasible set, from the global minimum-variance portfolio upward). Rational mean–variance investors hold only frontier portfolios.

Adding a **risk-free asset** turns the frontier into a straight line: combinations of the risk-free asset and the tangency portfolio dominate everything else. With homogeneous expectations, the tangency portfolio is the **market portfolio** — this is the two-fund **separation theorem**: every investor holds the same risky portfolio (the market) and adjusts risk via the risk-free weight.

## CAPM assumptions

1. Investors are rational mean–variance optimizers over a single common period.
2. No transaction costs or taxes; assets are infinitely divisible.
3. Unlimited borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate.
4. **Homogeneous expectations** — all investors see the same means, variances, covariances.
5. Investors are price takers; information is freely available to all.

Unrealistic — but the model's value is the testable equilibrium it produces.

## The CAPM and beta

In equilibrium, only **systematic** risk is rewarded (idiosyncratic risk is diversifiable, so it commands no premium). The measure of systematic risk is beta:

$$\beta_i = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\sigma_m^2} = \rho_{im}\,\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}$$

and the pricing relation is

$$E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i\left(E[R_m] - R_f\right)$$

- $$\beta = 1$$: moves with the market; $$\beta > 1$$: amplified; $$0 < \beta < 1$$: damped; $$\beta < 0$$: hedge asset, expected return *below* the risk-free rate.
- The market portfolio has $$\beta = 1$$; the risk-free asset $$\beta = 0$$.
- **Portfolio beta is the weighted average of component betas**: $$\beta_p = \sum_i w_i\beta_i$$.

## CML vs. SML

| | Capital market line (CML) | Security market line (SML) |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | $$E[R_p] = R_f + \dfrac{E[R_m]-R_f}{\sigma_m}\,\sigma_p$$ | $$E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i\left(E[R_m]-R_f\right)$$ |
| Risk axis | Total risk $$\sigma_p$$ | Systematic risk $$\beta_i$$ |
| Applies to | Only **efficient** portfolios (mixes of $$R_f$$ and the market) | **All** assets and portfolios, efficient or not |
| Slope | Market Sharpe ratio $$\frac{E[R_m]-R_f}{\sigma_m}$$ | Market risk premium $$E[R_m]-R_f$$ |

Assets plotting **above** the SML are underpriced (expected return exceeds the CAPM-required return → positive alpha); below the SML, overpriced.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: a single stock with large diversifiable risk lies **on the SML** (if fairly priced) but **below the CML** — only efficient portfolios sit on the CML. If a question gives you total volatility $$\sigma$$ for an individual stock and asks for CAPM expected return, you need beta, not $$\sigma$$; using $$\sigma$$ in the SML (or $$\beta$$ in the CML) is the classic wrong answer.

### Worked example 1 — two-asset portfolio risk and return

An investor puts 60% in equity fund A ($$E[R]=10\%$$, $$\sigma=20\%$$) and 40% in bond fund B ($$E[R]=5\%$$, $$\sigma=8\%$$), with correlation $$\rho = 0.25$$.

Expected return:

$$E[R_p] = 0.6(10\%) + 0.4(5\%) = 8\%$$

Variance:

$$\sigma_p^2 = (0.6)^2(0.20)^2 + (0.4)^2(0.08)^2 + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.25)(0.20)(0.08)$$

$$\sigma_p^2 = 0.0144 + 0.001024 + 0.00192 = 0.017344$$

$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.017344} \approx 13.17\%$$

Note 13.17% < the weighted-average volatility $$0.6(20\%)+0.4(8\%) = 15.2\%$$ — the diversification benefit from $$\rho < 1$$.

### Worked example 2 — beta and CAPM expected return

Stock X has $$\sigma_X = 30\%$$ and correlation 0.5 with the market; the market has $$\sigma_m = 15\%$$ and expected return 9%; the risk-free rate is 3%.

$$\beta_X = \rho_{Xm}\frac{\sigma_X}{\sigma_m} = 0.5 \times \frac{0.30}{0.15} = 1.0$$

$$E[R_X] = 3\% + 1.0\,(9\% - 3\%) = 9\%$$

Despite being twice as volatile as the market, X requires only the market return — half its variance is diversifiable and earns no premium. If X is forecast to return 11%, its alpha is $$11\% - 9\% = +2\%$$: it plots above the SML and is attractive.

### Worked example 3 — portfolio beta

A portfolio holds 50% in a stock with $$\beta=1.4$$, 30% in a stock with $$\beta=0.8$$, and 20% in T-bills ($$\beta=0$$). With $$R_f = 3\%$$ and market premium 6%:

$$\beta_p = 0.5(1.4) + 0.3(0.8) + 0.2(0) = 0.94$$

$$E[R_p] = 3\% + 0.94 \times 6\% = 8.64\%$$

## Limitations of the CAPM

- The true market portfolio (all risky assets, incl. human capital, real estate) is unobservable — Roll's critique: the CAPM is not properly testable.
- Empirically, the SML is too flat: low-beta stocks earn more, high-beta stocks less, than predicted; size and value effects (Ch 6) violate the single-factor view.
- Single period, homogeneous expectations, frictionless borrowing — all counterfactual.

These gaps motivate APT and multifactor models (Chapter 6).

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** Two assets each have $$\sigma = 25\%$$. An equally weighted portfolio of the two has $$\sigma = 25\%$$ only if their correlation is:
A. 0  B. −1  C. +1  D. 0.5

**Q2.** A fairly priced individual stock with substantial idiosyncratic risk will plot:
A. On the CML and on the SML  B. On the SML but below the CML  C. Above the CML  D. Below the SML

**Q3.** Stock Y: $$\mathrm{Cov}(R_Y,R_m) = 0.036$$, $$\sigma_m = 0.15$$. $$R_f = 2\%$$, $$E[R_m] = 8\%$$. CAPM expected return of Y?
A. 8.0%  B. 9.6%  C. 11.6%  D. 4.4%

**Q4.** Which is NOT a CAPM assumption?
A. Homogeneous expectations
B. Unlimited risk-free borrowing and lending
C. Investors hold heterogeneous, multi-period horizons
D. No taxes or transaction costs
