# 10. Anatomy of the Great Financial Crisis of 2007–2009

The full mechanism of the GFC: the housing/subprime build-up, securitization chains, the institutions involved, the short-term funding runs that turned a credit problem into a systemic collapse, and the policy response. Tested via cause-and-effect and "who did what" questions.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the historical background and provide an overview of the 2007–2009 financial crisis.
- Describe the build-up to the crisis and the factors that played an important role.
- Explain the role of subprime mortgages and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs).
- Compare the roles of banks, financial intermediaries, mortgage brokers and lenders, and rating agencies.
- Describe trends in short-term wholesale funding markets that contributed to the crisis, including their impact on systemic risk.
- Describe the responses taken by central banks and governments.

## The build-up

Ingredients accumulating from the early 2000s:

- **Cheap money**: post-2001 Fed funds at 1%, plus a global "savings glut" pushing investors toward yield.
- **A housing boom**: national US house prices rising for years → the universal assumption that they could not fall nationwide.
- **Underwriting collapse**: subprime and Alt-A mortgages, teaser-rate ARMs (2/28s), low/no-doc "liar loans," NINJA loans, piggyback seconds — viable only if prices kept rising (refinance before reset).
- **Securitization at scale**: originate-to-distribute (Ch 4) severed the link between underwriting and loss-bearing.
- **Leverage everywhere**: broker-dealers at 30–40x; off-balance-sheet conduits/SIVs hiding more; households levering into housing.
- **Regulatory gaps**: shadow banking outside bank supervision; OTC derivatives unregulated; capital rules favoring AAA tranches.

## Subprime mortgages and CDOs: the transmission machine

1. Brokers/lenders originate subprime mortgages for **fees**, passing risk on immediately.
2. Loans pooled into **RMBS**; tranching converts mostly-junk collateral into ~80% AAA-rated bonds via subordination.
3. The hard-to-sell **mezzanine (BBB) tranches** are re-pooled into **ABS CDOs**, which the agencies again rated largely AAA — a re-securitization alchemy resting on low default-correlation assumptions.
4. **Synthetic CDOs** (CDS on the same tranches) multiplied the exposure beyond the stock of actual mortgages; AIG and the monolines sold the protection.
5. When house prices fell nationally (from 2006), defaults exploded, correlation assumptions failed, AAA CDO tranches were downgraded en masse, and every holder — banks, SIVs, money funds, AIG — took simultaneous hits with no market prices to value the paper.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: tranching does not destroy risk — it **redistributes** it. The AAA rating of a CDO tranche depended on diversification/low-correlation assumptions across the pool; a *systematic* shock (national house-price decline) hit all collateral at once, so "AAA" senior tranches suffered losses thought to be virtually impossible. Re-securitization (mezz ABS → CDO) concentrated, rather than reduced, this systematic exposure.

## The cast of institutions

| Player | Role in the crisis | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage brokers/lenders | Originated for fees under OTD | No skin in the game → fraudulent/weak underwriting (e.g., New Century, Countrywide) |
| Commercial banks | Sponsored conduits/SIVs, warehoused pipelines, held "super-senior" | Off-balance-sheet exposure came back on; capital too thin (Citi, UBS) |
| Investment banks (broker-dealers) | Structured and held CDOs; 30x+ leverage on overnight repo | Bear Stearns (rescued via JPMorgan, Mar 2008), Lehman (failed, Sep 2008), Merrill (sold) |
| Rating agencies | Rated tranches AAA using flawed correlation models | **Issuer-pays conflict of interest**, ratings shopping, model error; mass downgrades destroyed confidence |
| GSEs (Fannie/Freddie) | Guaranteed/held mortgage risk with implicit state backing | Conservatorship, Sep 2008 |
| AIG / monolines | Sold CDS protection on CDO tranches | One-way bet; downgrade-triggered collateral calls → $182bn AIG rescue |
| Money market funds | Held ABCP and Lehman paper | Reserve Primary "broke the buck" → run on money funds |
| Investors (pensions, SIVs, foreign banks) | Reached for AAA yield | Outsourced credit analysis to ratings |

## Short-term wholesale funding: the systemic accelerant

The pre-crisis trend: banks and shadow banks increasingly funded long-dated, illiquid assets with **overnight and short-term wholesale money** — asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP), repo, interbank borrowing — rather than insured deposits.

- **ABCP**: conduits/SIVs rolled paper continuously against mortgage assets; in Aug 2007 (BNP Paribas fund suspensions) investors refused to roll → assets returned to sponsor banks.
- **Repo**: dealers financed inventories overnight; as collateral values fell, **haircuts spiked** (a "run on repo"), forcing deleveraging fire sales — the margin/loss spiral.
- **Interbank markets**: counterparty fear (who holds the toxic paper?) blew out LIBOR–OIS spreads; lending froze.

Why this is *systemic*: short-term funding makes lenders' positions runnable like deposits but without insurance; runs force asset sales that depress prices for everyone, transmitting one firm's trouble to all (fire-sale externalities). Funding liquidity and market liquidity collapse together.

## Timeline anchors

- **2006**: US house prices peak.
- **Feb–Apr 2007**: subprime lenders fail; HSBC subprime losses.
- **Jun–Aug 2007**: Bear Stearns hedge funds collapse; BNP freezes funds; ABCP run begins; central banks inject liquidity.
- **Sep 2007**: Northern Rock run (UK).
- **Mar 2008**: Bear Stearns rescued/sold to JPMorgan with Fed support.
- **Sep 2008**: Fannie/Freddie conservatorship; **Lehman bankruptcy (Sep 15)**; AIG rescue; Reserve Primary breaks the buck; money-fund and CP markets seize.
- **Oct 2008**: TARP ($700bn); bank recapitalizations; coordinated rate cuts.
- **2009**: stress tests (SCAP) restore confidence; recovery begins.

## Policy responses

**Central banks:**
- Slashed policy rates (Fed funds to ~0–0.25% by Dec 2008); coordinated international cuts.
- **Lender of last resort, broadened**: term auction facilities (TAF), primary dealer facilities (PDCF), securities lending (TSLF), CP funding facility (CPFF), money-fund backstops, FX swap lines with foreign central banks.
- **Quantitative easing**: large-scale purchases of Treasuries and agency MBS.

**Governments:**
- Capital injections (TARP; UK bank recapitalizations), expanded deposit insurance, debt guarantees.
- Resolution/nationalization of failing institutions; auto-sector support.
- Fiscal stimulus (2009 ARRA).

**Aftermath/reform** (bridges to Ch 3–4): Basel III capital and liquidity standards (LCR/NSFR), Dodd-Frank, Volcker rule, central clearing and margin for OTC derivatives, securitization risk retention, stress-testing regimes, systemically-important-institution surcharges.

{: .note }
Causation chain to remember: cheap money + housing bubble → bad underwriting via OTD → securitization/re-securitization spreads and *hides* the risk → ratings legitimize it → leverage and short-term wholesale funding amplify it → house prices fall → losses + opacity → runs on ABCP/repo/money funds → fire sales and counterparty panic → Lehman → systemic collapse → state backstop.
