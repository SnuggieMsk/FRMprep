# 9. Learning from Financial Disasters

Case studies organized by the risk factor that killed (or maimed) each firm. Near-guaranteed exam questions: match the case to the risk type and the lesson. Pure memory — learn the table cold.

## Learning objectives

- Analyze the key factors that led to, and derive the lessons learned from, case studies involving: interest rate risk (the savings & loan crisis); funding liquidity risk (Lehman Brothers, Continental Illinois, Northern Rock); implementing hedging strategies (Metallgesellschaft); model risk (Niederhoffer, LTCM, the London Whale); rogue trading and misleading reporting (Barings); financial engineering and complex derivatives (Bankers Trust, Orange County, Sachsen Landesbank); reputational risk (Volkswagen); corporate governance (Enron); and cyber risk (the SWIFT attacks).

## Master table

| Case | Year(s) | Primary risk type | One-line story | Core lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Savings & loan crisis** | 1980s | Interest rate risk | Thrifts funded long fixed-rate mortgages with short deposits; rates spiked → negative spread; deregulated gambling for resurrection | Manage the asset-liability repricing gap; deposit insurance + weak supervision breeds moral hazard |
| **Continental Illinois** | 1984 | Funding liquidity / credit | Aggressive loan growth (incl. purchased Penn Square energy loans) funded with wholesale money; credit losses triggered a wholesale run | Wholesale funding flees fast; credit growth without underwriting discipline; "too big to fail" born |
| **Northern Rock** | 2007 | Funding liquidity | UK mortgage lender ~75% funded in short-term wholesale/securitization markets; markets froze → first UK bank run in 140 years | Liquidity risk can kill a *solvent* bank; diversify funding; LCR/NSFR rationale |
| **Lehman Brothers** | 2008 | Funding liquidity / leverage | 30x+ leverage, huge illiquid real-estate exposure, overnight repo funding; confidence loss → no rollover → bankruptcy | Leverage + illiquid assets + short funding is fatal; counterparty webs transmit failure systemically |
| **Metallgesellschaft (MGRM)** | 1993 | Hedging implementation / funding liquidity | Long-dated fixed-price oil supply contracts hedged with stack-and-roll short-dated futures; prices fell → massive margin calls + rollover losses; firm unwound at the bottom | An economically sound hedge can fail through funding needs, maturity mismatch, and basis/rollover risk; size hedges to liquidity capacity |
| **Niederhoffer** | 1997 | Model risk | Sold deep out-of-the-money S&P puts on the "model" that markets don't crash >5% in a day; Oct 1997 Asian-crisis drop wiped the fund | Short-tail strategies look like alpha until the tail arrives; don't bet the firm on a distributional assumption |
| **LTCM** | 1998 | Model risk / leverage / liquidity | Convergence trades at ~25x leverage; models assumed normal-ish moves and stable correlations; Russia default → flight to quality, correlations went to 1, margin spiral; Fed-coordinated private bailout | Stress test beyond history; liquidity and crowding matter; correlations rise in crises; leverage magnifies model error |
| **London Whale (JPMorgan CIO)** | 2012 | Model risk / operational | Synthetic credit portfolio ballooned; VaR model changed (spreadsheet errors) to halve reported risk; limits breached and ignored; ~$6bn loss; positions mismarked | Model governance and independent validation; limit breaches must escalate; a "hedge" that grows for P&L is a prop trade |
| **Barings** | 1995 | Rogue trading / operational | Leeson controlled both trading and back office in Singapore; hid losses in account 88888 while doubling up on Nikkei futures/options; Kobe quake finished it; 233-year-old bank sold for £1 | Segregate front and back office; investigate "too good" profits; unsupervised stars are a control failure |
| **Sumitomo** | 1996 | Rogue trading / operational | Hamanaka manipulated the copper market and concealed unauthorized positions for a decade; ~$2.6bn loss | Long-tenured, unsupervised traders with control over confirmations = concealed risk |
| **Société Générale (Kerviel)** | 2008 | Rogue trading / operational | Junior trader built ~€50bn in hidden index futures positions using back-office knowledge and fake offsetting trades; €4.9bn loss on unwind | Enforce vacations/rotations, monitor cancelled/fake trades and gross (not just net) positions |
| **Bankers Trust** | 1994 | Financial engineering / conduct / reputation | Sold highly leveraged swaps to P&G and Gibson Greetings; staff caught on tape boasting about exploiting clients; lawsuits and franchise destruction | Complex products sold over client comprehension = legal + reputational risk; suitability matters |
| **Orange County** | 1994 | Financial engineering / market (leverage) | Treasurer Citron levered a "safe" municipal fund ~3x via reverse repos into inverse floaters/structured notes; 1994 rate hikes → ~$1.7bn loss, county bankruptcy | Understand embedded leverage and rate sensitivity; oversight of "star" managers; reported yield ≠ low risk |
| **Sachsen Landesbank** | 2007 | Financial engineering / liquidity | Off-balance-sheet conduits funded long-dated ABS with short-term ABCP; ABCP froze → forced rescue | Off-balance-sheet vehicles carry contingent liquidity risk that comes home in stress |
| **Volkswagen** | 2015 | Reputational | "Defeat device" software cheated emissions tests; fines, recalls, criminal charges, ~30%+ equity drop | Reputational risk is consequential to conduct failures; compliance shortcuts can cost decades of brand value |
| **Enron** | 2001 | Corporate governance / accounting fraud | Mark-to-market abuse and SPE-hidden debt fabricated earnings; board and auditor (Arthur Andersen) failed; bankruptcy | Independent boards and auditors; transparency of off-balance-sheet entities; Sarbanes-Oxley followed |
| **SWIFT / Bangladesh Bank** | 2016 | Cyber risk | Hackers used stolen SWIFT credentials to order $951m of transfers from the NY Fed; $81m got through via Philippine casinos | Cyber controls, payment authentication, and reconciliation are core operational risk; people/processes, not just firewalls |

## Recurring failure patterns

Across the cases, the same ingredients recur:

1. **Leverage + illiquidity + short-term funding** (LTCM, Lehman, Northern Rock, Orange County, Sachsen) — losses become death only when funding runs.
2. **Concentration in one bet** (S&Ls, Niederhoffer, the Whale) — size turns ordinary moves into extinction events.
3. **Control failures around individuals** (Barings, Sumitomo, Kerviel) — no segregation of duties, no independent confirmation, profits too good to question.
4. **Model worship** (LTCM, Niederhoffer, the Whale) — history-calibrated models treated as truth; correlations and liquidity assumed stable.
5. **Governance and culture rot** (Enron, VW, Bankers Trust) — incentives and tone from the top overwhelm formal controls.
6. **Hedges that aren't** (Metallgesellschaft, the Whale) — maturity mismatch, basis risk, or mission creep turn hedges into positions.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: classify Metallgesellschaft correctly. The hedge was arguably sound *economically*; the firm died of **funding liquidity risk from margin calls and rollover losses on the stack-and-roll futures hedge** — not from speculation and not (primarily) from basis risk alone. Similarly, Northern Rock and Lehman are **funding liquidity** cases, not credit-loss cases; LTCM and the London Whale are first and foremost **model risk** cases in GARP's taxonomy.

{: .tip }
Memory hook by risk factor: **Rates** = S&L. **Funding runs** = Continental Illinois, Northern Rock, Lehman. **Hedge gone wrong** = Metallgesellschaft. **Models** = Niederhoffer, LTCM, Whale. **Rogues** = Barings (also Sumitomo, Kerviel). **Engineering/derivative sales** = Bankers Trust, Orange County, Sachsen. **Reputation** = VW. **Governance** = Enron. **Cyber** = SWIFT.

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** Metallgesellschaft's losses are best attributed to:
A. Speculative directional bets on rising oil prices
B. Funding/rollover strains from hedging long-dated commitments with short-dated futures
C. Counterparty default on its supply contracts
D. A rogue trader concealing positions

**Q2.** Which pairing is correct?
A. Barings — model risk
B. Northern Rock — funding liquidity risk
C. Enron — cyber risk
D. LTCM — rogue trading

**Q3.** The common thread between Barings and Société Générale (Kerviel) is:
A. Excessive reliance on VaR models
B. Off-balance-sheet conduit funding
C. Weak segregation of duties enabling concealed unauthorized positions
D. Mis-sold derivatives to corporate clients

**Q4.** LTCM's 1998 collapse demonstrated most directly that:
A. Convergence trades are always unprofitable
B. Crisis correlations and market liquidity can deviate sharply from model assumptions, and leverage magnifies the damage
C. Regulators will always bail out hedge funds with public money
D. Interest rate risk is the dominant risk for hedge funds

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**Q1 — B.** Stack-and-roll margin calls plus rollover losses in a falling (contango-shifting) market — funding liquidity, not speculation.

**Q2 — B.** Northern Rock is the canonical wholesale-funding run; Barings = rogue trading, Enron = governance, LTCM = model risk/leverage.

**Q3 — C.** Both traders exploited back-office knowledge/control gaps to hide positions.

**Q4 — B.** Flight to quality broke correlation and liquidity assumptions; 25x leverage turned that into near-failure (and the bailout was private, Fed-*coordinated*).
