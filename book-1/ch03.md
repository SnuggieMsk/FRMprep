# 3. The Governance of Risk Management

Who is responsible for what in a firm's risk architecture — board, committees, CRO, business lines, audit. Post-crisis governance reform is a recurring exam theme; questions test roles, reporting lines, and best practices.

## Learning objectives

- Explain changes in regulation and corporate risk governance that resulted from the 2007–2009 financial crisis.
- Compare best practices in corporate governance and risk management.
- Assess the role and responsibilities of the board of directors in risk governance.
- Evaluate the relationship between a firm's risk appetite and its business strategy, including the role of incentives.
- Distinguish the mechanisms for transmitting risk governance throughout an organization.
- Illustrate the interdependence of functional units within a firm as it relates to risk management.
- Assess the role and responsibilities of a firm's audit committee.

## Post-crisis changes in regulation and governance

The 2007–09 crisis exposed boards that did not understand their firms' risks, risk functions without stature, and pay schemes that rewarded short-term risk taking. Responses:

- **Legislation/regulation**: Dodd-Frank (US) — including the Volcker rule restricting proprietary trading; stronger EU directives; **Basel III** — more and better capital, leverage ratio, liquidity standards (LCR, NSFR); mandatory **stress testing** (e.g., CCAR) tying capital plans to scenarios.
- **Board-level**: dedicated **board risk committees** with independent, financially literate members; direct board engagement with the risk appetite statement.
- **CRO elevation**: independent Chief Risk Officer with direct access/reporting to the board or its risk committee, not buried under the business or the CFO.
- **Compensation reform**: deferral, clawback/malus provisions, risk-adjusted performance metrics, and board compensation committees reviewing incentive risk.

## Corporate governance vs. risk governance best practices

**Corporate governance** is the system by which a firm is directed and controlled in the interests of stakeholders. **Risk governance** is the subset dealing with how risk taking is directed, constrained, and overseen. Best practices:

- A majority-independent, engaged, knowledgeable board; separation (or strong counterbalance) of CEO and chair roles.
- A board that approves **strategy and risk appetite together** — strategy is a risk decision.
- Clear, documented risk appetite cascaded into limits.
- An independent, adequately resourced risk function led by a CRO with stature and a board reporting line.
- Compensation aligned with *risk-adjusted*, multi-period performance.
- Robust management information: timely, accurate, aggregated risk reporting to the board.

{: .note }
The board's job is **oversight, not management**: set appetite, approve strategy and key policies, ensure capable management and sound incentive design, and monitor — it does not run the risk function day to day.

## Board of directors: risk responsibilities

- Approve the **risk appetite statement** and ensure it is consistent with strategy, capital, and liquidity plans.
- Oversee top management; ensure a credible CRO and risk organization exist and can act independently.
- Understand the firm's principal risks and the limits framework; challenge management (the "constructive dialogue" role).
- Oversee compensation design so incentives don't pay for risk-blind returns.
- Ensure integrity of financial statements and regulatory compliance (largely via the audit committee).

## Risk appetite, business strategy, and incentives

Risk appetite and strategy must be set **jointly**: a growth strategy implies risk consequences (new markets, new products, more leverage), and an appetite constraint shapes which strategies are feasible. Disconnects — a conservative stated appetite with aggressive growth targets — get resolved on the trading floor in favor of growth unless governance intervenes.

**Incentives are the transmission belt.** If bonuses pay on revenue or short-term P&L with no risk adjustment, employees will move the firm's risk profile beyond appetite regardless of what the policy documents say. Fixes: risk-adjusted performance measures (e.g., RAROC, Ch 7), deferred compensation, clawbacks, and risk-function input into pay decisions.

## Transmitting risk governance through the organization

Mechanisms that carry board-level appetite down to the desk:

1. **Risk appetite statement → limit framework** — cascading limits by business, desk, risk type.
2. **Committees** — board risk committee; management-level risk committees (ALCO for balance-sheet/interest-rate/liquidity risk, credit committee, operational risk committee).
3. **Policies and delegated authorities** — who can approve what size of exposure.
4. **The three lines of defense:**

| Line | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Business lines / risk owners | Own and manage the risk they generate; first responsibility for staying within limits |
| 2nd | Risk management & compliance | Set the framework, measure independently, monitor, challenge, report |
| 3rd | Internal audit | Independently assure that lines 1 and 2 work as designed; reports to the audit committee |

{: .warning }
Exam trap: the **business line (first line) owns its risks** — risk management (second line) measures, monitors, and challenges but does not "own" the risk; internal audit (third line) audits the framework and must stay independent of both. Questions love to assign first-line duties to the CRO or make audit a designer of controls — both wrong.

## Functional interdependence

Risk management cannot work as an island:

- **Front office (trading/lending)** originates risk and provides position data; needs risk's limits and pricing input.
- **Risk management** depends on operations and IT for clean trade capture and data; on finance for valuations and P&L.
- **Operations/back office** confirms and settles trades — the independent check that positions are real (its absence enabled Barings, Ch 9).
- **Finance/treasury** manages funding and capital; valuation control reconciles trader marks.
- **Compliance and legal** translate regulation into binding constraints.

A risk number is only as good as the trade capture, valuation, and data plumbing beneath it.

## The audit committee

- A **board** committee of independent (and financially literate) directors.
- Oversees the **integrity of financial reporting** and the external audit relationship.
- Oversees **internal audit** — approves its plan, receives its findings, protects its independence.
- Monitors the effectiveness of internal controls and compliance with law and regulation.
- Verifies not just that statements follow accounting rules, but that processes behind reported numbers are sound.

{: .tip }
Distinguish the committees: the **risk committee** owns risk appetite oversight and forward-looking risk; the **audit committee** owns financial-reporting integrity, controls assurance, and internal/external audit. Some duties overlap, but exam answers hinge on the primary owner.

## Quick quiz

**Q1.** Which is a board responsibility rather than a management responsibility?
A. Setting desk-level VaR limits
B. Approving the firm-wide risk appetite statement
C. Designing the daily risk report
D. Approving individual large loans

**Q2.** In the three-lines-of-defense model, independent measurement and challenge of business-line risk taking is performed by:
A. The business line  B. The risk management function  C. Internal audit  D. The external auditor

**Q3.** Which post-crisis change is correctly paired with its purpose?
A. Volcker rule — increase banks' proprietary trading capacity
B. Clawbacks — align pay with long-run, risk-adjusted outcomes
C. Basel III leverage ratio — replace risk-based capital entirely
D. Board risk committees — take over day-to-day risk management

**Q4.** The audit committee's primary responsibility is:
A. Setting the firm's risk appetite
B. Managing the internal audit function's day-to-day testing
C. Overseeing the integrity of financial reporting and the control environment
D. Approving new trading products

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**Q1 — B.** Appetite is approved at board level; A, C, D are management/operational tasks.

**Q2 — B.** Second line = risk/compliance: framework, independent measurement, challenge. Audit (third line) assures the system itself.

**Q3 — B.** Clawback/deferral aligns incentives with risk-adjusted, multi-period results; the other pairings are inverted or false.

**Q4 — C.** Financial reporting integrity, controls, and oversight of internal/external audit; it oversees internal audit but does not run it.
