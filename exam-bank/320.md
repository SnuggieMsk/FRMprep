**Q1.** A $100 million interest rate swap has 1.25 years remaining. An institution **receives 7% fixed** (semiannual) and pays 6-month floating. Continuously compounded zero rates are 9% (3 months), 9.5% (9 months), and 10% (15 months). The floating payment due in 3 months was set at 8% (semiannual) at the last reset. The value of the swap to this institution is closest to:
- A. +$3.67M
- B. +$1.93M
- C. −$1.93M
- D. −$3.67M

**Answer: D**

Restate: value the **receiver** swap as $$B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}$$ using the given zeros.

1. **Discount factors:** $$e^{-0.09(0.25)} = e^{-0.0225} = 0.977751$$; $$e^{-0.095(0.75)} = e^{-0.07125} = 0.931229$$; $$e^{-0.10(1.25)} = e^{-0.125} = 0.882497.$$
2. **Fixed bond** (coupon $$= 100 \times 0.07/2 = 3.5$$M at 0.25, 0.75, 1.25; notional 100 at 1.25):
$$B_{\text{fixed}} = 3.5(0.977751) + 3.5(0.931229) + 103.5(0.882497)$$
$$= 3.42213 + 3.25930 + 91.33844 = \$98.020\text{M}.$$
3. **Floating bond** (notional + known next payment, discounted to the next reset): next payment $$= 100 \times 0.08/2 = 4.0$$M:
$$B_{\text{float}} = 104.0 \times e^{-0.09(0.25)} = 104.0 \times 0.977751 = \$101.686\text{M}.$$
4. **Receiver value:**
$$V = B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}} = 98.020 - 101.686 = -\$3.67\text{M}.$$

Why D is correct: the receiver is *out of the money* because market rates (9–10%) far exceed the 7% fixed rate it receives, so $$B_{\text{fixed}} < B_{\text{float}}$$ and the value is −$3.67M.

- A (+$3.67M) flips the sign — that is the **payer's** value ($$B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$).
- C (−$1.93M) discounts the floating leg as if worth par now (uses 100 instead of 104, or discounts to maturity), shrinking the loss.
- B (+$1.93M) combines the par-FRN error with the wrong sign.

📌 Exam tip: Receiver value $$= B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}$$. With market rates above the fixed rate received, the receiver loses. Discount the floating leg as (notional + known next coupon) to the **next reset** only — it is par only *at* the reset, not now.

**Q2.** A counterparty **pays fixed** in a $100M swap. Using current zero rates, $$B_{\text{fixed}} = \$103.5$$M and $$B_{\text{float}} = \$100.8$$M. The swap's value to this counterparty is:
- A. +$2.7M
- B. −$2.7M
- C. +$3.5M
- D. −$0.8M

**Answer: B**

Restate: value a **payer** swap given the two bond legs.

1. **Payer value formula:** $$V = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}.$$
2. **Plug in:** $$V = 100.8 - 103.5 = -\$2.7\text{M}.$$

Why B is correct: the payer is short the fixed bond and long the floating; with $$B_{\text{fixed}} > B_{\text{float}}$$, the payer's value is negative (rates have fallen, so paying a high fixed rate hurts).

- A (+$2.7M) is the **receiver's** value — the classic sign-flip trap.
- C (+$3.5M) reports $$B_{\text{fixed}}$$ minus notional or similar — not the swap value.
- D (−$0.8M) subtracts notional incorrectly.

📌 Exam tip: Payer value $$= B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$; receiver value is the negative of that. Always confirm whose perspective the question takes before signing.

**Q3.** Firm X can borrow fixed at 5.0% or floating at SOFR + 0.3%. Firm Y can borrow fixed at 6.8% or floating at SOFR + 0.9%. The **total gain** available from a swap between them is:
- A. 1.8%
- B. 1.2%
- C. 0.6%
- D. 0.9%

**Answer: B**

Restate: total swap gain = difference in fixed spreads minus difference in floating spreads.

1. **Fixed-rate difference (Y − X):** $$6.8\% - 5.0\% = 1.8\%.$$
2. **Floating-rate difference (Y − X):** $$(\text{SOFR}+0.9\%) - (\text{SOFR}+0.3\%) = 0.6\%.$$
3. **Total gain:** $$1.8\% - 0.6\% = 1.2\%.$$

Why B is correct: the total gain is the absolute difference between the fixed spread (1.8%) and the floating spread (0.6%).

- A (1.8%) is just the fixed-rate difference — forgets to net the floating difference.
- C (0.6%) is just the floating-rate difference.
- D (0.9%) misreads Y's floating spread as the gain.

📌 Exam tip: Total comparative-advantage gain = |fixed-spread difference − floating-spread difference|. Identify each firm's comparative advantage (X in fixed, since 1.8% > 0.6%), then this gain is split among the parties.

**Q4.** A fixed-for-fixed currency swap: an institution **receives 4% on €80M and pays 6% on $100M**, with annual interest exchanges, 2 years remaining, and principals exchanged at maturity. Flat continuously compounded rates are 3% (euro) and 5% (dollar). The spot rate is $1.20 per €. The swap's value to the institution is closest to:
- A. +$3.87M
- B. −$3.87M
- C. −$20.16M
- D. +$1.62M

**Answer: B**

Restate: value as two bonds — $$V = S_0 B_F - B_D$$ for the party receiving foreign (euro) and paying dollars.

1. **Euro discount factors (3%):** $$e^{-0.03} = 0.970446$$; $$e^{-0.06} = 0.941765.$$
2. **Euro bond** (coupon $$= 80 \times 0.04 = €3.2$$M yr1; €3.2M + €80M yr2):
$$B_F = 3.2(0.970446) + 83.2(0.941765) = 3.10543 + 78.35485 = €81.460\text{M}.$$
3. **Dollar discount factors (5%):** $$e^{-0.05} = 0.951229$$; $$e^{-0.10} = 0.904837.$$
4. **Dollar bond** (coupon $$= 100 \times 0.06 = \$6$$M yr1; $6M + $100M yr2):
$$B_D = 6(0.951229) + 106(0.904837) = 5.70737 + 95.91272 = \$101.620\text{M}.$$
5. **Convert and net** ($$S_0 = 1.20$$ $/€):
$$V = S_0 B_F - B_D = 1.20 \times 81.460 - 101.620 = 97.752 - 101.620 = -\$3.87\text{M}.$$

Why B is correct: the euro leg converted at spot ($97.75M) is worth less than the dollar leg ($101.62M), so the institution's swap value is −$3.87M.

- A (+$3.87M) flips the sign — that is the value to the counterparty (pay euro, receive dollars).
- C (−$20.16M) **inverts the spot rate** (divides €81.46M by 1.20 = $67.88M, then subtracts $101.62M = −$33.7M-style error / wrong direction conversion) — multiply when the quote is dollars per euro.
- D (+$1.62M) omits one of the principal exchanges or mis-discounts a leg.

📌 Exam tip: Currency swap (receive foreign, pay dollars) $$V = S_0 B_F - B_D$$. Build each bond in its own currency at its own flat rate; with $$S_0$$ quoted as **dollars per euro**, *multiply* the euro bond by $$S_0$$. Then subtract the dollar bond.

**Q5.** Immediately **after** a reset date (the next floating rate has just been set and the current payment made), the floating leg of a plain vanilla interest rate swap is worth:
- A. Zero
- B. The notional principal (par)
- C. The present value of all projected future floating coupons only
- D. The fixed-leg value minus the total swap value

**Answer: B**

Restate: recall the value of the floating-rate note embedded in a swap right after a reset.

Why B is correct: a floating-rate note **reprices to par** immediately after each reset, because the coupon resets to the current market rate. So $$B_{\text{float}} = $$ notional just after reset.

- A (zero) confuses the *swap's* net value with the *floating leg's* value — the leg is worth par, not zero.
- C is wrong because you cannot value the FRN by projecting and discounting estimated floating coupons to maturity — the par-reset property makes that unnecessary and incorrect.
- D is a circular/nonsensical relation.

📌 Exam tip: FRN = par **at** each reset. *Between* resets it equals (notional + the already-fixed next payment) discounted to the next payment date. Never project floating coupons to maturity.

**Q6.** Compared with an interest rate swap of equal notional and maturity, a fixed-for-fixed **currency swap** typically has:
- A. Less credit risk because principals are exchanged and netted.
- B. Greater credit risk, concentrated **late** in the swap's life because of the final principal exchange.
- C. Identical credit risk, since both are valued as bond portfolios.
- D. No credit risk, since currency swaps are always centrally cleared.

**Answer: B**

Restate: compare the credit-risk profile of a currency swap versus an interest rate swap.

Why B is correct: a currency swap **exchanges principals** at maturity, so a large cash flow occurs at the end. Potential exposure therefore **grows toward maturity** and is concentrated late in the swap's life — making currency swaps carry materially **more** credit risk than rate swaps of the same notional (whose exposure peaks mid-life and involves no principal exchange).

- A reverses the conclusion: the principal exchange *increases* exposure, not decreases it.
- C is wrong: although both are valued as bond portfolios, their exposure *profiles* differ sharply (mid-life peak vs. late-life build).
- D is false: not all currency swaps are centrally cleared, and clearing mitigates but does not eliminate credit risk.

📌 Exam tip: Rate-swap exposure peaks **mid-life** (no principal exchange); currency-swap exposure builds toward **maturity** because the final principal swap dominates — so currency swaps are riskier per unit notional.

**Q7.** Which statement about the **comparative advantage** argument for swaps is the most valid criticism?
- A. The argument is invalid because total borrowing costs cannot be reduced by any financial contract.
- B. The fixed-rate spread is a long-term locked-in credit spread, while the floating spread applies only until the next reset; the apparent gain partly compensates one party for bearing rollover/credit-spread risk.
- C. The gain is always captured entirely by the swap dealer, so end users never benefit.
- D. Comparative advantage applies only to currency swaps, never to interest rate swaps.

**Answer: B**

Restate: identify the standard, valid criticism of the comparative-advantage story.

Why B is correct: the fixed-rate spread is **long-term and locked in**, while the floating spread is repriced at each reset. The weaker firm's "cheap" floating rate can be marked up if its credit deteriorates, so part of the apparent gain is **compensation for rollover/credit-spread risk**, not a free arbitrage from market inefficiency.

- A overstates: swaps genuinely *can* lower combined costs through comparative advantage; the criticism is about *why* the spread exists, not that it's impossible.
- C is false: end users typically share the gain; the dealer takes only a small spread.
- D is wrong: the comparative-advantage argument applies to interest rate swaps as well (and the criticism is *stronger* there than for currency swaps).

📌 Exam tip: The comparative-advantage "gain" is partly illusory because the **fixed** spread is locked in long-term while the **floating** spread resets — the weaker borrower is being paid to bear future credit-spread/rollover risk.
