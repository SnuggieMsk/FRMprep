**Q1.** A stock has a GDP-factor beta of 1.5 and an inflation-factor beta of −0.8. The factor risk premiums are 4.5% (GDP) and 2.0% (inflation), and the risk-free rate is 3%. Over the next year, GDP growth comes in 1.2% *above* expectations, inflation prints 0.5% *above* expectations, and there is firm-specific good news worth +0.3%. What is the stock's *realized* return under the APT factor model?

- A. 8.15%
- B. 9.85%
- C. 10.15%
- D. 9.55%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the *realized* return, which combines the expected return with the factor *surprises* and the idiosyncratic term.

1. First the expected return from APT:
$$E[R] = R_f + \beta_{GDP}\lambda_{GDP} + \beta_{INF}\lambda_{INF}$$
$$= 3\% + 1.5(4.5\%) + (-0.8)(2.0\%) = 3\% + 6.75\% - 1.6\% = 8.15\%$$
2. Realized return adds the factor *surprises* (actual − expected) times each beta, plus the firm-specific return:
$$R = E[R] + \beta_{GDP}F_{GDP} + \beta_{INF}F_{INF} + e$$
- GDP surprise = +1.2%: contribution $$1.5 \times 1.2\% = +1.8\%$$
- Inflation surprise = +0.5%: contribution $$-0.8 \times 0.5\% = -0.4\%$$
- Firm-specific: $$+0.3\%$$
3. Sum:
$$R = 8.15\% + 1.8\% - 0.4\% + 0.3\% = 9.85\%$$

That is B.

- A (8.15%) stops at the expected return, ignoring all surprises and the firm-specific news (stopped one step early).
- C (10.15%) flips the sign on the inflation surprise contribution (adds +0.4% instead of −0.4%), since the inflation beta is negative.
- D (9.55%) omits the +0.3% firm-specific term.

📌 Exam tip: realized return = expected return + Σ(beta × *surprise*) + idiosyncratic. Use surprises (actual − expected), not levels, and respect the *sign* of each beta — a negative beta times a positive surprise reduces return.

**Q2.** Fund Q has factor loadings $$\beta_m=1.2$$, $$\beta_{SMB}=-0.4$$, and $$\beta_{HML}=0.5$$. The premiums are market 6%, SMB 2.5%, HML 4.0%; the risk-free rate is 2.5%. Using the Fama–French three-factor model, what is Fund Q's expected return, and what style tilts do the loadings indicate?

- A. 10.7%; the fund is large-cap and value-tilted
- B. 12.7%; the fund is small-cap and value-tilted
- C. 10.7%; the fund is small-cap and growth-tilted
- D. 9.7%; the fund is large-cap and growth-tilted

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the Fama–French expected return and the style read on the loadings.

1. Apply the model term by term:
$$E[R_Q] = R_f + \beta_m(\text{mkt prem}) + \beta_{SMB}E[SMB] + \beta_{HML}E[HML]$$
$$= 2.5\% + 1.2(6\%) + (-0.4)(2.5\%) + 0.5(4.0\%)$$
2. Compute each:
- Market: $$1.2 \times 6\% = 7.2\%$$
- SMB: $$-0.4 \times 2.5\% = -1.0\%$$
- HML: $$0.5 \times 4.0\% = +2.0\%$$
3. Sum:
$$E[R_Q] = 2.5\% + 7.2\% - 1.0\% + 2.0\% = 10.7\%$$
4. Style: a **negative SMB** loading = a *large-cap* tilt; a **positive HML** loading = a *value* tilt.

So 10.7%, large-cap value — option A.

- B (12.7%) takes the absolute value of the SMB loading (adds +1.0% instead of −1.0%) and misreads the negative SMB as small-cap.
- C reads the positive HML as growth (it is value) and the negative SMB as small-cap (it is large-cap).
- D drops or mishandles the HML term (9.7%) and misreads the tilts.

📌 Exam tip: positive SMB = small-cap, negative SMB = large-cap; positive HML = value, negative HML = growth. Carry the *signs* of the loadings into the sum — the negative SMB term must be *subtracted*, not added as an absolute value.

**Q3.** A $200 million portfolio has $$\beta_{GDP}=0.6$$ and $$\beta_{INF}=-0.3$$. The manager wants to neutralize *both* factor exposures using pure factor portfolios (each has beta 1 to its own factor and 0 to the other). What trades achieve a net beta of zero on both factors?

- A. Short $120m of the GDP factor portfolio and short $60m of the inflation factor portfolio
- B. Short $120m of the GDP factor portfolio and go long $60m of the inflation factor portfolio
- C. Long $120m of the GDP factor portfolio and short $60m of the inflation factor portfolio
- D. Short $60m of the GDP factor portfolio and long $120m of the inflation factor portfolio

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the hedge trades to zero out two factor betas, being careful with the sign of the negative inflation beta.

1. GDP exposure is **positive** (+0.6). To offset it you must add **negative** 0.6 beta, i.e., **short** the GDP factor portfolio in dollar size:
$$0.6 \times \$200\text{m} = \$120\text{m short}$$
Net GDP beta: $$0.6 - 0.6 = 0$$.
2. Inflation exposure is **negative** (−0.3). To offset a negative beta you must add **positive** 0.3 beta, i.e., **go long** the inflation factor portfolio:
$$0.3 \times \$200\text{m} = \$60\text{m long}$$
Net inflation beta: $$-0.3 + 0.3 = 0$$.

So: short $120m GDP, long $60m inflation — option B.

- A shorts the inflation factor too, which would make the net inflation beta $$-0.3 - 0.3 = -0.6$$ (more negative, not zero) — it ignored that the exposure was already negative.
- C goes long the GDP factor (doubling the positive GDP beta) — wrong direction.
- D mixes up the dollar sizes ($60m vs. $120m) by swapping the betas between factors.

📌 Exam tip: to hedge, add the *opposite* sign of the existing beta. A *positive* exposure is hedged by *shorting* the factor portfolio; a *negative* exposure is hedged by *going long*. Hedge size = |beta| × portfolio value. The negative-beta trap flips the direction of the trade.

**Q4.** A single-factor model uses industrial production (IP) as its factor. A stock's IP factor beta is 0.9. At the start of the period the market expected IP growth of 2.0%; the announcement reveals actual IP growth of 3.5%. With no other news, what is the contribution of the IP announcement to the stock's return?

- A. +3.15%
- B. +1.35%
- C. +1.80%
- D. +0.00%

**Answer: B**

The question asks how much an IP announcement moves the stock, given that only the *surprise* matters.

1. Compute the factor surprise = actual − expected:
$$F_{IP} = 3.5\% - 2.0\% = 1.5\%$$
2. Multiply by the factor beta:
$$\beta_{IP} \times F_{IP} = 0.9 \times 1.5\% = 1.35\%$$

That is B.

- A (+3.15%) multiplies the beta by the *actual* level (0.9 × 3.5%), ignoring expectations — the level trap.
- C (+1.80%) multiplies the beta by 2.0% (the *expected* level) or otherwise mishandles the surprise.
- D (0.00%) would be correct only if actual equaled expected; here there is a +1.5% surprise.

📌 Exam tip: in factor models, only the *unanticipated* part of an announcement moves returns. Always compute surprise = actual − expected *before* multiplying by beta. A "high-sounding" actual number (3.5%) is irrelevant if it was largely expected.

**Q5.** An analyst prices a stock with a three-factor APT model: factor betas of 1.1, 0.7, and −0.4, with corresponding factor risk premiums of 5.0%, 3.0%, and 2.0%. The risk-free rate is 3.5%. What is the stock's APT expected return?

- A. 10.30%
- B. 11.90%
- C. 9.50%
- D. 6.80%

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the APT expected return summing three priced factors plus the risk-free rate.

1. APT expected return:
$$E[R] = R_f + \beta_1\lambda_1 + \beta_2\lambda_2 + \beta_3\lambda_3$$
2. Compute each term:
- $$\beta_1\lambda_1 = 1.1 \times 5.0\% = 5.5\%$$
- $$\beta_2\lambda_2 = 0.7 \times 3.0\% = 2.1\%$$
- $$\beta_3\lambda_3 = -0.4 \times 2.0\% = -0.8\%$$
3. Sum with $$R_f$$:
$$E[R] = 3.5\% + 5.5\% + 2.1\% - 0.8\% = 10.3\%$$

That is A.

- B (11.90%) takes the third term as +0.8% instead of −0.8% (ignoring the negative beta's sign).
- C (9.50%) drops the third factor entirely (stopped one factor short).
- D (6.80%) omits the risk-free rate (summed only the factor terms: 5.5 + 2.1 − 0.8 = 6.8%).

📌 Exam tip: APT expected return = $$R_f$$ + Σ(beta × *premium*). Two recurring traps: dropping $$R_f$$ (gives the risk-premium-only figure) and mishandling a negative beta term (which *subtracts* from the expected return).

**Q6.** A risk manager defends using a multifactor model for hedging but acknowledges its practical limitations. Which statement most accurately reflects the challenges of multifactor hedging as presented in the curriculum?

- A. Factor betas are estimated without error and are stable over time, so the only challenge is choosing the factors
- B. Betas are estimated with error and drift over time, the factor structure may be misspecified (omitted factors appear as alpha or unhedged risk), and hedging many factors requires many positions with transaction-cost and shorting frictions
- C. Hedging all factor exposures eliminates idiosyncratic risk as well, so no residual risk remains
- D. APT specifies exactly which factors to use, so misspecification is impossible in practice

**Answer: B**

The question asks which statement correctly captures the practical challenges of multifactor hedging.

1. The curriculum lists: betas are estimated with error and **drift over time**; the factor structure may be **misspecified** (omitted factors show up as "alpha" or unhedged risk); hedging many factors needs **many positions**, so transaction costs and shorting constraints bite; and factor-proxy correlations complicate clean isolation. Option B states these accurately.

- A is the opposite of reality — betas are *not* error-free or stable; that instability is the central challenge.
- C is false: hedging factor exposures leaves a "market-neutral" stance with **only idiosyncratic risk plus alpha** remaining; idiosyncratic risk is reduced by holding *many names*, not by factor hedging.
- D is false: a defining feature of APT is that it is **silent on what the factors are** — flexibility and weakness both. Misspecification is very possible.

📌 Exam tip: APT does *not* tell you the factors (its key generality/weakness), betas are noisy and drift, and factor hedging removes factor risk but leaves idiosyncratic risk + alpha (diversify names to cut the residual). Any answer claiming stable/error-free betas or APT-specified factors is a trap.

**Q7.** Which statement correctly compares the APT with the CAPM?

- A. The APT requires homogeneous expectations and that all investors hold the market portfolio, just like the CAPM
- B. The APT relies on a no-arbitrage argument enforced by some investors, allows multiple unspecified factors, and does not require identifying or observing the market portfolio
- C. Both the APT and the CAPM specify the economic factors that drive returns
- D. The APT holds exactly for every individual asset, whereas the CAPM holds only for well-diversified portfolios

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the correct APT-vs-CAPM comparison.

1. The APT rests on **no-arbitrage**: a few arbitrageurs eliminate mispricing in well-diversified portfolios — it does *not* need the full equilibrium machinery.
2. It allows **multiple factors**, which the theory leaves **unspecified**.
3. It does **not** require the market portfolio (sidestepping Roll's critique that the true market portfolio is unobservable).
All three points are in B, so B is correct.

- A is false: APT's whole advantage is dropping homogeneous expectations and the market-portfolio requirement; only the CAPM needs those.
- C is false: the CAPM specifies a single factor (the market), but the APT is *silent* on what its factors are.
- D reverses the precision: the **CAPM** holds exactly for every asset; the **APT** holds for well-diversified portfolios (and near-exactly for most single assets).

📌 Exam tip: APT = no-arbitrage, multiple *unspecified* factors, no market portfolio needed (avoids Roll's critique). CAPM = equilibrium, single market factor, homogeneous expectations, market portfolio required. The "exact for every asset" property belongs to the CAPM; APT is exact for well-diversified portfolios.
