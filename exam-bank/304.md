**Q1.** A speculator with $3,000 is bullish on a stock currently at $30. She can either buy shares outright or buy 3-month European calls with a strike of $33 priced at $1.50 each (she invests the full $3,000 in one strategy). At expiration the stock is at $39. Compared with simply buying shares, by how many percentage points does the call strategy's return exceed the stock strategy's return?

- A. 270 percentage points
- B. 300 percentage points
- C. 30 percentage points
- D. 200 percentage points

**Answer: A**

Restate the ask: compute the percentage return of each strategy at $$S_T = 39$$ and take the difference.

1. **Stock strategy:** $3,000 / $30 = 100 shares. Profit = 100 × ($39 − $30) = $900. Return = $900 / $3,000 = **+30%**.
2. **Call strategy:** $3,000 / $1.50 = 2,000 calls. Each call pays max($39 − $33, 0) = $6. Gross payoff = 2,000 × $6 = $12,000. Profit = $12,000 − $3,000 (premium paid) = $9,000. Return = $9,000 / $3,000 = **+300%**.
3. Difference = 300% − 30% = **270 percentage points**.

Why A is correct: the leveraged calls return 300% vs. 30% on the stock, a 270-point gap.

Distractor traps:
- B (300) reports the call return itself, not the *difference* over the stock return.
- C (30) reports the stock return only.
- D (200) forgets to subtract the premium from the option payoff (using $12,000/$3,000 − 1 incorrectly, or netting differently), understating the option return.

📌 Exam tip: Option leverage magnifies returns. Always subtract the premium paid to get option *profit*, then divide by the amount invested. The "difference in returns" asks for the gap, not either level.

**Q2.** A US exporter will receive €2,000,000 in three months. The 3-month forward rate is 1.08 USD/EUR. Alternatively, the exporter buys a 3-month European put on euros with strike 1.08 costing $0.015 per euro. If the spot rate in three months is 1.02 USD/EUR, the dollar proceeds under the forward hedge and under the put hedge are, respectively:

- A. $2,160,000 and $2,160,000
- B. $2,160,000 and $2,130,000
- C. $2,040,000 and $2,130,000
- D. $2,160,000 and $2,040,000

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute the exporter's dollar receipts at $$S_T = 1.02$$ under (i) selling euros forward at 1.08 and (ii) buying a put struck at 1.08.

1. **Forward:** locks in 1.08 regardless of spot. Proceeds = €2,000,000 × 1.08 = **$2,160,000**.
2. **Put:** the put gives the right to sell euros at 1.08; since spot 1.02 < 1.08, the exporter exercises and effectively sells at 1.08. Gross = €2,000,000 × 1.08 = $2,160,000.
3. Premium = $0.015 × 2,000,000 = $30,000 (paid up front).
4. Net put proceeds = $2,160,000 − $30,000 = **$2,130,000**.

Why B is correct: the forward yields $2,160,000; the put yields the same floor minus the $30,000 premium = $2,130,000.

Distractor traps:
- A forgets the put premium, equating the two hedges.
- C uses the unhedged spot conversion (1.02 → $2,040,000) for the forward, ignoring that the forward locks 1.08.
- D applies the put outcome to the forward and the spot to the put — both reversed.

📌 Exam tip: A forward locks the rate with no premium (but no upside). A bought put sets a *floor* (here 1.08) while keeping upside, costing the premium. When spot falls below the strike, the put's net proceeds = floor × notional − premium.

**Q3.** An investor is **short** a forward contract on 10,000 barrels of oil with a delivery price of $78 per barrel. At maturity the spot price is $86 per barrel. The investor's payoff is:

- A. +$80,000
- B. −$80,000
- C. $0 — the short can decline delivery
- D. +$860,000

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute the short forward payoff at maturity.

1. Short forward payoff per unit = $$K - S_T = 78 - 86 = -\$8$$ per barrel.
2. Total = 10,000 × (−$8) = **−$80,000**.

Why B is correct: the short is obligated to deliver at $78 while the market is at $86, losing $8/barrel × 10,000 = $80,000.

Distractor traps:
- A flips the sign — that would be the *long's* payoff ($$S_T - K = +\$8$$).
- C invents an option-like walk-away; a forward is a binding obligation for both sides.
- D uses the spot price as the payoff per unit (86 × 10,000) instead of the gain/loss.

📌 Exam tip: Short forward payoff = $$K - S_T$$ (profits when prices fall). Both sides of a forward are *obligated* — there is no walk-away, unlike an option.

**Q4.** A portfolio manager writes (sells) a European call with strike $50 for a premium of $4 and, separately, writes a European put with strike $50 for a premium of $3, both on the same stock and maturity (a short straddle). At expiration the stock is at $61. The combined profit on the two short positions is:

- A. +$7
- B. −$4
- C. −$11
- D. +$11

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute the total profit of writing both a call and a put (short straddle) at $$S_T = 61$$.

1. **Short call** (strike 50, premium 4): profit = $$c - \max(S_T - K, 0) = 4 - \max(61-50,0) = 4 - 11 = -\$7$$.
2. **Short put** (strike 50, premium 3): profit = $$p - \max(K - S_T, 0) = 3 - \max(50-61,0) = 3 - 0 = +\$3$$ (put expires worthless).
3. Combined = −$7 + $3 = **−$4**.

Why B is correct: the written call is deep in-the-money (a $11 payout the writer owes, against $4 collected = −$7), while the written put expires worthless (+$3 kept); net −$4.

Distractor traps:
- A (+$7) sums only the premiums collected ($4 + $3) and forgets the call payout owed.
- C (−$11) counts the call's intrinsic payout but ignores both premiums received.
- D (+$11) takes the intrinsic value as a gain to the writer — wrong sign; the writer *pays* it.

📌 Exam tip: For a *written* (short) option, profit = premium received − payoff owed. A short straddle keeps both premiums only if the stock pins the strike; a large move in either direction creates a loss. Sign discipline is everything.

**Q5.** Which statement correctly distinguishes forwards/futures from options regarding obligations, cost to enter, and linearity?

- A. Both forwards and options bind both counterparties; both cost a premium to enter; both are nonlinear.
- B. A forward binds both sides and costs nothing to enter (delivery price set so initial value is zero); an option binds only the writer, costs the holder a premium, and is nonlinear, while forwards are linear.
- C. A forward holder may walk away if prices move adversely; options bind both sides; forwards are nonlinear and options are linear.
- D. Both forwards and options cost nothing to enter, because the delivery/strike price is set so initial value is zero.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: contrast obligation, entry cost, and linearity for forwards vs. options.

1. **Obligation:** a forward (and futures) binds *both* parties; an option binds only the *writer* — the holder has a right, not an obligation.
2. **Cost to enter:** a forward costs nothing (the delivery price $$K$$ is set so initial value = 0); an option *always* costs the holder a **premium**, because the option payoff is never negative for the holder.
3. **Linearity:** forwards/futures/swaps are **linear** (constant payoff slope); options are **nonlinear** (kinked payoff, hence the Greeks).

Why B is correct: it correctly pairs obligation, cost, and linearity for each instrument.

Distractor traps:
- A wrongly makes both bind both sides, both cost a premium, and both nonlinear.
- C invents a forward walk-away and reverses the linearity labels.
- D wrongly claims options cost nothing — the holder always pays a premium.

📌 Exam tip: Forward = both obligated, zero cost to enter, linear. Option = only the writer obligated, holder pays a premium, nonlinear. Every derivative is also zero-sum: the long's gain equals the short's loss.

**Q6.** A stock trades at $204 in New York and £150 in London while the exchange rate is 1.35 USD/GBP. Ignoring transaction costs, an arbitrageur trading 100 shares can lock in a riskless profit by:

- A. Buying in New York and selling in London for a profit of about $300.
- B. Buying in London and selling in New York for a profit of about $150.
- C. Buying in London and selling in New York for a profit of about $1,350.
- D. There is no arbitrage; the prices are equal once converted.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: convert the London price to dollars, find the cheaper market, and compute the per-100-share riskless profit.

1. London price in dollars = £150 × 1.35 = **$202.50**.
2. New York price = **$204.00**. London is cheaper, so **buy in London, sell in New York**.
3. Per-share profit = $204.00 − $202.50 = $1.50.
4. For 100 shares: 100 × $1.50 = **$150**.

Why B is correct: buy cheap (London, $202.50) and sell dear (New York, $204.00) for $1.50/share × 100 = $150.

Distractor traps:
- A reverses the direction (buy high, sell low → a loss) and inflates the figure.
- C uses the *gross* London proceeds (£150 × 1.35 × ... ) or treats $1,350 as profit — that is roughly the full converted price, not the spread.
- D wrongly claims parity; $202.50 ≠ $204.00, leaving a $1.50 gap.

📌 Exam tip: Convert both prices to one currency, buy in the cheaper market and sell in the dearer, and the profit is the *spread* × quantity — not the full price. Arbitrage demand closes the gap, which is why no-arbitrage pricing holds.

**Q7.** The 1995 collapse of Barings Bank and the 2008 Société Générale loss are both cited as derivatives-misuse cases. What was the common structural control failure, and what is the standard mitigant?

- A. Both losses came from a CCP default; the mitigant is to avoid central clearing.
- B. In both, one person controlled both trading and the back-office confirmation/settlement function, defeating segregation of duties; the mitigant is separating front and back office with independent valuation and position/loss limits.
- C. Both arose from clients posting too little collateral; the mitigant is higher initial margin.
- D. Both were caused by linear-derivative pricing errors; the mitigant is to trade only options.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify the shared control failure in Barings/SocGen and the appropriate mitigant.

1. Barings (Nick Leeson) and SocGen (Jérôme Kerviel) both involved a trader who *also* controlled the back-office confirmation/settlement, letting them **conceal directional positions and losses** — a breakdown of **segregation of duties**.
2. Mitigants: **separate the front office (trading) from the back office (settlement/confirmation)**, use **independent valuation**, and impose **position and loss limits** — plus scrutinize traders whose reported profits look too good for their mandate.

Why B is correct: the common thread is one person controlling both trading and its verification; the fix is segregation of duties and independent controls.

Distractor traps:
- A invents a CCP default — these were rogue-trader/internal-control failures, not clearing-house failures.
- C misattributes the cause to client collateral; the failures were internal control breakdowns.
- D fabricates a pricing-error cause and a nonsensical "trade only options" remedy.

📌 Exam tip: The rogue-trader lesson (Barings, SocGen) is *segregation of duties* — never let the trader also confirm/settle their own trades. Controls: front/back-office separation, independent valuation, position/loss limits, and suspicion of profits too good for the mandate.
