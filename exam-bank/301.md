**Q1.** A bank holds a $4 billion corporate loan portfolio. Its risk team estimates a 0.6% expected one-year loss rate and, from the loss distribution, a 99.9th-percentile one-year loss rate of 2.4%. Separately, the Basel regulatory minimum capital required against this portfolio is $90 million. How much total capital (consistent with prudent practice) should the bank hold against this portfolio, and what is its economic capital?

- A. Economic capital $96M; hold $96M
- B. Economic capital $72M; hold $90M
- C. Economic capital $96M; hold $90M
- D. Economic capital $24M; hold $90M

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute economic capital (which covers *unexpected* loss only) and then determine total capital held, given a regulatory minimum.

1. Expected loss (EL): $$EL = 0.6\% \times \$4{,}000M = \$24M$$. EL is covered by loan pricing/provisions, NOT by capital.
2. 99.9th-percentile loss: $$2.4\% \times \$4{,}000M = \$96M$$.
3. Economic capital = unexpected loss = 99.9% loss − EL: $$\$96M - \$24M = \$72M$$.
4. The bank holds the **greater** of economic and regulatory capital: $$\max(\$72M, \$90M) = \$90M$$.

Why B is correct: economic capital is $72M (UL only), but the regulatory floor of $90M binds, so the bank holds $90M.

Distractor traps:
- A uses the full 99.9% loss ($96M) as economic capital — the classic mistake of failing to subtract EL.
- C makes the same EL error AND holds the smaller of the two figures.
- D correctly subtracts but uses... actually $24M is the EL itself mistaken for capital; capital never equals EL.

📌 Exam tip: Capital absorbs only *unexpected* loss — always subtract EL first. When both an economic and a regulatory figure are given, the bank holds the larger.

**Q2.** A regional bank funds a portfolio of high-yield real-estate development loans almost entirely with retail deposits that are fully covered by deposit insurance. Which statement best captures the moral-hazard mechanism at work, and the correct mitigant?

- A. Insured depositors demand higher rates as the bank's risk rises, so the bank is forced to de-risk; no mitigant is needed.
- B. Because insured depositors do not monitor or reprice the bank's risk, the bank can fund risky lending cheaply; risk-based deposit-insurance premiums help mitigate it.
- C. Deposit insurance transfers the bank's credit risk to the FDIC, eliminating the bank's incentive to make loans at all.
- D. Moral hazard here is a pre-contract screening failure, best mitigated by requiring medical-style underwriting of borrowers.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify the moral-hazard channel created by deposit insurance and the appropriate mitigant.

1. Without insurance, depositors in a risky bank would demand higher rates or withdraw — market discipline.
2. With insurance, depositors are made whole regardless, so they have **no incentive to monitor or price** the bank's risk.
3. The bank therefore obtains cheap, risk-insensitive funding and can pursue aggressive lending: "heads shareholders win, tails the insurance fund pays."
4. Mitigants: capital regulation, supervision, and **risk-based insurance premiums** (charging riskier banks more).

Why B is correct: it states both the mechanism (no depositor discipline → cheap risky funding) and a textbook mitigant.

Distractor traps:
- A describes the *no-insurance* world and wrongly concludes no mitigant is needed.
- C confuses deposit insurance (protects depositors) with credit insurance; the bank still bears loan credit risk.
- D mislabels the problem as adverse selection (pre-contract) — moral hazard is the *behavioral* (post-arrangement) risk-taking; underwriting borrowers is irrelevant to depositor behavior.

📌 Exam tip: The S&L crisis is the canonical example. Moral hazard = behavior change *because* you are insured; the fix is to restore risk sensitivity (risk-based premiums, capital rules).

**Q3.** An investment bank agrees on a **firm-commitment** basis to underwrite 80 million shares, buying them from the issuer at $46.50 and offering them to the public at $48.00. Demand is weaker than expected and the bank ultimately places the entire block at an average realized price of $45.75. What is the bank's underwriting profit or loss?

- A. +$120 million
- B. −$60 million
- C. −$180 million
- D. $0 — under firm commitment the issuer absorbs the shortfall

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute the underwriter's P&L on a firm-commitment deal where the resale price falls below the purchase price.

1. Under firm commitment the bank *buys the whole issue* at $46.50 and bears all placement risk.
2. Per-share P&L = realized resale price − purchase price = $45.75 − $46.50 = −$0.75.
3. Total = 80M × (−$0.75) = **−$60 million**.

Why B is correct: the bank loses $0.75 per share on 80M shares.

Distractor traps:
- A uses the *offer* price ($48.00) instead of the realized price: 80M × ($48.00 − $46.50) = +$120M — the profit only if it had sold at the offer price.
- C computes off the offer price as the cost basis: 80M × ($45.75 − $48.00) = −$180M, double-counting the spread.
- D describes *best efforts*, not firm commitment — in firm commitment the *bank*, not the issuer, bears the shortfall.

📌 Exam tip: Firm commitment → bank's cost basis is the price it *paid the issuer* ($46.50), and its risk is on the *realized* resale price. Best efforts → issuer bears the risk; bank earns only a per-share fee.

**Q4.** A bank's treasury reclassifies a block of illiquid securitized credit exposures from its banking book into its trading book in early 2007. Which consequence is most accurate?

- A. The assets move to amortized-cost accounting and attract a higher credit-risk capital charge.
- B. The assets are now marked to market daily and attract market-risk capital rules, which were lower — a regulatory-arbitrage concern that "market" prices were unreliable for illiquid positions.
- C. The reclassification eliminates the credit risk because trading-book assets are guaranteed by a CCP.
- D. The bank must immediately recognize impairment provisions because trading-book assets use historical cost.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify the accounting and capital consequences of moving assets from banking book to trading book.

1. Banking book: held at amortized/historical cost; impairment provisions when loss is likely; subject to **credit-risk** capital rules.
2. Trading book: **marked to market daily**, gains/losses through P&L; subject to **market-risk** capital rules.
3. In 2007–08 market-risk charges on these positions were lower than credit-risk charges, so reclassification reduced required capital — regulatory arbitrage.
4. For illiquid positions, true "market" values were hard to observe, making mark-to-market unreliable. Regulators now restrict reclassification.

Why B is correct: it correctly pairs trading book → mark-to-market → market-risk rules (lower) and flags the arbitrage and unreliable-pricing problems.

Distractor traps:
- A reverses the books — amortized cost and credit-risk charges belong to the *banking* book.
- C invents CCP coverage; trading-book assets are not guaranteed by a clearing house and credit risk is not eliminated.
- D wrongly assigns historical cost and impairment provisions to the trading book (those are banking-book features).

📌 Exam tip: Banking book = accrual/credit-risk; trading book = mark-to-market/market-risk. The 2008 lesson: reclassification can be capital arbitrage, and MTM is meaningless for genuinely illiquid assets.

**Q5.** Under a **Dutch auction IPO**, investors submit the following sealed bids for a total offering of 10 million shares:

| Bidder | Shares | Price |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3M | $52 |
| B | 4M | $50 |
| C | 5M | $48 |
| D | 2M | $46 |

At what clearing price is the offering sold, and what does each successful bidder pay?

- A. $48; each successful bidder pays $48 (the single clearing price)
- B. $50; bidders pay their own bid prices (pay-as-bid)
- C. $46; each successful bidder pays $46
- D. $48; bidders A, B, and C each pay their own bid price

**Answer: A**

Restate the ask: find the Dutch-auction clearing price (highest price at which all shares sell) and the payment rule (single uniform price).

1. Rank bids high to low and accumulate shares until the 10M offering is filled:
   - A at $52: 3M cumulative.
   - B at $50: 3M + 4M = 7M cumulative.
   - C at $48: 7M + 5M = 12M cumulative — the 10-millionth share clears here.
2. The clearing price is the highest price at which *all* 10M shares can be sold = **$48** (the bid that absorbs the last available shares).
3. In a Dutch auction every successful bidder pays the **single clearing price**, $48 — not their individual bids.

Why A is correct: $48 clears the full 10M, and the uniform-price rule means all winners pay $48.

Distractor traps:
- B picks $50 (only 7M shares fill — under-allocates) and applies pay-as-bid, which is not the Dutch-auction rule.
- C uses the lowest bid ($46) rather than the marginal clearing bid.
- D gets the price right but applies pay-as-bid pricing — the defining feature of a Dutch auction is the *single* clearing price for all.

📌 Exam tip: Dutch auction = clearing price is where cumulative demand first meets supply, and *everyone pays that one price*. Google's 2004 IPO used this to reduce underwriter underpricing.

**Q6.** Which of the following is the clearest example of a conflict of interest that **Chinese walls** are specifically designed to prevent?

- A. A commercial banking division charging different loan rates to different borrowers based on credit quality.
- B. An equity research analyst being pressured to publish a "buy" rating on a company the bank's investment-banking division is currently underwriting.
- C. A bank holding the same bond in both its banking book and its trading book.
- D. A bank choosing a firm-commitment rather than a best-efforts underwriting.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify a genuine cross-divisional conflict of interest addressed by information barriers (Chinese walls).

1. Chinese walls prevent improper *information flow and influence* between divisions whose interests conflict.
2. Research vs. underwriting is the textbook conflict: analysts may be pressured to favor companies the bank underwrites, to win or retain mandates — biasing research investors rely on.
3. The wall separates research from investment banking so underwriting interests do not corrupt published opinions.

Why B is correct: it is a direct research-vs-underwriting conflict, precisely what walls target.

Distractor traps:
- A is normal risk-based pricing, not a conflict of interest.
- C is a book-classification/capital issue, not an information-barrier matter.
- D is a structuring choice about who bears placement risk — no client-information conflict.

📌 Exam tip: Chinese walls = information barriers between divisions with conflicting incentives (research/underwriting, lending/securities, banking/trading). Look for *misuse of confidential information or cross-division pressure*, not ordinary business decisions.

**Q7.** Two banks face the same $5 billion loan portfolio with EL = 1.0% and a 99.9% loss of 3.5%. Bank A targets an AA rating (default tolerance 0.1%); Bank B is content with a lower rating and uses an 99.0% loss figure of 2.2% for its internal target. All else equal, which statement about their **economic capital** is correct?

- A. Both hold $175M because EL is irrelevant to economic capital.
- B. Bank A's economic capital is $125M; Bank B's is $60M — the higher rating target requires more capital.
- C. Bank A's economic capital is $175M; Bank B's is $110M.
- D. Economic capital is identical for both because it is set by regulators, not by rating targets.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute each bank's economic capital, recognizing that a more demanding (higher) confidence target requires more capital, and that EL is always subtracted.

1. EL = 1.0% × $5,000M = $50M (covered by pricing, not capital).
2. Bank A (99.9% target): loss = 3.5% × $5,000M = $175M. Economic capital = $175M − $50M = **$125M**.
3. Bank B (99.0% target): loss = 2.2% × $5,000M = $110M. Economic capital = $110M − $50M = **$60M**.
4. The higher (more conservative) confidence level demands more capital.

Why B is correct: $125M for A, $60M for B, with EL subtracted in each case.

Distractor traps:
- A forgets to subtract EL ($175M) and wrongly says both targets give the same number.
- C subtracts EL for B ($110M is actually the *gross* loss, not economic capital) but fails to subtract it for A — inconsistent treatment.
- D confuses *economic* capital (the bank's own choice, driven by its rating target) with *regulatory* capital (the supervisory minimum).

📌 Exam tip: Economic capital = (loss at the chosen confidence level) − EL, and the bank picks the confidence level to match its desired rating. A higher target percentile → larger economic capital.
