**Q1.** Metallgesellschaft's US subsidiary (MGRM) sold long-dated, fixed-price oil-supply contracts to customers and hedged them with a "stack-and-roll" of short-dated long futures. When spot oil prices fell sharply in 1993, the firm suffered enormous cash drains and unwound at the bottom. In GARP's taxonomy, the *primary* risk that destroyed MGRM is best classified as:
- A. Speculative directional risk — the firm was effectively betting oil would rise
- B. Funding liquidity risk — margin calls and rollover losses on the short-dated futures hedge created cash demands the firm could not meet
- C. Credit risk — its supply-contract counterparties defaulted
- D. Market risk on the customer contracts, which lost value as oil fell

**Answer: B**

The ask: classify the *primary* cause of MGRM's losses per GARP's framework.

1. The customer contracts and the futures hedge were economically offsetting in the long run — the position was, arguably, a sound hedge of a long-dated commitment.
2. The futures, however, were marked to market *daily*: as oil fell, the long futures generated immediate margin calls (cash *out*), while the offsetting gains on the long-dated supply contracts were only realized *over years* (cash in much later). This maturity/cash-flow mismatch, plus rollover (basis) losses when the curve moved against the stack, produced a funding crisis.
3. The firm could not fund the margin calls and was forced to liquidate at the worst possible time.

Why B is correct: the death blow was the *funding* strain from margin and rollover on the hedge, not directional speculation.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A mislabels a hedge as a bet — the classic MGRM trap. The economic hedge was defensible; the *implementation* (cash-flow timing) failed.
- C invents counterparty default; there was no supply-contract default driving the collapse.
- D points at the contracts losing value, but those were the *hedged* item whose loss was offset by the futures — the issue was timing of cash flows, i.e., funding liquidity.

📌 Exam tip: MGRM is the canonical "an economically sound hedge can still kill you through funding" case — maturity mismatch + daily margin + rollover/basis risk. Do not classify it as speculation or as pure basis risk; GARP's answer is *funding liquidity risk from the hedge*.

**Q2.** An examiner asks candidates to match three disasters to their *primary* GARP risk classification: (i) Northern Rock (2007), (ii) the London Whale / JPMorgan CIO (2012), (iii) Barings (1995). The correct mapping is:
- A. (i) credit risk; (ii) rogue trading; (iii) model risk
- B. (i) funding liquidity risk; (ii) model risk; (iii) rogue trading
- C. (i) model risk; (ii) funding liquidity risk; (iii) operational fraud only
- D. (i) reputational risk; (ii) credit risk; (iii) rogue trading

**Answer: B**

The ask: assign each case its primary risk type.

1. **Northern Rock** funded roughly three-quarters of its book in short-term wholesale/securitization markets; when those froze it suffered the first UK bank run in 140 years despite being solvent — a *funding liquidity* case.
2. **London Whale**: a synthetic credit book ballooned while a changed/erroneous VaR model halved reported risk and limit breaches were ignored; GARP classifies it first as *model risk* (with operational/governance overlay).
3. **Barings**: Leeson controlled both trading and back office and hid losses while doubling up on Nikkei futures — the archetypal *rogue trading* / segregation-of-duties failure.

Why B is correct: it matches all three to their GARP-primary classifications.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A mislabels Northern Rock as credit (its losses were a *funding run*, not primarily credit losses) and the Whale as rogue trading (it was model/risk-governance, not a hidden-by-one-rogue scheme).
- C flips Northern Rock to model risk and the Whale to funding liquidity — both reversed — and reduces Barings to "fraud only," ignoring the segregation-of-duties control failure GARP emphasizes.
- D miscasts Northern Rock as reputational and the Whale as credit risk.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the "primary risk" labels GARP uses, even when a case has several flavors. Funding runs = Continental Illinois, Northern Rock, Lehman. Model risk = Niederhoffer, LTCM, London Whale. Rogue trading = Barings, Sumitomo, Kerviel.

**Q3.** LTCM (1998) and Niederhoffer (1997) are both classified primarily as *model-risk* failures. Which statement best identifies what their models got wrong and how the failure propagated?
- A. Both relied on assuming stable, history-calibrated distributions/correlations; when a tail event arrived (Russia default; the 1997 Asian-crisis drop), losses spiked and — for LTCM — high leverage plus a margin/liquidity spiral magnified a model error into near-failure
- B. Both were rogue-trading frauds in which a single trader hid positions
- C. Both failed because of interest rate risk on long bond portfolios
- D. Both were brought down by counterparty default on their derivative contracts

**Answer: A**

The ask: identify the shared model-risk mechanism and its propagation.

1. **Niederhoffer** sold deep out-of-the-money S&P puts on the model assumption that markets do not drop more than ~5% in a day. The October 1997 Asian-crisis fall violated that assumption and wiped the fund — a short-tail strategy that looked like alpha until the tail arrived.
2. **LTCM** ran convergence trades at ~25x leverage assuming roughly normal moves and stable correlations. Russia's 1998 default triggered a flight to quality; correlations went toward 1, liquidity evaporated, and margin calls forced losses — leverage turned a model error into a systemic threat (Fed-coordinated *private* rescue).
3. Common thread: history-calibrated distributional/correlation assumptions failed in crisis; for LTCM, leverage and liquidity spirals amplified the damage.

Why A is correct: it names both the distributional/correlation assumption failure and the leverage/liquidity amplification.

Why the distractors trap you:
- B confuses them with rogue-trading cases (Barings/Sumitomo/Kerviel) — neither involved a single hidden trader.
- C reduces them to interest rate risk, which was not the core failure.
- D invents counterparty default as the cause; the failures were driven by their own positions and assumptions, not a counterparty's collapse.

📌 Exam tip: "Model worship" = treating history-calibrated models as truth. The lesson: stress *beyond* history, watch correlations rising toward 1 in crises, and remember that leverage magnifies model error (LTCM) while short-tail option selling hides risk until the tail (Niederhoffer).

**Q4.** A control reviewer studies Barings (Leeson), Société Générale (Kerviel), and Sumitomo (Hamanaka). Which single control weakness is the common thread, and which remedy most directly addresses it?
- A. Excessive VaR reliance; remedy: scenario analysis
- B. Lack of segregation of duties / independent confirmation, letting traders conceal unauthorized positions; remedy: separate front and back office, enforce mandatory vacations and rotation, and monitor cancelled/fake trades and gross positions
- C. Off-balance-sheet conduit funding; remedy: consolidate SIVs
- D. Mis-selling complex derivatives to clients; remedy: suitability standards

**Answer: B**

The ask: find the shared control failure across three rogue-trader cases and the matching remedy.

1. Leeson controlled both trading and the back office in Singapore (no segregation); Kerviel used back-office knowledge and fake offsetting trades to hide ~€50bn of index futures; Hamanaka, long-tenured and unsupervised, controlled confirmations and concealed copper positions for a decade.
2. The common gap: no segregation of duties and no independent confirmation/monitoring, which let each trader hide unauthorized positions.
3. Remedies map directly: split front/back office, force vacations and rotation (Kerviel never took leave), and monitor cancelled/fake trades and *gross* (not just net) positions.

Why B is correct: it states the shared weakness and the specific operational controls that address it.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A describes the LTCM/Whale "model" theme, not the rogue-trading control gap.
- C describes Sachsen Landesbank (off-balance-sheet ABCP), a different case family.
- D describes Bankers Trust (client mis-selling), not the segregation/concealment problem.

📌 Exam tip: Rogue-trading cases share segregation-of-duties failure and concealed positions. The named remedies — front/back-office separation, mandatory vacations and rotation, monitoring of cancelled trades and gross exposures, and investigating "too good to be true" profits — are favorite exam answers.

**Q5.** Which pairing correctly matches a disaster to its *primary* risk type as GARP classifies it?
- A. Orange County (1994) — reputational risk
- B. Bankers Trust (1994) — financial-engineering/conduct risk (mis-selling complex leveraged swaps to corporate clients)
- C. Volkswagen (2015) — funding liquidity risk
- D. Enron (2001) — cyber risk

**Answer: B**

The ask: pick the correct case-to-risk-type pairing.

1. **Bankers Trust** sold highly leveraged swaps to P&G and Gibson Greetings; staff were taped boasting about exploiting clients, triggering lawsuits and franchise destruction — a *financial-engineering / conduct / reputation* case centered on selling products beyond client comprehension (suitability).

Why B is correct: it correctly classifies Bankers Trust as a conduct/financial-engineering mis-selling case.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A miscasts Orange County as reputational; it is a *financial-engineering / market (leverage)* case — Treasurer Citron levered a "safe" fund ~3x via reverse repos into inverse floaters/structured notes, and 1994 rate hikes caused ~$1.7bn of losses and county bankruptcy.
- C miscasts VW; the diesel "defeat device" is the textbook *reputational* (conduct-driven) case, not funding liquidity.
- D miscasts Enron; it is a *corporate governance / accounting-fraud* case (mark-to-market abuse, SPE-hidden debt, failed board and auditor), not cyber risk. (Cyber = SWIFT/Bangladesh Bank.)

📌 Exam tip: Use the memory hook — Engineering/derivative sales = Bankers Trust, Orange County, Sachsen; Reputation = VW; Governance = Enron; Cyber = SWIFT. Examiners swap these labels to trap you.

**Q6.** The 2016 SWIFT / Bangladesh Bank attack saw hackers use stolen SWIFT credentials to instruct the New York Fed to transfer about $951 million; roughly $81 million escaped through Philippine casinos before the rest was halted. Which lesson does GARP draw, and how does it relate to operational risk?
- A. Cyber risk is purely a technology problem solved by stronger firewalls alone
- B. Cyber risk is a core component of operational risk; payment authentication, access controls, and transaction reconciliation (people and processes, not just firewalls) are essential defenses
- C. The attack was a market-risk event because exchange rates moved
- D. The episode shows that wholesale funding runs are the dominant cyber threat

**Answer: B**

The ask: state the SWIFT-attack lesson and tie it to operational risk.

1. The breach exploited stolen *credentials* and weak *controls/reconciliation*, not merely a perimeter failure. Spelling errors in some instructions and reconciliation eventually flagged transfers, but $81m had already leaked.
2. Cyber risk is a subset of *operational risk* (people, processes, systems, external events). Defenses are about authentication, access management, segregation, and reconciliation — process and people controls — not firewalls alone.

Why B is correct: it correctly locates cyber within operational risk and names process/people controls.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A is the "firewalls fix everything" fallacy; the attack used legitimate credentials, defeating perimeter-only thinking.
- C mislabels it as market risk because of incidental FX movement — the loss was theft via compromised payments, an operational event.
- D conflates two unrelated themes; this was cyber/operational, not a funding run.

📌 Exam tip: Cyber risk = a major, growing *operational* risk. The lesson is layered controls — authentication, access, segregation, reconciliation — and that "people and processes" matter as much as technology.

**Q7.** A risk officer summarizes recurring "ingredients" across the disaster case studies. Which scenario best illustrates the pattern *"leverage + illiquidity + short-term funding turns losses into death,"* and which other case shares that exact pattern?
- A. Enron, sharing the pattern with Volkswagen
- B. Lehman Brothers, sharing the pattern with LTCM (and Northern Rock, Orange County, Sachsen)
- C. Bankers Trust, sharing the pattern with Sumitomo
- D. Barings, sharing the pattern with the London Whale

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the case best fitting the leverage + illiquidity + short-term funding pattern and a peer case sharing it.

1. **Lehman** ran 30x+ leverage on largely illiquid real-estate exposure financed with overnight repo; when confidence broke, lenders refused to roll, and the firm failed — the textbook leverage/illiquidity/short-funding death spiral.
2. **LTCM** matches precisely: ~25x leverage, positions that became illiquid in the crisis, and margin/funding pressure that forced losses. Northern Rock, Orange County, and Sachsen also share the funding-amplification ingredient.

Why B is correct: Lehman is the cleanest example, and LTCM (plus Northern Rock/Orange County/Sachsen) shares the same lethal combination.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A pairs governance/accounting (Enron) with reputational/conduct (VW) — neither is primarily a leverage-plus-short-funding death.
- C pairs two conduct/rogue cases (Bankers Trust mis-selling; Sumitomo rogue trading) — wrong pattern.
- D pairs two control/model cases (Barings rogue trading; the Whale model risk) — also the wrong recurring ingredient.

📌 Exam tip: GARP's recurring patterns are testable in themselves. "Losses become death only when funding runs" links Lehman, LTCM, Northern Rock, Orange County, and Sachsen — high leverage + illiquid assets + short-term/wholesale funding.
