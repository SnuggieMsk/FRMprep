**Q1.** A jet-fuel buyer will purchase 1,500,000 gallons in two months and hedges with heating-oil futures (42,000 gallons per contract). From historical monthly price changes: standard deviation of jet-fuel spot changes σ_S = 0.032, standard deviation of heating-oil futures changes σ_F = 0.040, and correlation ρ = 0.80. Using the minimum-variance hedge ratio (quantity basis), how many contracts should be traded, and in which direction?
- A. Long 23 contracts
- B. Long 36 contracts
- C. Short 23 contracts
- D. Long 29 contracts

**Answer: A**

The ask: compute h*, then N* on a quantity basis, and choose the direction for a future *purchase*.

1. Minimum-variance hedge ratio (spot vol on top):
$$h^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F} = 0.80 \times \frac{0.032}{0.040} = 0.80 \times 0.80 = 0.64$$

2. Optimal number of contracts:
$$N^* = \frac{h^* Q_A}{Q_F} = \frac{0.64 \times 1{,}500{,}000}{42{,}000} = \frac{960{,}000}{42{,}000} = 22.86 \approx 23$$

3. The buyer needs fuel later and fears *rising* prices, so it takes a **long** hedge. Answer: **long 23 contracts**.

- **B** (36) uses the *inverted* ratio h* = ρ·σ_F/σ_S = 0.80 × (0.040/0.032) = 0.80 × 1.25 = 1.0 → N = 1.0 × 1.5M/42,000 = 35.7 ≈ 36. Putting futures vol on top is the classic trap.
- **C** (short 23) gets the magnitude right but the wrong direction — a buyer hedges *long*, not short.
- **D** (29) drops ρ entirely (uses σ_S/σ_F = 0.80, N = 0.80 × 1.5M/42,000 = 28.6 ≈ 29) — omitting correlation overstates the hedge.

📌 Exam tip: h* = ρσ_S/σ_F (spot SD on top). A future *buyer* goes long; a future *seller/holder* goes short. Round N* to the nearest whole contract.

**Q2.** A pension fund holds a $60,000,000 equity portfolio with beta 1.10 relative to the S&P 500. Index futures are quoted at 4,800 with a $50 multiplier. The manager wants to *raise* the portfolio's beta to 1.50 ahead of an expected rally. How many futures contracts are required, and in which direction?
- A. Long 100 contracts
- B. Short 100 contracts
- C. Long 275 contracts
- D. Long 138 contracts

**Answer: A**

The ask: change beta from 1.10 to 1.50 using index futures.

1. Futures contract value: F = 4,800 × $50 = $240,000.

2. Number of contracts to change beta:
$$N = (\beta^* - \beta)\frac{P}{F} = (1.50 - 1.10)\times \frac{60{,}000{,}000}{240{,}000} = 0.40 \times 250 = 100$$

3. N is positive → **buy (long) 100 contracts** (raising beta means going long the market).

- **B** (short 100) has the right magnitude but wrong direction — shorting *lowers* beta; raising beta requires longs.
- **C** (275) applies the *target* beta to the whole position as if fully establishing 1.50 exposure (1.50 × 250 = 375, or similar) instead of the *change* (β* − β).
- **D** (138) uses the change in beta but with the *current* beta 1.10 baked in incorrectly (e.g., 0.40 × 1.10 × ... ) — only (β* − β)·P/F is correct, no extra beta factor.

📌 Exam tip: N = (β* − β)·P/F. Positive ⇒ long (raise beta); negative ⇒ short (lower beta). Use F = quote × multiplier, not the bare quote.

**Q3.** A $90,000,000 portfolio has beta 1.25. The manager wants to fully hedge market risk for three months using index futures quoted at 3,600 with a $250 multiplier. After a perfect full hedge, what return should the hedged portfolio approximately earn over the period (ignoring alpha and basis risk), and how many contracts are shorted?
- A. The market return; short 125 contracts
- B. The risk-free rate; short 125 contracts
- C. The risk-free rate; short 100 contracts
- D. Zero; short 156 contracts

**Answer: B**

The ask: number of contracts for a full hedge and the expected return of the fully hedged position.

1. Full hedge sets β* = 0:
$$N^* = \beta\frac{P}{F},\quad F = 3{,}600 \times 250 = \$900{,}000$$
$$N^* = 1.25 \times \frac{90{,}000{,}000}{900{,}000} = 1.25 \times 100 = 125 \text{ contracts (short)}$$

2. A fully hedged (β = 0) portfolio has no systematic market exposure, so by CAPM it should earn approximately the **risk-free rate** over the period (plus any alpha, which we ignore). Answer: risk-free rate; short 125 contracts → **B**.

- **A** says "market return" — wrong; a zero-beta hedged portfolio earns the risk-free rate, not the market return.
- **C** has the right return but miscounts contracts (drops the 1.25 beta, giving 100).
- **D** says "zero" return — a common trap; the hedge removes *market risk*, not the time value of money, so the expected return is the risk-free rate, not zero. The 156 count also mis-scales (e.g., 1.25 × 90M/720k).

📌 Exam tip: A fully hedged equity portfolio earns ≈ the risk-free rate (plus alpha), NOT zero and NOT the market return. Full hedge N* = βP/F.

**Q4.** In May, a copper producer expecting to sell 500,000 lb in September shorts September futures at F₁ = $4.10/lb. In September the producer closes the hedge when spot is S₂ = $3.85/lb and the September futures price is F₂ = $3.78/lb. What effective price per pound does the producer realize, and did the basis strengthen or weaken relative to the initial basis assumption of zero?
- A. $3.85; basis strengthened
- B. $4.17; basis strengthened
- C. $4.03; weakened
- D. $4.10; unchanged

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the effective sale price via the basis and judge the basis move for the short hedger.

1. Basis at close: $$b_2 = S_2 - F_2 = 3.85 - 3.78 = +0.07$$.

2. Effective sale price for a short hedger:
$$\text{Effective price} = F_1 + b_2 = 4.10 + 0.07 = \$4.17/\text{lb}$$
Check via spot + futures gain: futures gain = F₁ − F₂ = 4.10 − 3.78 = 0.32; effective = S₂ + gain = 3.85 + 0.32 = $4.17. ✓

3. A positive basis of +0.07 vs. an assumed zero is a **strengthening** basis, which *benefits the short hedger* (sells at an effectively higher price). Answer: **$4.17; basis strengthened** → B.

- **A** ($3.85) reports the raw spot price, forgetting the futures gain.
- **C** ($4.03) subtracts the basis instead of adding (4.10 − 0.07), the sign trap, and mislabels the move.
- **D** ($4.10) assumes the basis converged to zero (only true at expiration with matched grade/location).

📌 Exam tip: Short hedger effective price = F₁ + b₂. A *strengthening* (higher) basis helps the short hedger; a *weakening* basis hurts. Add the closing basis, don't subtract.

**Q5.** A natural-gas marketer hedges a long-dated fixed-price supply contract by stacking the full notional in near-month long futures and rolling them forward each month. Gas prices fall sharply over the next two quarters. Which risk most directly threatens the marketer, as illustrated by Metallgesellschaft (1993)?
- A. The hedge over-protects, generating excess futures gains that must be returned
- B. Funding/liquidity risk: futures losses trigger immediate margin calls while the offsetting gains on the long-term contracts are distant and unrealized
- C. Counterparty risk on the exchange clearing house
- D. The minimum-variance hedge ratio becomes negative, reversing the hedge direction

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the cash-flow timing/liquidity danger in a stack-and-roll hedge when prices move against the futures leg.

1. The marketer is *long* near-month futures (hedging a future obligation to deliver gas at a fixed price). If gas prices *fall*, the long futures lose money daily, triggering immediate variation-margin calls.
2. The offsetting economic gain — being able to source gas cheaply for the long-dated supply contracts — is *unrealized and distant*. The mismatch between immediate cash outflows (margin) and deferred inflows is a funding/liquidity crisis. This is precisely what sank Metallgesellschaft, with roughly $1.3 billion of losses. Answer: **B**.

- **A** misframes the problem: the issue is *losses* on futures, not excess gains being clawed back.
- **C** is essentially nil because the clearing house guarantees performance — not the threat here.
- **D** is a red herring; the hedge ratio does not flip sign because prices fall.

📌 Exam tip: Stack-and-roll adds *rollover basis risk* AND *cash-flow timing risk*. Margin calls on the futures hit now; offsetting gains on the underlying are unrealized — a funding mismatch (the Metallgesellschaft lesson).

**Q6.** Monthly price-change data give σ_S = 0.045, σ_F = 0.050, and ρ = 0.90 between a hedged asset and the futures used to hedge it. What fraction of the variance of the hedged position is eliminated by the minimum-variance hedge, and what is the hedge ratio?
- A. 90% eliminated; h* = 0.90
- B. 81% eliminated; h* = 0.90
- C. 81% eliminated; h* = 0.81
- D. 95% eliminated; h* = 1.00

**Answer: C**

The ask: compute hedge effectiveness (R² = ρ²) and the hedge ratio h* = ρσ_S/σ_F.

1. Hedge ratio:
$$h^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F} = 0.90 \times \frac{0.045}{0.050} = 0.90 \times 0.90 = 0.81$$

2. Hedge effectiveness (proportion of variance eliminated):
$$R^2 = \rho^2 = 0.90^2 = 0.81 \Rightarrow 81\%$$
So 81% of the position's variance is removed; 19% remains as basis risk. Answer: **C** — 81% eliminated, h* = 0.81.

- **A** confuses effectiveness with ρ itself (90%) and uses h* = 0.90 (forgetting the σ_S/σ_F factor).
- **B** gets effectiveness right (81%) but uses h* = ρ = 0.90, dropping the volatility ratio.
- **D** invents 95% and uses h* = 1.00 (ignoring both ρ and the volatility ratio).

📌 Exam tip: Hedge *effectiveness* = R² = ρ² (variance eliminated), not ρ. Hedge *ratio* = ρσ_S/σ_F. Don't confuse the two — ρ = 0.90 gives only 81% variance reduction.

**Q7.** A portfolio manager argues the firm should NOT hedge its commodity input costs with futures. Which of the following is a *valid* argument against hedging in this context?
- A. Hedging always increases expected profit
- B. If competitors do not hedge, industry output prices and input costs tend to move together, naturally stabilizing margins; a lone hedger then makes its *own* margins more volatile
- C. Hedging eliminates basis risk entirely, which regulators prohibit
- D. Shareholders are unable to diversify or hedge on their own account

**Answer: B**

The ask: pick the genuine argument *against* corporate hedging.

1. A recognized argument against hedging: if rivals do not hedge, input and output prices move together across the industry, so unhedged firms enjoy naturally stable margins. A firm that hedges its inputs while competitors don't can find its margins *more* volatile relative to peers. Answer: **B**.

- **A** is false: hedging is about *reducing variability*, not increasing expected profit; it can reduce expected profit by the cost of hedging.
- **C** is false: hedging does not eliminate basis risk (it *replaces* price risk with basis risk), and there is no such regulatory prohibition.
- **D** reverses a key point: shareholders *can* diversify and hedge themselves (often more cheaply), which is itself an argument *against* corporate hedging.

📌 Exam tip: Valid anti-hedging arguments: shareholders can hedge/diversify themselves; competitive (peer) effects can make a lone hedger's margins *more* volatile; and visible futures losses are hard to explain even when the combined position performed as designed.
