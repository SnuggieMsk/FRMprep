**Q1.** During 2002–2006, US originate-to-distribute (OTD) securitization severed the link between mortgage underwriting and loss-bearing. Which sequence correctly orders the transmission machine from origination to the most toxic re-securitized instrument?
- A. Subprime mortgages → ABS CDO → RMBS tranches → synthetic CDO
- B. Subprime mortgages → RMBS (tranched, ~80% AAA) → mezzanine (BBB) tranches re-pooled into ABS CDOs (again largely AAA) → synthetic CDOs (CDS on the same tranches)
- C. Synthetic CDOs → subprime mortgages → RMBS → ABS CDO
- D. Subprime mortgages → AAA Treasuries → RMBS → ABS CDO

**Answer: B**

The ask: order the securitization chain that spread and hid mortgage risk.

1. Brokers/lenders originate subprime mortgages *for fees*, passing risk on immediately.
2. Loans are pooled into **RMBS**; subordination/tranching converts mostly weak collateral into roughly 80% AAA-rated bonds.
3. The hard-to-sell **mezzanine (BBB)** tranches are re-pooled into **ABS CDOs**, which agencies again rated largely AAA — re-securitization "alchemy" resting on low default-correlation assumptions.
4. **Synthetic CDOs** (CDS written on the same tranches) multiplied exposure beyond the actual stock of mortgages; AIG and the monolines sold the protection.

Why B is correct: it reflects the exact origination → RMBS → mezz-into-ABS-CDO → synthetic ordering.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A puts the ABS CDO *before* the RMBS tranches it is built from — backward; you cannot re-pool tranches that do not yet exist.
- C starts with synthetic CDOs, which are *derivatives referencing* tranches that must already exist — impossible as a first step.
- D inserts AAA Treasuries into the chain; Treasuries are not part of the subprime securitization pipeline.

📌 Exam tip: Know the chain cold — mortgages → RMBS (tranched to manufacture AAA) → mezzanine BBB tranches → ABS CDO (re-securitization) → synthetic CDO. Tranching *redistributes* risk; re-securitization *concentrates* systematic (housing) risk while pretending to diversify it.

**Q2.** An exam item states: "AAA-rated senior tranches of ABS CDOs built from mezzanine RMBS were thought almost incapable of loss, yet they suffered severe losses in 2007–2009." Which explanation is the most accurate?
- A. The tranches actually held mostly prime, low-risk mortgages, so the losses were fraud-driven only
- B. The AAA ratings depended on *low cross-pool default correlation*; a national house-price decline was a common systematic shock that hit all underlying pools at once, so diversification assumed in the models did not materialize
- C. The tranches were exchange-traded and over-margined, which forced their prices down mechanically
- D. The FDIC insured the tranches, and its insolvency caused the losses

**Answer: B**

The ask: explain why "safe" senior CDO tranches lost so much.

1. Tranche ratings relied on the assumption that defaults across the many underlying mortgage pools were *weakly correlated* — diversification would protect the senior claim.
2. A nationwide house-price decline is a *systematic* shock: it hits essentially all pools simultaneously, driving correlations toward 1. The assumed diversification vanished, so losses cascaded up into supposedly remote senior tranches.
3. Re-securitization (mezz BBB → ABS CDO) made this worse by concentrating the same systematic housing exposure rather than diversifying it.

Why B is correct: it pinpoints the correlation/systematic-shock failure at the heart of the GFC.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A is false — these CDOs were built largely from *subprime/mezzanine* exposure, and the losses were driven by the systematic shock, not solely fraud.
- C invents an exchange-trading/margin mechanism; these were largely OTC, opaque instruments with *no* reliable market prices, not exchange-margined.
- D fabricates FDIC insurance of CDO tranches — they were never FDIC-insured.

📌 Exam tip: The single most-tested GFC concept: tranching does not destroy risk, it *redistributes* it, and AAA senior CDO tranches rested on *low-correlation* assumptions. A systematic shock (national house-price fall) breaks those assumptions and inflicts "impossible" senior losses.

**Q3.** The "issuer-pays" rating-agency model is widely cited as a contributor to the crisis. Which description best captures the mechanism by which it biased structured-finance ratings?
- A. Issuers paid agencies for ratings and could shop among agencies competing for lucrative structured-finance fees, creating a conflict of interest that biased ratings upward
- B. Investors paid agencies directly, so agencies inflated ratings to please buyers
- C. Regulators set the ratings, and their models were flawed
- D. Agencies were legally barred from rating structured products, so unrated junk circulated

**Answer: A**

The ask: identify the conflict-of-interest mechanism in issuer-pays ratings.

1. Under issuer-pays, the *issuer* of the structured product pays the agency for the rating. Issuers could approach multiple agencies and "shop" for the most favorable grade.
2. Because structured-finance ratings were highly profitable and competition for that fee income was intense, agencies faced strong pressure to assign generous ratings — an upward bias — compounded by flawed correlation models.

Why A is correct: it captures both the payer conflict and ratings-shopping that inflated structured grades.

Why the distractors trap you:
- B reverses who pays — it was *issuers*, not investors, under the criticized model.
- C is false; agencies (not regulators) set ratings, though their models were indeed flawed — the conflict, not regulator-set ratings, is the mechanism.
- D is fabricated; agencies actively rated structured products — that was the problem, not a prohibition.

📌 Exam tip: Issuer-pays + ratings shopping + competition for structured-finance fees = upward ratings bias. Investors outsourced credit analysis to these ratings, so the conflict propagated systemic mispricing.

**Q4.** Which statement best describes the "run on repo" of 2007–2008 and why it was systemically dangerous?
- A. Insured retail depositors withdrew cash from commercial banks, draining deposits
- B. Repo lenders sharply raised haircuts and refused to roll short-term funding against securitized collateral; dealers had to sell assets into falling markets (fire sales), depressing prices for all holders and transmitting one firm's stress to everyone
- C. The Fed stopped accepting Treasury securities as repo collateral
- D. Hedge funds voluntarily reduced repo leverage, calming markets

**Answer: B**

The ask: define the repo run and its systemic mechanism.

1. Dealers financed large inventories of securities *overnight* in the repo market. As the value and trustworthiness of mortgage-related collateral fell, lenders demanded much larger *haircuts* (more collateral per dollar lent) and increasingly refused to roll funding at all.
2. Cut off from funding, dealers were forced to sell assets immediately — *fire sales* — which pushed prices down further, raised haircuts again, and inflicted mark-to-market losses on every other holder. Funding liquidity and market liquidity collapsed together.
3. This is a wholesale-market analogue of a deposit run, but *without* deposit insurance, which is what makes it systemic (fire-sale externalities).

Why B is correct: it captures haircut spikes, refusal to roll, fire sales, and contagion.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A describes a *retail* deposit run on insured deposits — the wrong market; the repo run was wholesale and uninsured.
- C invents a Fed refusal of Treasury collateral; in fact the Fed *broadened* its lending facilities to accept more collateral.
- D is the opposite of reality — leverage and forced selling *amplified* the crisis; there was no calming voluntary deleveraging.

📌 Exam tip: Short-term wholesale funding (ABCP, repo, interbank) made positions *runnable like deposits but uninsured*. A repo run = haircut spiral + refusal to roll → fire sales → contagion. This is the systemic accelerant that turned a credit problem into collapse.

**Q5.** Match each institution to its primary *role/failure mode* in the GFC: (i) AIG, (ii) rating agencies, (iii) commercial banks sponsoring SIVs/conduits.
- A. (i) sold CDS protection on CDO tranches (one-way bet) with downgrade-triggered collateral calls; (ii) issuer-pays conflict and flawed correlation models; (iii) off-balance-sheet exposure returned on-balance-sheet as conduits could not roll ABCP
- B. (i) originated subprime loans for fees; (ii) guaranteed mortgages with state backing; (iii) broke the buck
- C. (i) ran a money market fund; (ii) levered 30x on repo; (iii) entered conservatorship
- D. (i) entered conservatorship; (ii) sold CDS protection; (iii) rated tranches AAA

**Answer: A**

The ask: correctly map three players to their roles.

1. **AIG** (and the monolines) *sold CDS protection* on CDO tranches — a one-way bet on housing. Rating downgrades triggered large collateral calls, leading to the ~$182bn rescue.
2. **Rating agencies** rated tranches AAA using flawed correlation models under an *issuer-pays* conflict (ratings shopping).
3. **Commercial banks** sponsored conduits/SIVs and warehoused pipelines; when ABCP could not be rolled, the off-balance-sheet exposure came *back onto* their balance sheets, and thin capital was overwhelmed (e.g., Citi, UBS).

Why A is correct: each role is matched precisely.

Why the distractors trap you:
- B mislabels AIG as a loan originator, the agencies as a GSE guarantor, and banks as a money fund — all swapped roles.
- C assigns AIG a money-fund role, the agencies a 30x repo-leverage role (that was broker-dealers), and banks the GSE conservatorship — scrambled.
- D gives AIG the GSE conservatorship (that was Fannie/Freddie), the agencies AIG's CDS role, and banks the agencies' rating role — also scrambled.

📌 Exam tip: Keep the cast straight — AIG/monolines = CDS protection sellers (collateral calls on downgrade); agencies = AAA-stamping under issuer-pays; commercial banks = SIV/conduit sponsors whose off-balance-sheet risk came home; broker-dealers = 30x+ repo leverage (Bear, Lehman, Merrill); GSEs = conservatorship; Reserve Primary = broke the buck.

**Q6.** Which event is a *direct* consequence of the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy on September 15, 2008?
- A. US national house prices peaked
- B. The Reserve Primary money market fund "broke the buck" because it held Lehman commercial paper, triggering a run on money market funds and a freeze in commercial paper markets
- C. Northern Rock experienced a retail deposit run
- D. The first Basel III capital rules took immediate legal effect

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify a direct downstream effect of the Lehman failure.

1. The Reserve Primary Fund held Lehman commercial paper. When Lehman defaulted, that paper's value collapsed, pushing the fund's net asset value below $1 — it "broke the buck."
2. Investors fled money market funds (previously treated as cash-equivalents), and the commercial paper market seized, prompting Fed money-fund backstops and the CPFF.

Why B is correct: the money-fund break and CP freeze flowed directly from Lehman's default.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A is a timing trap — US house prices peaked in *2006*, two years *before* Lehman; it cannot be a consequence.
- C is also a timing trap — the Northern Rock retail run occurred in September *2007*, a year earlier.
- D is false; Basel III was a *post-crisis reform* phased in over years, not an immediate effect of Lehman.

📌 Exam tip: Anchor the timeline. 2006: house prices peak. Sep 2007: Northern Rock run. Mar 2008: Bear Stearns rescued. Sep 15, 2008: Lehman fails → Reserve Primary breaks the buck → money-fund run and CP freeze → AIG rescue. Basel III/Dodd-Frank are *aftermath* reforms.

**Q7.** A central bank's crisis response is described to a candidate: "It slashed policy rates to near zero, created new facilities to lend against a broad range of collateral to dealers and money funds, opened FX swap lines with foreign central banks, and purchased large quantities of Treasuries and agency MBS." Which classification of these actions is most accurate?
- A. All four are examples of fiscal policy executed by the Treasury
- B. They are central-bank actions: rate cuts (conventional policy), broadened lender-of-last-resort facilities (TAF/PDCF/TSLF/CPFF, money-fund backstops, FX swap lines), and quantitative easing (large-scale Treasury and agency-MBS purchases)
- C. They are all examples of TARP, a government capital-injection program
- D. They are micro-prudential supervision measures unrelated to liquidity

**Answer: B**

The ask: classify the listed crisis-response tools.

1. Near-zero policy rates = *conventional monetary policy* (coordinated international cuts to ~0–0.25% by December 2008).
2. New lending facilities against broad collateral to dealers and money funds, plus FX swap lines with foreign central banks = a *broadened lender-of-last-resort* role (TAF, PDCF, TSLF, CPFF, money-fund backstops).
3. Large-scale purchases of Treasuries and agency MBS = *quantitative easing*.
All three are *central-bank* (monetary/liquidity) actions.

Why B is correct: it correctly buckets the actions into rate cuts, LOLR facilities, and QE, all central-bank tools.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A calls them fiscal/Treasury actions; fiscal measures were TARP capital injections, deposit-insurance expansion, and 2009 ARRA stimulus — separate from these monetary tools.
- C labels everything TARP, which was a *government* capital-injection program, not central-bank lending or QE.
- D mislabels these macro-liquidity interventions as micro-prudential supervision, which they are not.

📌 Exam tip: Separate the responders. Central banks: rate cuts, broadened lender-of-last-resort facilities (TAF/PDCF/TSLF/CPFF, swap lines), and QE. Governments: TARP capital injections, expanded deposit insurance/guarantees, resolution/nationalization, fiscal stimulus. Don't lump QE and TARP together.
