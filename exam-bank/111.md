**Q1.** A risk consultant in Country Z recommends a derivatives platform to a client. He receives an undisclosed referral fee from the platform's vendor. Local regulation in Country Z does not require disclosure of such referral arrangements, and "everyone in the market does it." Under the GARP Code of Conduct, the consultant's conduct is:
- A. Permissible, because local law does not require disclosure and local practice accepts it
- B. A violation, because the Code requires full disclosure of actual or potential conflicts of interest to all affected parties, and members must follow the stricter of the Code or local law
- C. Permissible, because the referral fee did not change the quality of his recommendation
- D. A violation only if the client later suffers a financial loss on the platform

**Answer: B**

The ask: apply the conflict-of-interest principle and the "stricter standard" rule.

1. The Code's conflicts principle requires members to act fairly and to *fully disclose* any actual or potential conflict of interest to all affected parties. An undisclosed referral fee is a textbook undisclosed conflict.
2. "Local law allows it" is *not* a defense: members must comply with the Code even where local law or practice is laxer, following the stricter standard consistent with law.

Why B is correct: the duty is disclosure, and the laxer local norm does not excuse non-disclosure.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A invokes the "my country's rules allow it" defense the Code explicitly rejects — a flagged exam trap.
- C argues the fee was harmless, but the Code requires disclosure of *potential* conflicts regardless of whether the advice was actually biased; appearance of compromised independence is enough.
- D ties the violation to a later loss, but the breach occurs *at the moment of non-disclosure*, independent of any subsequent outcome.

📌 Exam tip: The Code's remedy for conflicts is *disclosure, not necessarily avoidance* — and disclosure must go to *all affected parties*. Whenever an answer relies on "local rules allow it," treat it as wrong: members follow the stricter of Code or law.

**Q2.** A GARP member and risk manager receives a lawful subpoena (valid legal process) compelling production of a client's confidential portfolio records. Separately, a friend at a hedge fund asks the same member to share a different client's positions, promising it will "stay between us." Under the Code, the member should:
- A. Refuse the subpoena (confidentiality is absolute) but may share with the friend since it stays private
- B. Comply with the subpoena (disclosure required by legal process is permitted) and refuse the friend (using/revealing confidential information for others' advantage violates confidentiality)
- C. Comply with both, since the client relationship gives the member discretion over the data
- D. Refuse both, because confidentiality under the Code admits no exceptions

**Answer: B**

The ask: apply the confidentiality principle and its exceptions to two contrasting requests.

1. Confidentiality is *not absolute*. The Code permits revealing confidential information where the client consents *or* where disclosure is required by law or legal/regulatory process. A valid subpoena is such legal process → complying is permitted (indeed required by law).
2. Sharing a different client's positions with a hedge-fund friend is revealing confidential information for another's inappropriate advantage, with no consent and no legal compulsion → a clear confidentiality violation.

Why B is correct: it distinguishes lawful compelled disclosure (allowed) from voluntary leakage for others' benefit (prohibited).

Why the distractors trap you:
- A treats confidentiality as absolute (so refuses the subpoena) and then permits the leak — both backward.
- C wrongly assumes the member has open discretion over client data; sharing with the friend has neither consent nor legal basis.
- D again asserts no exceptions exist, ignoring the consent and legal-process carve-outs the Code explicitly provides.

📌 Exam tip: Confidentiality has two exceptions — *client consent* and *disclosure required by law/legal process*. Complying with a subpoena is fine; tipping a friend is a violation (and likely illegal). "Confidentiality is absolute" is always a wrong answer.

**Q3.** An FRM Part II candidate who has passed Part I but not yet Part II updates her LinkedIn headline to "FRM (Certified)" and tells prospective employers she "holds the FRM designation." Under the Code, this most directly:
- A. Is acceptable, since she has passed Part I and is enrolled in Part II
- B. Violates professional integrity by misrepresenting her qualifications and the meaning of the FRM designation
- C. Violates only confidentiality, not integrity
- D. Is outside the Code's scope, because candidates are not bound until certified

**Answer: B**

The ask: classify the candidate's misstatement under the correct principle.

1. The professional-integrity principle prohibits members from knowingly misrepresenting facts or making false/misleading statements, *including* about their own qualifications and the meaning of the FRM designation.
2. Claiming to "hold the FRM designation" before completing Part II (and the program's requirements) is a misrepresentation of qualifications.

Why B is correct: this is a textbook integrity/misrepresentation breach (mirroring the chapter's "FRM certified while awaiting Part II results" example).

Why the distractors trap you:
- A treats partial progress as entitlement to the designation; passing Part I does not confer FRM status.
- C points to confidentiality, which is unrelated — no confidential information is involved.
- D claims candidates are exempt, but the Code binds *candidates from registration*, not only certified holders — a specifically flagged trap.

📌 Exam tip: Misstating your credentials or the meaning of the FRM designation is an *integrity* violation. And remember the scope rule: the Code binds members, certification holders, *and registered candidates* from the moment they register.

**Q4.** A risk manager presents the firm's one-day VaR to the board as the firm's "maximum possible loss," omitting that the model excludes the firm's largest illiquid position and assumes normally distributed returns. Which best identifies the Code provision most directly violated?
- A. Conflict-of-interest rules, because the manager benefits from a low VaR
- B. The duty to use generally accepted practices and to communicate risk information clearly, accurately, and not misleadingly — including making model assumptions, limitations, and uncertainty transparent
- C. Confidentiality, because VaR is proprietary
- D. Nothing, because VaR is an industry-standard metric and its limitations are common knowledge

**Answer: B**

The ask: pinpoint the violated standard in a misleading risk-communication scenario.

1. Calling VaR the "maximum possible loss" is itself false — VaR is a quantile, not a maximum. Omitting that the largest illiquid position is excluded and that normality is assumed hides material limitations.
2. The standard on *generally accepted practices* requires communications of risk information to be clear, accurate, and not misleading, and to make assumptions, limitations, and the degree of uncertainty transparent (and to distinguish fact from opinion).

Why B is correct: the breach is misleading communication and failure to disclose model limitations — the heart of the generally-accepted-practices standard.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A reaches for a conflict of interest, but the scenario shows no personal benefit or undisclosed interest — it is a *communication/competence* failure.
- C invokes confidentiality, which is irrelevant; presenting VaR to one's own board reveals nothing improperly.
- D excuses the omission as "common knowledge," but the Code affirmatively requires *transparency* about limitations and forbids overstating accuracy — "everyone knows" is not a defense.

📌 Exam tip: When risk outputs are presented as more certain or complete than they are (overstating accuracy, hiding assumptions/limitations, calling VaR a "maximum loss"), the violation is *generally accepted practices / clear-accurate-not-misleading communication* — not confidentiality or conflicts.

**Q5.** Two answer choices on an exam item both look ethical. Choice 1: the member quietly avoids a situation that could create a potential conflict. Choice 2: the member fully discloses the potential conflict to all affected parties and the limitations/uncertainty in his analysis. Per the chapter's guidance on resolving such ties, which is generally the Code-compliant choice and why?
- A. Choice 1, because avoidance always beats disclosure under the Code
- B. Choice 2, because when two options both seem ethical, the one involving disclosure/escalation and honest communication of uncertainty is usually the Code-compliant answer
- C. Neither, because the Code is silent on potential (as opposed to actual) conflicts
- D. Choice 1, because disclosing a conflict is itself a breach of confidentiality

**Answer: B**

The ask: apply the tie-breaking heuristic the chapter gives for Code questions.

1. The Code repeatedly emphasizes *transparency about limitations* and *disclosure* of conflicts. The chapter's explicit guidance: when two choices both look ethical, prefer the one involving disclosure/escalation and honest communication of uncertainty.
2. Choice 2 discloses the potential conflict to all affected parties *and* is transparent about analytical uncertainty — squarely the Code's preferred posture.

Why B is correct: it states the disclosure-over-silence tie-breaker the chapter prescribes.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A claims avoidance "always" beats disclosure, but the Code's operative duty for conflicts is *disclosure, not necessarily avoidance* — "always" is the giveaway.
- C says the Code ignores *potential* conflicts, but the conflicts principle explicitly covers "actual or potential" conflicts.
- D inverts confidentiality — disclosing one's *own* conflict to affected parties is not a confidentiality breach (no client secret is revealed).

📌 Exam tip: Tie-breaker for Code questions — choose disclosure, escalation, and honest communication of uncertainty over silence or quiet avoidance. The Code's twin themes are *transparency about limitations* and *full disclosure of conflicts to affected parties*.

**Q6.** A GARP member is found, after a disciplinary investigation, to have knowingly falsified model-validation results that also breached securities law. Which statement about the consequences is most accurate under the Code?
- A. GARP will criminally prosecute the member and impose a fine of up to $1 million
- B. GARP may suspend or revoke the member's membership and the right to use the FRM designation; separately, the legal violation may be referred to legal/regulatory authorities — the Code does not shield the member from the law
- C. The member faces no GARP action because securities law, not the Code, governs the conduct
- D. The only possible consequence is mandatory retraining administered by the employer

**Answer: B**

The ask: state the consequences of a Code violation that also breaks the law.

1. GARP's sanctions for Code violations can include *suspension or revocation of membership* and of the *right to use the FRM (or other GARP) designation*; candidates can be barred from the exam program.
2. Separately, *violations of law* are subject to referral to and action by legal/regulatory/governmental authorities — the Code does not shield anyone from the law. GARP itself does not criminally prosecute or levy fines.

Why B is correct: it correctly pairs GARP's disciplinary sanctions with separate legal referral.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A wrongly has GARP criminally prosecuting and fining — GARP's powers are membership/designation sanctions, not criminal penalties or monetary fines.
- C wrongly exempts the member from GARP action; falsifying validation results breaches the Code regardless of the parallel legal violation.
- D understates the consequences and invents an employer-retraining remedy not in the Code.

📌 Exam tip: GARP's teeth = suspension/revocation of membership and designation rights (and barring candidates). It does *not* impose criminal penalties or fines; legal violations are referred to the proper authorities. "GARP fines/prosecutes" answers are wrong.

**Q7.** Which of the following situations is *not* a violation of the GARP Code of Conduct?
- A. A member accepts lavish hospitality from a vendor that could reasonably be expected to appear to compromise her independence, and does not disclose it
- B. A member, served with a valid regulatory production order, discloses the specific client records the order requires
- C. A member presents a credit model's outputs to clients as "precise default probabilities" despite wide confidence bands
- D. A member lists himself as holding the FRM designation while still awaiting Part II results

**Answer: B**

The ask: find the *compliant* action among four scenarios.

1. The confidentiality principle permits revealing confidential information when disclosure is required by law or legal/regulatory process. Complying with a valid regulatory production order (analogous to a subpoena) is expressly permitted — and limiting disclosure to what the order requires is exactly right.

Why B is correct: legally compelled disclosure, narrowly scoped to what is required, is not a breach.

Why the distractors are violations:
- A breaches the *integrity/independence* duty — members must not accept gifts/benefits that could reasonably be expected to compromise (or appear to compromise) independence, and the non-disclosure compounds it.
- C breaches *generally accepted practices* — presenting model outputs as "precise" while hiding wide uncertainty overstates accuracy and fails to communicate limitations clearly.
- D breaches *professional integrity* — misrepresenting qualifications/the meaning of the FRM designation before earning it.

📌 Exam tip: The lone "not a violation" answer in Code questions is usually *consent-based or legally compelled disclosure*. Compelled disclosure (subpoena/regulatory order), kept to what the law requires, is permitted; everything involving overstated accuracy, hidden conflicts, or misrepresented credentials is a breach.
