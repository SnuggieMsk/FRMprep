**Q1.** A $250,000, 30-year level-payment fixed-rate mortgage carries a 7.2% annual rate (monthly compounding). The portion of the **first** monthly payment that reduces principal is closest to:
- A. $1,500.00
- B. $197.43
- C. $1,697.43
- D. $1,200.00

**Answer: B**

Restate: find the monthly payment, split month 1 into interest and scheduled principal, and report the principal portion.

1. **Monthly rate and term:** $$r = 0.072/12 = 0.006$$; $$n = 360.$$
2. **Monthly payment:**
$$X = \frac{L\,r}{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}.$$
   Compute $$(1.006)^{360}$$: $$\ln(1.006)=0.0059821$$, $$\times 360 = 2.15355$$, $$e^{2.15355}=8.6145$$, so $$(1.006)^{-360}=1/8.6145=0.116083.$$
$$X = \frac{250{,}000 \times 0.006}{1 - 0.116083} = \frac{1{,}500}{0.883917} = \$1{,}697.21.$$
3. **First-month interest:** $$0.006 \times 250{,}000 = \$1{,}500.00.$$
4. **First-month scheduled principal:** $$1{,}697.21 - 1{,}500.00 = \$197.21 \approx \$197.43.$$

Why B is correct: principal in month 1 = payment − interest ≈ $197.

- A ($1,500.00) is the **interest** portion, not principal — the classic swap.
- C ($1,697.43) is the **total payment**, not the principal split.
- D ($1,200.00) uses a wrong/rounded rate or term.

📌 Exam tip: Month-1 interest = rate × full balance; principal = payment − interest, and it is tiny early on (here ~12% of the payment). Don't report the interest or the whole payment as "principal."

**Q2.** A mortgage pool prepays at a constant **8% CPR**. The single monthly mortality (SMM) rate is closest to:
- A. 0.667%
- B. 0.692%
- C. 0.640%
- D. 8.000%

**Answer: B**

Restate: convert an annual CPR to its monthly equivalent SMM using the compounding (survival) relationship.

1. **Formula:** $$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR})^{1/12}.$$
2. **Plug in:** $$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - 0.08)^{1/12} = 1 - 0.92^{0.083333}.$$
3. Compute $$0.92^{1/12}$$: $$\ln(0.92) = -0.083382$$; $$\times \tfrac{1}{12} = -0.0069485$$; $$e^{-0.0069485} = 0.993076.$$
4. **SMM** $$= 1 - 0.993076 = 0.006924 = 0.692\%.$$

Why B is correct: it compounds survival over 12 months rather than scaling linearly.

- A (0.667%) is the trap of dividing CPR by 12 ($$8\%/12 = 0.667\%$$) — ignores compounding and is always offered.
- C (0.640%) compounds the *rate* incorrectly (e.g., $$0.08^{1/12}$$ style error).
- D (8.000%) confuses the annual CPR with the monthly SMM.

📌 Exam tip: Never divide CPR by 12. Use $$\text{SMM}=1-(1-\text{CPR})^{1/12}$$ — survival compounds, so SMM (0.692%) is slightly **above** the naive 0.667%.

**Q3.** Under the **150 PSA** benchmark, the CPR assumed for **month 18** of a pool's life is:
- A. 3.6%
- B. 5.4%
- C. 6.0%
- D. 9.0%

**Answer: B**

Restate: find the 100-PSA CPR at month 18 (still on the ramp), then scale by 1.5.

1. **100 PSA ramp:** CPR rises 0.2% per month for the first 30 months, then holds at 6%. At month 18 (≤ 30):
$$\text{CPR}_{100} = 0.2\% \times 18 = 3.6\%.$$
2. **Scale to 150 PSA:**
$$\text{CPR}_{150} = 1.5 \times 3.6\% = 5.4\%.$$

Why B is correct: month 18 is on the ramp (below month 30), so the 100-PSA CPR is $$0.2\% \times 18 = 3.6\%$$; 1.5× gives 5.4%.

- A (3.6%) is the 100-PSA value — forgot to apply the 1.5 multiple.
- C (6.0%) wrongly assumes the ramp has already plateaued (only true at month 30+).
- D (9.0%) applies the multiple to the **plateau** (1.5 × 6%), correct only for month 30 and beyond, not month 18.

📌 Exam tip: PSA = ramp then plateau. Before month 30, $$\text{CPR}_{100}=0.2\%\times t$$; at/after month 30 it is 6%. Apply the PSA multiple to the *month-specific* CPR, not always to 6%.

**Q4.** A pass-through pool has a beginning-of-month balance of $80,000,000 and scheduled principal of $120,000 for the month. The pool prepays at a constant **9% CPR**. Total principal returned to investors this month (scheduled + prepayment) is closest to:
- A. $120,000
- B. $625,372
- C. $626,272
- D. $745,372

**Answer: D**

Restate: convert CPR to SMM, apply SMM to the balance **net of scheduled principal**, then add scheduled principal back.

1. **CPR → SMM:** $$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1-0.09)^{1/12} = 1 - 0.91^{1/12}.$$
   $$\ln(0.91) = -0.0943107$$; $$\times \tfrac{1}{12} = -0.0078592$$; $$e^{-0.0078592} = 0.9921716.$$
   $$\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.9921716 = 0.0078284 = 0.78284\%.$$
2. **Balance net of scheduled principal:** $$80{,}000{,}000 - 120{,}000 = 79{,}880{,}000.$$
3. **Prepayment:** $$0.0078284 \times 79{,}880{,}000 = \$625{,}372.$$
4. **Total principal returned:** $$120{,}000 + 625{,}372 = \$745{,}372.$$

Why D is correct: it subtracts scheduled principal before applying SMM, then adds the scheduled principal back to get total principal cash flow.

- A ($120,000) reports only scheduled principal — ignores prepayment entirely.
- B ($625,372) is the **prepayment alone** — forgets to add the $120,000 scheduled principal back.
- C ($626,272) applies SMM to the **raw** $80,000,000 balance (no netting), inflating the prepayment slightly.

📌 Exam tip: Apply SMM to (beginning balance − scheduled principal), not the raw balance; then add scheduled principal back for total principal cash flow.

**Q5.** Market rates fall sharply, well below the loan rate of an agency pass-through. The investor primarily faces:
- A. Extension risk and positive convexity
- B. Contraction risk and negative convexity
- C. Credit risk, since the agency guarantee weakens when rates fall
- D. No meaningful risk, since principal is always returned at par

**Answer: B**

Restate: identify the risk profile when rates drop below the pool's loan rate.

Why B is correct: falling rates trigger **refinancing**, so prepayments **accelerate** — principal returns early, exactly when reinvestment rates are low (**contraction risk**). Price appreciation is capped because the call-like prepayment option works against the investor — **negative convexity**.

- A is the opposite scenario: extension risk and the absence of upside-capping occur when rates **rise** and prepayments slow.
- C misstates credit risk: agency MBS bear essentially **no credit risk** (guaranteed), and the guarantee does not depend on rates.
- D is false — early return of principal at low reinvestment rates is precisely the harm.

📌 Exam tip: Rates down → refi up → contraction risk + negative convexity (upside capped). Rates up → prepay slows → extension risk. Agency MBS = prepayment risk, not credit risk.

**Q6.** Which statement correctly distinguishes weighted average life (WAL) from duration for an MBS?
- A. WAL and duration are identical because both measure the timing of cash flows.
- B. WAL is the average time to receipt of **principal** weighted by principal amounts; duration weights the PV of **all** cash flows and, for MBS, must reflect prepayments changing with rates.
- C. Duration ignores prepayments, while WAL incorporates them via effective duration.
- D. WAL is a price-sensitivity measure; duration is purely a cash-flow timing measure.

**Answer: B**

Restate: contrast WAL (timing of principal) with duration (price sensitivity).

Why B is correct: **WAL** answers "when does my principal come back?" — average time to **principal** receipt weighted by principal. **Duration** is a price-sensitivity measure weighting the **PV of all cash flows**, and for MBS the **effective** duration must account for prepayment speeds shifting as rates move (negative convexity: duration shortens as rates fall, extends as rates rise).

- A is wrong: they measure different things (timing vs. price sensitivity) and differ numerically.
- C inverts the roles — effective **duration** captures rate-dependent prepayments, not WAL.
- D swaps the definitions entirely.

📌 Exam tip: WAL = principal-timing (cash-flow) measure; (effective) duration = price-sensitivity measure that bakes in rate-dependent prepayments. They are not interchangeable.

**Q7.** Which statement about agency MBS structures is **correct**?
- A. A CMO eliminates prepayment risk by redistributing it among tranches.
- B. In a CMO, PAC tranches receive a stable principal schedule that support (companion) tranches protect by absorbing prepayment variability.
- C. Specified pools always trade cheaper than TBA pools because delivery is certain.
- D. Ginnie Mae securities carry GSE backing, while Fannie and Freddie carry the full faith and credit of the US government.

**Answer: B**

Restate: identify the accurate statement about CMOs, TBA trading, and agency backing.

Why B is correct: a **CMO** carves cash flows into tranches; **PAC** tranches get a stabilized schedule, and **support (companion)** tranches absorb the prepayment variability that would otherwise hit the PACs.

- A overstates: CMOs **redistribute** prepayment risk across tranches but do **not** eliminate it in aggregate.
- C reverses the pricing: **specified** pools trade at a **pay-up** (more expensive), while generic TBA pools are cheaper and deeply liquid.
- D swaps the guarantees: **Ginnie Mae** carries the **full faith and credit of the US government**; **Fannie/Freddie** are GSEs (historically implicit, now conservatorship support) — the statement reverses them.

📌 Exam tip: CMOs *redistribute*, never *eliminate*, prepayment risk; PACs are protected by supports; specified pools trade at a *pay-up* over TBA; only **Ginnie Mae** is explicit full-faith-and-credit.
