**Q1.** A White test is run on a regression with $$n = 150$$ observations. The auxiliary regression of squared residuals on the regressors, their squares, and cross-products has 5 slope regressors and yields $$R^2_{\text{aux}} = 0.09$$. The critical $$\chi^2_5$$ at 5% is 11.07. What is the test statistic and conclusion?

- A. 13.5; reject homoskedasticity — use robust standard errors
- B. 13.5; fail to reject — errors are homoskedastic
- C. 0.45; fail to reject — errors are homoskedastic
- D. 9.0; fail to reject — errors are homoskedastic

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the White test statistic and conclusion.

1. The White test statistic is the sample size times the auxiliary $$R^2$$:
$$nR^2_{\text{aux}} = 150 \times 0.09 = 13.5$$
2. The degrees of freedom equal the number of auxiliary slope regressors, $$m = 5$$. Compare $$13.5$$ to $$\chi^2_5 = 11.07$$.
3. Since $$13.5 > 11.07$$, **reject** the null of homoskedasticity — the errors are heteroskedastic, so switch to White (robust) standard errors before trusting any t- or F-test.

Why A is correct: $$nR^2 = 13.5 > 11.07$$ ⇒ reject homoskedasticity.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes the statistic correctly but flips the decision (13.5 exceeds the critical value).
- **C** uses $$R^2_{\text{aux}} \times m / n$$ or some inverted form ($$0.09 \times 5 = 0.45$$) — not the White statistic.
- **D** uses $$n = 100$$ (the worked-example value) instead of 150, getting 9.0 and wrongly failing to reject.

📌 Exam tip: White test statistic = $$nR^2_{\text{aux}} \sim \chi^2_m$$, where $$m$$ = number of auxiliary slope terms. Large statistic ⇒ reject homoskedasticity. Heteroskedasticity does not bias coefficients, but you must use robust SEs.

**Q2.** In a five-regressor model, an analyst regresses regressor $$X_4$$ on the other four regressors and obtains $$R^2_4 = 0.92$$. What is the variance inflation factor for $$\hat\beta_4$$, and by what factor is its standard error inflated relative to the orthogonal case?

- A. VIF = 12.5; standard error about 3.5 times larger
- B. VIF = 12.5; standard error about 12.5 times larger
- C. VIF = 0.92; standard error about 0.96 times larger
- D. VIF = 8.0; standard error about 2.8 times larger

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the VIF and the corresponding inflation in the standard error.

1. The variance inflation factor:
$$VIF_4 = \frac{1}{1 - R_4^2} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.92} = \frac{1}{0.08} = 12.5$$
2. VIF multiplies the **variance** of the coefficient. The standard error scales with the square root:
$$\sqrt{VIF_4} = \sqrt{12.5} = 3.54 \approx 3.5$$
So the standard error is about 3.5 times what it would be with orthogonal regressors.

Why A is correct: $$1/0.08 = 12.5$$ for the variance factor, $$\sqrt{12.5} \approx 3.5$$ for the SE factor.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** correctly computes the VIF but applies it to the standard error directly — VIF inflates the *variance*; the SE grows by $$\sqrt{VIF}$$, not VIF.
- **C** reports $$R^2_4$$ as the VIF — forgetting the $$1/(1-R^2)$$ transformation.
- **D** uses $$R^2_4 = 0.875$$ (VIF 8) or otherwise miscomputes; $$1/0.08 = 12.5$$, not 8.

📌 Exam tip: $$VIF = 1/(1-R_j^2)$$ measures variance inflation; the standard error grows by $$\sqrt{VIF}$$. $$VIF > 10$$ (i.e., $$R_j^2 > 0.9$$) is the standard red flag for multicollinearity.

**Q3.** An analyst estimating the effect of firm size ($$X_1$$) on returns omits a relevant variable, profitability, which has a **positive** effect on returns and is **negatively** correlated with firm size. What is the direction of the resulting bias in $$\hat\beta_1$$?

- A. Downward (negative) bias
- B. Upward (positive) bias
- C. No bias, only a loss of efficiency
- D. The bias affects only the intercept, not $$\hat\beta_1$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the direction of omitted variable bias.

1. Omitted variable bias in the included coefficient has sign equal to:
$$\text{sign of bias} = \text{sign}(\text{omitted variable's effect on } Y) \times \text{sign}(\text{correlation of omitted with included } X)$$
2. Here the omitted variable (profitability) has a **positive** effect on $$Y$$ (+) and is **negatively** correlated with $$X_1$$ (−).
3. Product: $$(+) \times (-) = (-)$$ ⇒ the bias is **downward (negative)**. The size coefficient is pulled below its true value because it partly absorbs the negatively-correlated profitability effect.

Why A is correct: positive effect × negative correlation = negative bias.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** would be correct only if the correlation were positive (both signs positive ⇒ upward). The correlation here is negative.
- **C** describes including an *irrelevant* variable (unbiased, just less efficient) — the opposite specification error. Omitting a *relevant*, correlated variable biases the coefficient.
- **D** is wrong — omitted variable bias hits the slope coefficient of the included regressor, not just the intercept.

📌 Exam tip: Omitted variable bias sign = (effect of omitted on Y) × (correlation of omitted with included X). Only an omitted *relevant and correlated* variable biases coefficients; an *irrelevant* included variable just costs efficiency.

**Q4.** Which statement correctly contrasts the consequences of (i) omitting a relevant variable correlated with an included regressor versus (ii) including an irrelevant variable?

- A. Omitting a relevant correlated variable biases coefficients; including an irrelevant variable leaves coefficients unbiased but raises estimator variance
- B. Both errors bias the coefficients, but omission is worse
- C. Including an irrelevant variable biases coefficients; omission only reduces efficiency
- D. Both errors leave coefficients unbiased; only the standard errors are affected

**Answer: A**

We are asked to compare the two specification errors.

1. **Omitting a relevant variable** that is correlated with an included regressor: the included coefficient absorbs the omitted effect, making it **biased and inconsistent**. This is the serious error.
2. **Including an irrelevant variable**: the coefficients remain **unbiased and consistent**, but the estimator's variance rises (wasted degrees of freedom and possible collinearity) — a pure **efficiency** cost, not a bias.

Why A is correct: it pairs the bias (omission) with the efficiency loss (inclusion) correctly.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** wrongly claims inclusion biases coefficients — it does not.
- **C** reverses the two: inclusion does not bias, and omission does more than reduce efficiency (it biases).
- **D** wrongly says neither biases coefficients — omitting a relevant correlated variable certainly biases them.

📌 Exam tip: Omission of a relevant correlated variable = bias (dangerous). Inclusion of an irrelevant variable = unbiased but inefficient (a cost, not a bias). When in doubt, a researcher errs toward including, because the penalty is milder.

**Q5.** A scatter plot reveals a single observation with an extreme $$X$$ value lying far from the rest of the data. The analyst is concerned about its influence on the OLS fit. Which statement is most accurate?

- A. High-leverage outliers can dominate OLS because residuals are squared; investigate with diagnostics like Cook's distance and verify data validity before deleting
- B. Outliers in $$X$$ have no effect on OLS slope estimates because OLS is unbiased
- C. The single point should be deleted immediately to restore a good fit
- D. Outliers only matter if they are in the $$Y$$ direction, never the $$X$$ direction

**Answer: A**

We are asked about the influence of a high-leverage outlier on OLS.

1. OLS minimizes the **sum of squared** residuals. Because errors are squared, an observation with an extreme $$X$$ value (high leverage) can exert disproportionate pull on the fitted line — a single point can swing the slope.
2. The recommended practice is to **diagnose** the influence (scatter plots, Cook's distance) and **verify data validity** (is it a recording error or a genuine observation?) before deciding whether to delete, downweight, or use a robust estimator.

Why A is correct: it correctly notes the squared-error mechanism and the diagnose-then-verify approach.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** confuses unbiasedness (a property over many samples / correct model) with sensitivity to a single influential point. A leverage point absolutely can distort the estimate in the realized sample.
- **C** "delete immediately" is bad practice — the point may be valid and informative; deletion without investigation introduces its own bias.
- **D** is false — extreme $$X$$ values (leverage) are precisely the dangerous outliers, often more so than $$Y$$-direction outliers.

📌 Exam tip: Squaring residuals is what gives outliers their bite. High-leverage points (extreme in $$X$$) are especially influential. Diagnose with Cook's distance; never delete data without checking validity.

**Q6.** An analyst is choosing among candidate models and worries about the bias–variance trade-off. Which description correctly characterizes the trade-off and a model-selection method that directly targets out-of-sample prediction error?

- A. Too few variables causes bias (underfitting); too many causes variance (overfitting); m-fold cross-validation directly targets out-of-sample error
- B. Too few variables causes variance; too many causes bias; in-sample $$R^2$$ directly targets out-of-sample error
- C. More variables always reduce both bias and variance; AIC measures in-sample fit only
- D. The general-to-specific method adds variables one at a time until $$R^2$$ stops rising

**Answer: A**

We are asked to characterize the bias–variance trade-off and identify a method aimed at out-of-sample error.

1. **Too few variables ⇒ bias (underfitting):** the model misses real structure.
2. **Too many variables ⇒ variance (overfitting):** the model fits in-sample noise and predicts poorly out-of-sample.
3. **m-fold cross-validation** splits the data into $$m$$ blocks, repeatedly fits on $$m-1$$ blocks and evaluates on the held-out block, then averages. Because it scores models on data not used in fitting, it **directly targets out-of-sample prediction error** rather than in-sample fit.

Why A is correct: it correctly assigns bias to underfitting and variance to overfitting, and names cross-validation as the out-of-sample tool.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** reverses bias and variance, and claims in-sample $$R^2$$ targets out-of-sample error — in-sample $$R^2$$ rewards overfitting, the opposite.
- **C** is false — more variables reduce bias but *increase* variance; AIC penalizes parameters (it is not "in-sample fit only").
- **D** misdescribes general-to-specific: it *starts with everything* and iteratively **drops** the least significant variable, and it uses $$|t|$$ significance, not "until $$R^2$$ stops rising" (R² never falls when adding variables).

📌 Exam tip: Underfit = bias, overfit = variance. Cross-validation and BIC/AIC are the tools that fight overfitting by penalizing or testing out-of-sample. General-to-specific *drops* the least-significant regressor iteratively.

**Q7.** A researcher runs an OLS regression and detects heteroskedasticity via the White test, but confirms exogeneity holds and no relevant variables are omitted. The researcher then reports the original OLS coefficients with the usual (non-robust) standard errors. What is the most accurate critique?

- A. The coefficients are fine, but the reported standard errors are likely understated, so significance is overstated — robust standard errors are needed
- B. Both the coefficients and standard errors are biased and must be re-estimated by a different method
- C. Nothing is wrong; heteroskedasticity has no effect on inference in large samples
- D. The coefficients are biased, but the standard errors are valid, so confidence intervals are reliable

**Answer: A**

We are asked to critique reporting non-robust SEs under confirmed heteroskedasticity (with exogeneity intact).

1. Heteroskedasticity violates only the constant-variance assumption. Since exogeneity holds and no relevant variable is omitted, the OLS **coefficients remain unbiased and consistent** — they are fine.
2. The usual (non-robust) standard errors assume homoskedasticity and are therefore **wrong under heteroskedasticity — typically understated**. Understated SEs inflate t-statistics, **overstating significance**.
3. The fix: report **heteroskedasticity-robust (White) standard errors**, keeping the same coefficients.

Why A is correct: coefficients valid, SEs understated/invalid, significance overstated — use robust SEs.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** claims biased coefficients — false; heteroskedasticity does not bias coefficients when exogeneity holds.
- **C** "no effect on inference" is wrong — heteroskedasticity is precisely an inference (standard-error) problem.
- **D** has it backwards: coefficients are unbiased and the SEs are the unreliable part.

📌 Exam tip: Heteroskedasticity is an inference disease, not a bias disease. Non-robust SEs are usually too small under heteroskedasticity, so t-stats look better than they should. Keep the coefficients; swap in robust SEs.

**Q8.** Two diagnostic situations are described: (i) regressors $$X_1$$ and $$X_2$$ have a sample correlation of 0.97 but neither is an exact function of the other; (ii) a dummy for "weekday" and a dummy for "weekend" are both included along with an intercept, and every day is exactly one or the other. How should each be classified and treated?

- A. (i) is imperfect multicollinearity — OLS runs but SEs inflate; (ii) is perfect collinearity — a regressor must be dropped
- B. (i) is perfect collinearity — OLS fails; (ii) is imperfect multicollinearity — keep all variables
- C. Both are imperfect multicollinearity; robust standard errors fix both
- D. Both are perfect collinearity; OLS cannot be estimated in either case

**Answer: A**

We are asked to distinguish imperfect multicollinearity from perfect (exact) collinearity and prescribe the treatment.

1. **Situation (i):** correlation of 0.97 is high but **less than 1**, and neither regressor is an exact linear function of the other. This is **imperfect multicollinearity**: OLS still runs and coefficients stay unbiased, but standard errors balloon (individual t's look weak, signs unstable). Remedies: drop/combine variables, get more data, or accept the imprecision.
2. **Situation (ii):** weekday dummy + weekend dummy = 1 for every observation = the intercept column. That is an **exact** linear dependence — **perfect collinearity** (the dummy variable trap). OLS cannot be computed; one of the redundant regressors **must be dropped**.

Why A is correct: (i) imperfect (runs, inflated SEs); (ii) perfect (must drop a regressor).

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** swaps the two classifications.
- **C** calls both imperfect and claims robust SEs fix them — robust SEs address heteroskedasticity, not collinearity, and case (ii) cannot even be estimated.
- **D** calls both perfect — but a 0.97 correlation is not exact dependence, so (i) is estimable.

📌 Exam tip: Perfect collinearity = exact linear relationship ⇒ OLS undefined ⇒ drop a regressor. Imperfect multicollinearity = high-but-not-exact correlation ⇒ OLS runs, coefficients unbiased, but SEs inflate. The dummy-trap (all categories + intercept) is the canonical perfect-collinearity case.
