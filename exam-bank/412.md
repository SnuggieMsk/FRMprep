**Q1.** A bond has a price of $102.50, a modified duration of 6.4, and a convexity of 75. Using the duration-plus-convexity estimate, the dollar price change for a 120 bp increase in yield is closest to:

- A. −$7.87
- B. −$7.32
- C. −$8.42
- D. −$6.97

**Answer: B**

The ask: estimate the dollar price change for Δy = +0.012 using both the duration and convexity terms.

1. Formula: $$\Delta P \approx -D_{Mod} \cdot P \cdot \Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot C \cdot P \cdot (\Delta y)^2$$
2. Duration term:
   $$-6.4 \times 102.50 \times 0.012 = -7.8720$$
3. Convexity term:
   $$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 75 \times 102.50 \times (0.012)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 75 \times 102.50 \times 0.000144 = 0.5535$$
4. Total:
   $$-7.8720 + 0.5535 = -\$7.32$$

- A (−$7.87) is the duration-only estimate — it ignores the convexity cushion (the +$0.55 term).
- C (−$8.42) wrongly SUBTRACTS the convexity term (sign error) — convexity always adds value for an option-free bond.
- D (−$6.97) forgets the ½ on the convexity term, overstating the cushion to +$1.11.

📌 Exam tip: Convexity is always positive for option-free bonds, so it ADDS to value in both directions — it cushions losses when rates rise. Keep Δy in decimal and never drop the ½.

**Q2.** A trader is long a corporate bond portfolio with a DV01 of $18,400. She hedges with a Treasury bond whose DV01 is $0.091 per $100 of face value. The face amount of the Treasury bond she must short is closest to:

- A. $202,200
- B. $20.2 million
- C. $2.02 million
- D. $167.4 million

**Answer: B**

The ask: find the face amount of the hedge bond so the combined DV01 is zero.

1. The hedge must supply a DV01 of $18,400 (opposite sign, achieved by shorting).
2. The hedge bond's DV01 is $0.091 per $100 face. Per $1 of face that is $0.00091.
3. Required face:
   $$F = \frac{18{,}400}{0.00091} = 20{,}219{,}780 \approx \$20.2\text{ million}$$
   Equivalently, working in "hundreds of face": $$18{,}400 / 0.091 = 202{,}198$$ hundreds of face = $20.2 million.

- A ($202,200) stops at the number of $100 units and forgets to multiply by 100 to get dollars of face.
- C ($2.02 million) is off by a factor of 10 — a decimal slip in the per-$1 DV01 (using $0.0091 instead of $0.00091).
- D ($167.4 million) wrongly divides by a much smaller DV01 or inverts the ratio.

📌 Exam tip: Watch the DV01 quoting convention. "Per $100 face" must be converted to per $1 of face (divide by 100) before dividing into the position DV01, or work consistently in units of $100.

**Q3.** A trader holds $40 million of a 10-year bullet bond with modified duration 7.2. He builds a duration-matched barbell from a 2-year bond (duration 1.8) and a 30-year bond (duration 17.0), equal total market value. The fraction invested in the 2-year bond is closest to:

- A. 64.5%
- B. 35.5%
- C. 56.9%
- D. 43.1%

**Answer: A**

The ask: solve for the weight w in the 2-year bond so the barbell's duration equals the bullet's 7.2.

1. Duration-matching equation:
   $$w \cdot D_{short} + (1-w) \cdot D_{long} = D_{bullet}$$
   $$1.8w + 17.0(1-w) = 7.2$$
2. Expand:
   $$1.8w + 17.0 - 17.0w = 7.2$$
   $$17.0 - 15.2w = 7.2$$
3. Solve:
   $$15.2w = 9.8 \;\Rightarrow\; w = \frac{9.8}{15.2} = 0.6447 \approx 64.5\%$$

- B (35.5%) is the complement (1 − w) — the weight in the 30-year bond, not the 2-year.
- C (56.9%) uses the wrong target or arithmetic, e.g., dividing 9.8 by 17.0.
- D (43.1%) misplaces the durations (treating the 2-year as the long leg).

📌 Exam tip: Set up $$w D_{short} + (1-w) D_{long} = D_{target}$$ and solve for w on the SHORT bond. Then double-check which leg the question asks about — the complement is a classic distractor.

**Q4.** A barbell and a bullet portfolio have identical market value and identical modified duration. Following a sudden 200 bp parallel SHIFT DOWN in yields, which statement is correct?

- A. The bullet outperforms because it has shorter cash flows.
- B. The barbell outperforms because it has greater convexity.
- C. The two portfolios produce identical P&L because durations match.
- D. The bullet outperforms only if the curve simultaneously flattens.

**Answer: B**

The ask: compare barbell vs bullet P&L for a large parallel move when durations are matched.

1. Equal duration ⟹ identical FIRST-ORDER (linear) price response to a parallel move.
2. The barbell's cash flows are more dispersed (short + long legs), giving it greater convexity. Convexity rises roughly with maturity squared.
3. The second-order term $$+\tfrac{1}{2} C P (\Delta y)^2$$ is positive and larger for the barbell. For a large 200 bp move, this convexity advantage makes the barbell outperform — in EITHER direction.

- A is backward — the bullet does not outperform on a large parallel move; the barbell does.
- C ignores convexity; durations matching only equalizes the first-order term, and 200 bp is large enough for the second-order term to matter.
- D introduces an irrelevant curve-shape condition; the question specifies a PARALLEL move, where the barbell's convexity wins outright.

📌 Exam tip: Same duration ⟹ same first-order P&L; the barbell's extra convexity makes it win for ANY large parallel move (up or down). The price of that convexity is usually a lower portfolio yield.

**Q5.** A portfolio holds $30 million of Bond A (duration 4.0, convexity 25) and $20 million of Bond B (duration 9.0, convexity 110). The portfolio's modified duration and convexity are closest to:

- A. Duration 6.0, convexity 59
- B. Duration 6.5, convexity 67.5
- C. Duration 6.0, convexity 67.5
- D. Duration 6.5, convexity 59

**Answer: A**

The ask: compute value-weighted portfolio duration and convexity.

1. Total value = $30M + $20M = $50M. Weights: A = 30/50 = 0.60, B = 20/50 = 0.40.
2. Portfolio duration (value-weighted average):
   $$D_{port} = 0.60 \times 4.0 + 0.40 \times 9.0 = 2.40 + 3.60 = 6.0$$
3. Portfolio convexity (value-weighted average):
   $$C_{port} = 0.60 \times 25 + 0.40 \times 110 = 15.0 + 44.0 = 59.0$$

- B uses equal weights (0.5/0.5) for both metrics: duration $$0.5 \times 4 + 0.5 \times 9 = 6.5$$, convexity 67.5.
- C pairs the right duration with an equal-weighted convexity ($$0.5 \times 25 + 0.5 \times 110 = 67.5$$).
- D mixes an equal-weighted duration (6.5) with the value-weighted convexity (59).

📌 Exam tip: Portfolio duration and convexity are VALUE-weighted (by market value), not equal-weighted or face-weighted. Compute the weights once and apply them to both metrics.

**Q6.** Effective duration, rather than modified duration, must be used to measure the interest-rate sensitivity of which instrument?

- A. A 10-year option-free par bond
- B. A callable corporate bond
- C. A zero-coupon Treasury STRIP
- D. A 30-year option-free premium bond

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the instrument whose cash flows are rate-dependent, requiring a repricing-based duration measure.

1. Modified duration uses a closed-form yield-based formula that assumes FIXED cash flows.
2. A callable bond's cash flows change with rates: when rates fall, the issuer is more likely to call, truncating the cash flows. This makes the yield-based formula invalid.
3. Effective duration is computed by shifting the curve and repricing (capturing the option), so it correctly handles callables, putables, and MBS.

- A, C, D are all option-free with fixed cash flows, so modified ≈ effective duration — the closed-form measure is fine.
- C (a zero) in fact has the cleanest closed-form duration of all ($$D_{Mac} = T$$).

📌 Exam tip: Use EFFECTIVE duration whenever cash flows change with rates — callables, putables, MBS. For option-free bonds, effective ≈ modified, so either works.

**Q7.** A bond is priced at $96.40 today. When the entire yield curve is shifted up by 25 bp it is repriced at $95.13, and when shifted down by 25 bp it is repriced at $97.69. The bond's effective duration is closest to:

- A. 5.31
- B. 10.62
- C. 2.65
- D. 5.25

**Answer: A**

The ask: compute effective duration from the up-shift and down-shift repriced values.

1. Formula: $$D_{Eff} = \frac{P_{-} - P_{+}}{2 \cdot P_0 \cdot \Delta y}$$
   where $$P_{-}$$ = price after a DOWN shift = $97.69, $$P_{+}$$ = price after an UP shift = $95.13, $$P_0$$ = $96.40, Δy = 0.0025.
2. Numerator:
   $$P_{-} - P_{+} = 97.69 - 95.13 = 2.56$$
3. Denominator:
   $$2 \times 96.40 \times 0.0025 = 0.4820$$
4. Effective duration:
   $$D_{Eff} = \frac{2.56}{0.4820} = 5.31$$

- B (10.62) forgets the 2 in the denominator (uses one-sided scaling), doubling the result.
- C (2.65) uses Δy = 0.005 (50 bp, the full up-to-down range) instead of the 25 bp shift size in the denominator's 2·Δy convention applied incorrectly.
- D (5.25) is a near-miss from rounding the repriced values or mis-subtracting.

📌 Exam tip: In the effective duration formula the numerator is (down-shift price − up-shift price) and the denominator is 2·P₀·Δy, where Δy is the SIZE of one shift (0.0025), not the total range. Keep P₋ and P₊ straight.
