**Q1.** A risk committee is comparing stress testing with VaR and expected shortfall (ES). Which statement most accurately captures the relationship?

- A. Stress testing replaces VaR because VaR failed to predict the 2007–09 crisis.
- B. Stress testing and VaR/ES are complements: VaR/ES attach probabilities within the sampled distribution, while stress testing probes specific extreme scenarios without attaching probabilities.
- C. Stress testing assigns precise probabilities to severe scenarios, making VaR redundant.
- D. A 99% ES is always larger than any stress-test loss because ES captures the entire tail.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct characterization of stress testing versus VaR/ES.

Why B is correct: VaR/ES are probabilistic measures calibrated to recent history (blind beyond the sample); stress tests examine "what happens in *this specific* extreme scenario," generally without attaching a probability. They answer different questions and are used together.

Distractor traps:
- A overstates the relationship — backward-looking VaR did fail in 2007 (calm prior sample), but stress testing *complements*, it does not *replace*, VaR.
- C is the classic false statement — stress tests usually do NOT assign probabilities; that is precisely what they give up in exchange for capturing unprecedented events.
- D imposes a false ordering — a stress loss can exceed or fall short of a 99% ES; there is no fixed relationship because they measure different things.

📌 Exam tip: "Stress testing replaces VaR" (false), "stress tests assign probabilities" (false), and "ES > stress loss always" (false) are three of GARP's favorite trap statements. They are complements; stress losses have no fixed ordering versus ES.

**Q2.** Which description best defines reverse stress testing?

- A. Applying historical crisis scenarios in reverse chronological order.
- B. Stressing only the liability side of the balance sheet.
- C. Starting from a defined adverse outcome (e.g., breaching capital minima or failure) and searching backward for the scenarios that would produce it.
- D. Re-running a standard stress test with the signs of all shocks reversed.

**Answer: C**

The ask: identify the defining feature of reverse stress testing.

Why C is correct: a standard test runs scenario → loss; the reverse test starts from the outcome ("what would make us fail?") and searches the factor space backward for the most plausible scenarios that reach that loss threshold.

Distractor traps:
- A confuses "reverse" with chronological ordering — irrelevant.
- B arbitrarily restricts the exercise to liabilities — reverse tests can involve any risk factors.
- D literally flips shock signs, which is not what "reverse" means here.

📌 Exam tip: Reverse stress testing is *outcome-first*. Its value is surfacing hidden vulnerabilities and concentration combinations management never imagined; UK/EU regulators require it for recovery and resolution planning.

**Q3.** An analyst builds a hypothetical scenario in which global equities fall 40%, but leaves credit spreads, volatility, and FX unchanged from current levels. The principal weakness of this scenario is that it:

- A. Is too severe to be plausible.
- B. Lacks coherence: in a 40% equity crash, credit spreads would widen and volatility would spike, so holding them flat understates the loss.
- C. Uses a historical episode, which cannot apply to a current portfolio.
- D. Assigns a probability that is too low.

**Answer: B**

The ask: diagnose the flaw in a hypothetical scenario that shocks one variable in isolation.

Why B is correct: a coherent scenario requires all variables to move in an internally consistent way. A 40% equity collapse would co-move with wider credit spreads and higher volatility; freezing them violates coherence and understates the true stressed loss.

Distractor traps:
- A misdiagnoses — severity is not the issue; *incoherence* is. A severe-but-coherent scenario is desirable.
- C is irrelevant — this is a hypothetical (constructed) scenario, not a historical replay.
- D is irrelevant — hypothetical scenarios generally carry no probability at all.

📌 Exam tip: Coherence = consistent co-movement. You cannot shock equities −40% while leaving spreads/vol/FX untouched. Approaches to coherence: shock core variables and set the rest via estimated conditional relationships, or use a full macro model.

**Q4.** Under the U.S. CCAR program, supervisory stress-test results primarily constrain a large bank's:

- A. Trading-desk VaR limits.
- B. Deposit interest rates.
- C. Capital distributions (dividends and buybacks) via projected post-stress capital ratios over a multi-quarter horizon.
- D. Loan pricing to retail customers.

**Answer: C**

The ask: identify what CCAR results gate.

Why C is correct: CCAR (and DFAST) project revenues, losses, and capital ratios over a 9-quarter horizon under supervisory baseline and severely adverse scenarios; the results feed the stress capital buffer, which gates capital distributions.

Distractor traps:
- A — desk-level VaR limits are an internal market-risk control, not the CCAR output.
- B and D — CCAR does not set deposit rates or loan prices; those are commercial decisions.

📌 Exam tip: CCAR/DFAST → projected capital ratios under adverse scenarios → stress capital buffer → constraint on dividends/buybacks. The 2009 SCAP restored confidence by publishing credible results with a recapitalization backstop.

**Q5.** A bank's stress-testing program produces detailed reports, but the results are never escalated to the board and never change risk appetite, limits, or capital plans. The most accurate governance critique is that the program:

- A. Is adequate, since producing the reports satisfies the requirement.
- B. Fails the "use test" — results must feed risk-management decisions; tests that never affect decisions are theater.
- C. Should be discontinued because stress testing is a regulatory formality.
- D. Needs only more severe scenarios to become useful.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the governance failure.

Why B is correct: board and senior management own the program and must act on results. The "use test" requires that outputs feed risk appetite, limits, and capital planning; reports that change nothing are theater.

Distractor traps:
- A is exactly the misconception — producing reports is not the goal; influencing decisions is.
- C dismisses stress testing wrongly — it is a core risk-governance tool, not an empty formality.
- D misdiagnoses — the problem is governance/use, not scenario severity.

📌 Exam tip: Governance failures GARP flags: scenarios watered down to pass, results not escalated, and infrastructure unable to aggregate exposures quickly (addressed by BCBS 239 data-aggregation principles).

**Q6.** Which statement about historical versus hypothetical stress scenarios is most accurate?

- A. Historical scenarios are internally coherent but may omit novel risks (e.g., crypto, climate) absent from past data.
- B. Hypothetical scenarios are always more objective than historical scenarios.
- C. Historical scenarios are sufficient on their own because every future crisis resembles a past one.
- D. Hypothetical scenarios cannot include second-round (knock-on) effects.

**Answer: A**

The ask: identify the correct comparison of scenario types.

Why A is correct: historical scenarios replay actual crises, so their co-movements are internally coherent and objective — but the next crisis is never the last one, and current portfolios may carry new risks absent from history.

Distractor traps:
- B reverses objectivity — *historical* scenarios are more objective; hypothetical ones are subjective by construction.
- C is the "historical scenarios are sufficient" trap — false, because they miss unprecedented risks.
- D is false — well-built hypothetical scenarios *should* include knock-on effects (fire sales, funding runs, counterparty failures), which made 2008 far worse than first-round losses.

📌 Exam tip: Historical = coherent, objective, but backward-looking. Hypothetical = forward-looking and tailored, but subjective and hard to keep coherent. Good scenarios are severe *and* plausible and include second-round effects.

**Q7.** During 2008 and again in 2023 (Silicon Valley Bank), a key lesson about stress testing was that:

- A. Capital stress tests alone are sufficient to ensure survival.
- B. Liquidity stress tests are unnecessary once capital ratios are strong.
- C. Liquidity can kill an institution before solvency does, so liquidity stress tests (survival horizons, funding-run scenarios) complement capital stress tests.
- D. Reverse stress testing is irrelevant for deposit-funded banks.

**Answer: C**

The ask: identify the correct capital-versus-liquidity stress-testing lesson.

Why C is correct: capital tests ask whether equity survives losses; liquidity tests (LCR-style, survival horizons) ask whether cash outflows can be met under funding runs. 2008 and SVB in 2023 showed a firm can be solvent yet fail because deposits/funding flee faster than assets can be liquidated.

Distractor traps:
- A is false — capital adequacy did not save SVB from a deposit run.
- B reverses the lesson — strong capital ratios do not remove the need for liquidity stress testing.
- D is false — reverse stress testing is highly relevant for deposit-funded banks (deposit-flight scenarios are exactly what it surfaces).

📌 Exam tip: Distinguish capital (solvency) from liquidity (funding) stress tests. The SVB-era takeaway: liquidity risk can be fatal even when capital looks adequate. Severe-but-plausible scenarios should capture funding runs and second-round effects.
