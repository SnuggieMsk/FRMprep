**Q1.** A bank holds a single $40 million corporate loan with a 1-year probability of default of 3%, an expected recovery rate of 35%, and exposure at default equal to the full face amount. Which value is closest to the correct one-year expected loss?

- A. $420,000
- B. $780,000
- C. $1,200,000
- D. $1,560,000

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute one-year expected loss for the single loan.

1. Formula: $$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$
2. LGD = 1 − recovery rate = 1 − 0.35 = 0.65.
3. Plug in: $$EL = 0.03 \times 0.65 \times \$40{,}000{,}000$$
4. Compute: $$0.03 \times 0.65 = 0.0195$$, then $$0.0195 \times \$40{,}000{,}000 = \$780{,}000.$$

Why B is correct: it uses LGD = 65%, the fraction actually lost given default.

Distractor traps:
- A ($420,000) uses LGD = 35% — confuses the recovery rate with LGD (the classic recovery/LGD flip): $$0.03 \times 0.35 \times 40\text{M} = 420{,}000.$$
- C ($1,200,000) uses LGD = 100%, ignoring recovery entirely: $$0.03 \times 1.0 \times 40\text{M} = 1.2\text{M}.$$
- D ($1,560,000) doubles the correct figure (e.g., misreads PD as 6%).

📌 Exam tip: LGD = 1 − recovery. The single most common EL error is plugging the recovery rate where LGD belongs. The stem gave you a recovery rate, not an LGD — always convert.

**Q2.** A $20 million exposure has PD = 4% (treated as Bernoulli), an LGD with mean 50% and standard deviation 30%, and EAD equal to the full $20 million. The unexpected loss (standard deviation of the credit loss) is closest to:

- A. $400,000
- B. $1,200,000
- C. $2,298,000
- D. $5,000,000

**Answer: C**

The ask: compute the unexpected loss (UL) — the standard deviation of loss for a single exposure.

1. Formula: $$UL = EAD \times \sqrt{PD \cdot \sigma_{LGD}^2 + LGD^2 \cdot \sigma_{PD}^2}$$
2. Default variance (Bernoulli): $$\sigma_{PD}^2 = PD(1-PD) = 0.04 \times 0.96 = 0.0384.$$
3. First term: $$PD \cdot \sigma_{LGD}^2 = 0.04 \times 0.30^2 = 0.04 \times 0.09 = 0.0036.$$
4. Second term: $$LGD^2 \cdot \sigma_{PD}^2 = 0.50^2 \times 0.0384 = 0.25 \times 0.0384 = 0.0096.$$
5. Sum inside the root: $$0.0036 + 0.0096 = 0.0132.$$
6. Square root: $$\sqrt{0.0132} = 0.11489.$$
7. Multiply by EAD: $$\$20{,}000{,}000 \times 0.11489 = \$2{,}297{,}800 \approx \$2.30\text{M}.$$

Why C is correct: UL captures both sources of variance — uncertainty over whether default occurs and uncertainty over the loss given default.

Distractor traps:
- A ($400,000) is just EL = $$0.04 \times 0.50 \times 20\text{M}$$ — confuses UL with EL.
- B ($1,200,000) keeps only the first term, $$EAD \times \sqrt{PD\cdot\sigma_{LGD}^2} = 20\text{M}\times\sqrt{0.0036}=20\text{M}\times0.06=1.2\text{M}$$ — it omits the dominant $$LGD^2\sigma_{PD}^2$$ piece (default-occurrence uncertainty).
- D ($5,000,000) ignores PD and computes $$EAD \times LGD/2$$, treating the loss as nearly certain.

📌 Exam tip: UL has two variance pieces — PD uncertainty (Bernoulli, $$PD(1-PD)$$) and LGD uncertainty. If the problem gives a σ for LGD, both terms belong. UL is typically several times larger than EL.

**Q3.** A well-diversified, granular loan portfolio has PD = 1% and asset (copula) correlation ρ = 0.20. Using the Vasicek single-factor model at the 99.9% confidence level, with $$N^{-1}(0.01) = -2.326$$ and $$N^{-1}(0.999) = 3.090$$, the worst-case default rate (WCDR) is closest to:

- A. 1.00%
- B. 7.75%
- C. 14.56%
- D. 22.45%

**Answer: C**

The ask: compute the 99.9% WCDR under the single-factor (Vasicek) model.

1. Formula: $$WCDR = N\!\left(\frac{N^{-1}(PD) + \sqrt{\rho}\,N^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)$$
2. $$\sqrt{\rho} = \sqrt{0.20} = 0.4472$$; $$\sqrt{1-\rho} = \sqrt{0.80} = 0.8944.$$
3. Numerator: $$-2.326 + 0.4472 \times 3.090 = -2.326 + 1.382 = -0.944.$$
4. Divide: $$\frac{-0.944}{0.8944} = -1.0555.$$
5. Apply the normal CDF: $$N(-1.0555) \approx 0.1456 = 14.56\%.$$

Why C is correct: signs handled properly — $$N^{-1}(PD)$$ negative, $$N^{-1}(0.999)$$ positive — and division by $$\sqrt{1-\rho}.$$

Distractor traps:
- A (1.00%) is the unconditional PD — correct only if ρ = 0; here ρ = 0.20, so systematic risk does not diversify away.
- B (7.75%) is the WCDR for ρ = 0.10, not 0.20 — using the wrong correlation.
- D (22.45%) flips a sign (e.g., divides by $$\sqrt{\rho}$$ instead of $$\sqrt{1-\rho}$$), inflating the argument.

📌 Exam tip: WCDR climbs with ρ. With ρ = 0 it collapses to PD (full diversification); higher ρ fattens the tail. Keep signs straight and divide by $$\sqrt{1-\rho}.$$

**Q4.** Continuing from Q3 (WCDR = 14.56%, PD = 1%), the portfolio has EAD = $300 million and LGD = 60%. The one-year 99.9% credit VaR (economic capital) is closest to:

- A. $1.80 million
- B. $24.41 million
- C. $26.21 million
- D. $43.68 million

**Answer: B**

The ask: convert the WCDR into credit VaR (capital above expected loss).

1. Formula: $$\text{Credit VaR} = EAD \times LGD \times (WCDR - PD)$$
2. Plug in: $$\$300\text{M} \times 0.60 \times (0.1456 - 0.01)$$
3. Inner term: $$0.1456 - 0.01 = 0.1356.$$
4. Compute: $$300\text{M} \times 0.60 = 180\text{M}$$; $$180\text{M} \times 0.1356 = \$24.41\text{M}.$$

Why B is correct: capital covers *unexpected* loss, so we subtract PD (the EL already provisioned).

Distractor traps:
- A ($1.80M) is the expected loss, $$300\text{M}\times0.60\times0.01$$ — that is provisioned, not capital.
- C ($26.21M) forgets to subtract PD: $$300\text{M}\times0.60\times0.1456$$ — overstates capital (the #1 trap).
- D ($43.68M) uses $$WCDR - PD$$ but with WCDR for a higher ρ, or double-counts LGD.

📌 Exam tip: Credit VaR uses $$(WCDR - PD)$$, not WCDR alone. EL is a cost of doing business (provisioned); capital absorbs the gap between the 99.9% loss and EL.

**Q5.** Two loans each have UL = $1,000,000. The default correlation between them is ρ = 0.25. The portfolio unexpected loss is closest to:

- A. $2,000,000
- B. $1,581,000
- C. $1,118,000
- D. $500,000

**Answer: B**

The ask: aggregate two equal ULs given a default correlation.

1. Formula: $$UL_P = \sqrt{UL_1^2 + UL_2^2 + 2\rho\,UL_1 UL_2}$$
2. Plug in (in millions): $$\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 2(0.25)(1)(1)}$$
3. Inside: $$1 + 1 + 0.5 = 2.5.$$
4. Root: $$\sqrt{2.5} = 1.5811 \Rightarrow \$1{,}581{,}000.$$

Why B is correct: with imperfect correlation (ρ < 1), portfolio UL sits below the simple sum because risks partially offset.

Distractor traps:
- A ($2,000,000) is the simple sum of ULs — valid only if ρ = 1 (perfect correlation); expectation adds linearly but standard deviations do not.
- C ($1,118,000) subtracts the correlation term instead of adding it: $$\sqrt{1 + 1 - 0.5} = \sqrt{1.25} = 1.118$$ — positive correlation must *add* risk, not remove it.
- D ($500,000) averages the ULs — no statistical basis.

📌 Exam tip: ELs add linearly (no correlation needed); ULs do NOT. Portfolio UL uses the variance-covariance form and lies between the larger single UL and the simple sum, depending on ρ.

**Q6.** In the Merton model, a firm's assets are worth V = $120 million today, with asset volatility σ_V = 25% and expected asset return μ = 10%. Debt with face value D = $100 million matures in T = 1 year. Using $$DD = \dfrac{\ln(V/D) + (\mu - \sigma_V^2/2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt{T}}$$, the distance to default is closest to:

- A. 0.73
- B. 1.00
- C. 1.30
- D. 1.96

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the distance to default (DD).

1. $$\ln(V/D) = \ln(120/100) = \ln(1.20) = 0.18232.$$
2. Drift term: $$\mu - \sigma_V^2/2 = 0.10 - 0.25^2/2 = 0.10 - 0.03125 = 0.06875.$$
3. Numerator: $$0.18232 + 0.06875 \times 1 = 0.25107.$$
4. Denominator: $$\sigma_V\sqrt{T} = 0.25 \times \sqrt{1} = 0.25.$$
5. DD: $$\dfrac{0.25107}{0.25} = 1.004 \approx 1.00.$$

Why B is correct: all terms combined with the correct drift adjustment $$(\mu - \sigma_V^2/2).$$

Distractor traps:
- A (0.73) drops the drift term entirely: $$0.18232/0.25 = 0.729$$ — omits $$(\mu - \sigma_V^2/2)T.$$
- C (1.30) adds $$+\sigma_V^2/2$$ instead of subtracting it (wrong sign on the variance correction).
- D (1.96) uses $$\mu$$ without the $$-\sigma_V^2/2$$ adjustment and mis-scales.

📌 Exam tip: The DD numerator subtracts half the variance, $$(\mu - \sigma_V^2/2)$$ — the same lognormal drift correction as in $$d_2$$ of Black-Scholes. Higher DD = safer firm; PD is obtained by mapping DD to historical default rates (KMV/EDF), not via $$N(-DD)$$ alone in practice.

**Q7.** Which statement about the credit loss distribution and economic capital is correct?

- A. The credit loss distribution is symmetric, so VaR and expected loss roughly coincide.
- B. Economic capital equals expected loss plus the 99.9% worst-case loss.
- C. Economic capital equals the 99.9% worst-case loss minus expected loss, because EL is covered by pricing and provisions.
- D. Lowering the asset correlation ρ thickens the loss-distribution tail and raises economic capital.

**Answer: C**

The ask: identify the correct conceptual statement.

Why C is correct: the loss distribution is highly skewed; EL (the mean) is a cost of doing business covered by loan pricing and provisions, while capital absorbs *unexpected* loss — the distance from the 99.9% quantile down to EL. So $$\text{Economic capital} = \text{WCL}_{99.9\%} - EL.$$

Distractor traps:
- A is false — credit loss distributions are strongly right-skewed (most years near EL, rare catastrophic tail from correlated defaults).
- B double-counts EL by adding it instead of subtracting it; capital is *in excess of* EL.
- D reverses the correlation effect — *higher* ρ thickens the tail and raises capital; lower ρ diversifies idiosyncratic risk away.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the partition — EL → provisions/pricing; UL (up to 99.9%) → capital; beyond 99.9% → shareholders'/the bank's failure. Tail thickness is driven up by correlation, concentration (low granularity), and adverse LGD-default correlation.
