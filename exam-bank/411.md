**Q1.** An investor buys a 3-year, 5% annual-coupon bond at par ($100, face $100) and holds it to maturity. Each $5 coupon is reinvested at a flat 3% annual rate until the maturity date. The investor's realized annual compound return on the position is closest to:

- A. 5.00%
- B. 4.91%
- C. 3.00%
- D. 4.65%

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the realized compound return when coupons are reinvested below the purchase YTM (3% vs the 5% par yield), held to maturity.

1. Since the bond is bought at par, YTM = coupon = 5%. Coupons of $5 are paid at t = 1, 2, 3, plus face $100 at t = 3.
2. Grow each reinvested coupon to maturity (t = 3) at 3%:
   - Coupon at t = 1 grows 2 years: $$5 \times (1.03)^2 = 5 \times 1.0609 = 5.3045$$
   - Coupon at t = 2 grows 1 year: $$5 \times 1.03 = 5.1500$$
   - Coupon at t = 3 (no reinvestment): $$5.0000$$
3. Total terminal value = reinvested coupons + face:
   $$5.3045 + 5.1500 + 5.0000 + 100 = 115.4545$$
4. Solve $$100(1+R)^3 = 115.4545$$:
   $$(1+R)^3 = 1.154545 \;\Rightarrow\; 1+R = 1.154545^{1/3}$$
   $$\ln(1.154545) = 0.143726,\quad \div 3 = 0.047909,\quad e^{0.047909} = 1.04907$$
   $$R \approx 4.91\%$$

- A (5.00%) is the trap of assuming realized return = YTM regardless of reinvestment rate — true only if coupons are reinvested AT the YTM.
- C (3.00%) wrongly takes the reinvestment rate itself as the total return, ignoring the par price and principal.
- D (4.65%) overstates the reinvestment drag, e.g., by compounding the shortfall on all three coupons including the final one (which is never reinvested).

📌 Exam tip: Realized compound return equals purchase YTM only when every coupon is reinvested at the YTM and the bond is held to maturity. Reinvestment below YTM drags realized return below YTM.

**Q2.** A 2-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon on $100 face. The benchmark spot curve is z₁ = 4.00% and z₂ = 4.50% (annual compounding). The bond trades at a market price of $101.70. The constant spread s added to each spot rate that reprices the bond is closest to:

- A. −70 bp
- B. +20 bp
- C. +70 bp
- D. +130 bp

**Answer: C**

The ask: find the constant spread s over the spot curve that makes discounted cash flows equal the $101.70 market price.

1. Price on the pure benchmark curve (s = 0). Cash flows: $6 at t = 1, $106 at t = 2.
   $$P_0 = \frac{6}{1.04} + \frac{106}{(1.045)^2} = 5.7692 + \frac{106}{1.092025} = 5.7692 + 97.0673 = 102.836$$
2. Market price ($101.70) is BELOW the benchmark-curve value ($102.836). A lower price requires a HIGHER discount rate, so the spread is positive.
3. Solve for s. Try s = +0.70% (rates 4.70% and 5.20%):
   $$\frac{6}{1.047} + \frac{106}{(1.052)^2} = 5.7307 + \frac{106}{1.106704} = 5.7307 + 95.7800 = 101.51$$
   Slightly low; try s = +0.60% (rates 4.60%, 5.10%):
   $$\frac{6}{1.046} + \frac{106}{(1.051)^2} = 5.7361 + \frac{106}{1.104601} = 5.7361 + 95.9624 = 101.70$$
   So s ≈ +60–70 bp; the closest listed value is **+70 bp**.

- A (−70 bp) gets the sign backward — a bond priced BELOW its curve-fair value has a POSITIVE spread.
- B (+20 bp) underestimates the gap between market ($101.70) and benchmark ($102.84) price.
- D (+130 bp) overshoots; at +130 bp the price would fall well below $101.

📌 Exam tip: Determine the spread's sign first. Market price below the curve-implied value ⟹ positive spread (discount harder to reach the lower price); above ⟹ negative spread. Then iterate.

**Q3.** Two bonds have identical 10-year maturities. Bond X has a 3% annual coupon; Bond Y has an 8% annual coupon. The benchmark spot curve is upward-sloping. Which statement about their yields to maturity is correct?

- A. Both bonds have the same YTM because they have the same maturity.
- B. Bond X (lower coupon) has the higher YTM.
- C. Bond Y (higher coupon) has the higher YTM.
- D. The YTM ordering cannot be determined without the exact spot rates.

**Answer: B**

The ask: rank the YTMs of two same-maturity bonds with different coupons on an upward-sloping curve.

1. YTM is a cash-flow-weighted "complex average" of the spot rates discounting a bond's cash flows. On an upward-sloping curve, early cash flows are discounted at low spot rates and late cash flows at high spot rates.
2. The high-coupon bond (Y) puts relatively more weight on early cash flows (its large coupons), so its YTM averages toward the lower short-end spots → lower YTM.
3. The low-coupon bond (X) puts relatively more weight on the late principal repayment, so its YTM averages toward the higher long-end spots → higher YTM.
4. Therefore Bond X has the higher YTM.

- A is the misconception that maturity alone pins down YTM; YTM is bond-specific when the curve is not flat.
- C reverses the logic — the higher-coupon bond has the LOWER YTM on an upward-sloping curve.
- D is too cautious: the qualitative ordering follows from the upward slope alone.

📌 Exam tip: On an upward-sloping curve, lower-coupon bonds have higher YTMs (weight on the high long-end spot). On a downward-sloping curve the ordering flips. Same maturity ≠ same YTM.

**Q4.** A 4-year, 5% annual-coupon bond ($100 face) is priced to yield 4% (annual). Using the annuity-plus-principal formula, the price is closest to:

- A. $103.63
- B. $96.49
- C. $100.00
- D. $105.45

**Answer: A**

The ask: compute price from YTM using the annuity factor for the coupons plus the discounted principal.

1. Per-period rate = 4%, periods = 4, coupon = $5, face = $100.
2. Discount factor at t = 4: $$(1.04)^4 = 1.169859 \;\Rightarrow\; (1.04)^{-4} = 0.854804$$
3. Annuity factor for coupons:
   $$\frac{1 - 0.854804}{0.04} = \frac{0.145196}{0.04} = 3.629895$$
4. Price:
   $$P = 5 \times 3.629895 + 100 \times 0.854804 = 18.1495 + 85.4804 = \$103.63$$

- B ($96.49) reverses the premium/discount logic (e.g., pricing a 5% coupon at a 6% yield).
- C ($100.00) is the par trap — par requires coupon = yield, but here 5% > 4%, a premium.
- D ($105.45) overstates the premium, typically from using a 3% yield.

📌 Exam tip: Coupon (5%) > yield (4%) ⟹ premium (price > 100). Sanity-check the premium/discount direction before trusting the arithmetic.

**Q5.** An investor holds a coupon bond over a 1-year horizon during which the yield curve is completely unchanged. The investor's realized return over the year consists of which components?

- A. Carry (coupon income) only
- B. Carry plus rolldown
- C. Carry, rolldown, and the impact of the rate change
- D. Rolldown only

**Answer: B**

The ask: decompose 1-year P&L into components when the curve does not move.

1. The standard decomposition: return = carry (coupon income) + rolldown (price change from sliding along an unchanged curve) + impact of curve/rate changes + spread change.
2. The curve is "completely unchanged," so the rate-change and spread-change terms are both zero.
3. What remains: carry (the coupon earned) plus rolldown (price drifts as the bond ages and rolls to a shorter maturity along a static curve).

- A omits rolldown — even with a static curve, an aging bond's price changes as it moves to a different point on the curve.
- C wrongly includes the rate-change impact, the exact term that vanishes when the curve is unchanged.
- D omits coupon income, which is always part of carry.

📌 Exam tip: "Curve unchanged" ⟹ only carry + rolldown survive. The rate-change and spread-change terms are precisely what a static curve zeroes out.

**Q6.** A perpetuity pays $7 per year and is currently priced at $87.50. If required yields fall by 100 bp, the new price and the percentage gain are closest to:

- A. $97.22 and +11.1%
- B. $100.00 and +14.3%
- C. $116.67 and +33.3%
- D. $93.33 and +6.7%

**Answer: B**

The ask: infer the current yield from the price, reprice after a 100 bp drop, and compute the percentage gain.

1. Back out the current yield from $$P = C/y$$:
   $$87.50 = \frac{7}{y} \;\Rightarrow\; y = \frac{7}{87.50} = 0.08 = 8\%$$
2. Drop the yield by 100 bp: new y = 7%.
3. New price:
   $$P_{new} = \frac{7}{0.07} = \$100.00$$
4. Percentage gain:
   $$\frac{100.00 - 87.50}{87.50} = \frac{12.50}{87.50} = 14.3\%$$

- A ($97.22) uses a wrong new yield of 7.2% — $$7/0.072 = 97.22$$ — misreading the 100 bp shift.
- C ($116.67) drops the yield to 6% (a 200 bp move) — $$7/0.06 = 116.67$$.
- D ($93.33) drops the yield only to 7.5% (a 50 bp move) — $$7/0.075 = 93.33$$.

📌 Exam tip: For a perpetuity, invert $$P = C/y$$ to recover the current yield, then reapply at the shifted yield. Perpetuities have extreme rate sensitivity.

**Q7.** A 5-year zero-coupon bond ($100 face) is purchased to yield 6% (annual compounding) and held to maturity. Which statement about the investor's realized return is correct?

- A. The realized compound return depends on the reinvestment rate of intermediate cash flows.
- B. The realized compound return equals 6%, regardless of how rates move over the 5 years.
- C. The realized return equals 6% only if rates stay at 6%.
- D. The realized return is undefined because a zero pays no coupons.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the realized return on a zero-coupon bond held to maturity.

1. A zero has no intermediate coupon cash flows, so there is no reinvestment assumption embedded in its yield.
2. Purchase price $$P_0 = 100/(1.06)^5 = 100/1.338226 = 74.726$$. The held-to-maturity return is locked:
   $$\left(\frac{100}{74.726}\right)^{1/5} - 1 = (1.338226)^{1/5} - 1 = 6.00\%$$
3. With no coupons to reinvest, intervening rate moves do not affect the realized return — it equals the purchase YTM exactly.

- A describes a coupon bond; a zero has no intermediate cash flows to reinvest.
- C wrongly conditions on rates staying flat — irrelevant for a zero held to maturity.
- D is false — the return is well-defined and exactly the YTM.

📌 Exam tip: A zero-coupon bond held to maturity is the one case where realized return = YTM with certainty (no reinvestment risk). This is the exception to "YTM is not a promised return."
