**Q1.** An open-end mutual fund holds securities and cash worth $812 million and owes $32 million in accrued fees and payables, with 39 million shares outstanding. It charges a 5.5% front-end load. An investor places $50,000 today. How many fund shares (rounded to the nearest share) does the investor acquire?

- A. 2,500 shares
- B. 2,363 shares
- C. 2,500 shares before load, 2,363 after
- D. 2,646 shares

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute NAV per share, then the shares bought after the front-end load reduces the amount actually invested.

1. NAV = (Assets − Liabilities) / Shares = ($812M − $32M) / 39M = $780M / 39M = **$20.00 per share**.
2. The 5.5% front-end load is deducted from the $50,000 first: amount invested = $50,000 × (1 − 0.055) = $50,000 × 0.945 = **$47,250**.
3. Shares acquired = $47,250 / $20.00 = **2,362.5 ≈ 2,363 shares**.

Why B is correct: only $47,250 is put to work after the load, buying 2,362.5 ≈ 2,363 shares.

Distractor traps:
- A ignores the load entirely: $50,000 / $20 = 2,500 shares.
- C confuses the framing — the load reduces *dollars invested*, not a "before/after share count"; you never buy 2,500 shares.
- D *adds* the load to the investment ($50,000 × 1.055 / $20 = 2,637.5) or mishandles the percentage, inflating the share count.

📌 Exam tip: A front-end load is subtracted from the gross investment before buying shares: shares = [investment × (1 − load)] / NAV. A 5.5% load means only 94.5% of your money is invested.

**Q2.** A hedge fund begins year 4 with AUM of $200M and a high-water mark (HWM) of $240M, carried over because of prior losses. The fund charges a 2% management fee (on beginning AUM) and a 20% incentive fee on gains above the HWM. The management fee is charged first; the incentive fee applies to the AUM in excess of the HWM measured *after* the management fee. The gross return for year 4 is +30%. The incentive fee is closest to:

- A. $12.0M
- B. $3.2M
- C. $4.0M
- D. $7.2M

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: compute the incentive fee when the 2% management fee is deducted first and the $240M HWM must be exceeded before any incentive fee applies.

1. Management fee = 2% × $200M = $4.0M (charged first).
2. Gross gain = 30% × $200M = $60M, so pre-fee AUM = $260M.
3. AUM after management fee = $260M − $4.0M = $256.0M.
4. Excess over the HWM = $256.0M − $240M = $16.0M (positive, so an incentive fee is owed).
5. Incentive fee = 20% × $16.0M = **$3.2M**.

Why B is correct: only the $16.0M by which post-management AUM ($256M) exceeds the $240M HWM is fee-bearing; 20% of that is $3.2M.

Distractor traps:
- A ($12.0M) ignores the HWM entirely: 20% × the full $60M gross gain.
- C ($4.0M) ignores the management-fee deduction: 20% × ($260M − $240M) = 20% × $20M.
- D ($7.2M) adds the management fee to the incentive fee ($4.0M + $3.2M) — that is *total* fees, not the incentive fee asked for.

📌 Exam tip: With a high-water mark, the incentive fee applies only to AUM *above the HWM*. If the structure deducts the management fee first, net it out before measuring the excess over the HWM. Read carefully whether the question wants the incentive fee alone or total fees.

**Q3.** A closed-end fund holds investments worth $480M against $30M of liabilities, with 25M shares outstanding. Its shares trade on the exchange at $16.20. The fund is trading at:

- A. A 10% discount to its $18.00 NAV
- B. A 10% premium to its $18.00 NAV
- C. NAV exactly, by construction
- D. A 16% discount to its $19.20 NAV

**Answer: A**

Restate the ask: compute NAV, then express the market price relative to NAV as a premium or discount.

1. NAV = (Assets − Liabilities) / Shares = ($480M − $30M) / 25M = $450M / 25M = **$18.00**.
2. Price / NAV = $16.20 / $18.00 = 0.90 = 90%.
3. Trading at 90% of NAV is a **10% discount** — typical of closed-end funds.

Why A is correct: $16.20 is 90% of the $18.00 NAV, i.e., a 10% discount.

Distractor traps:
- B reverses sign: a price below NAV is a discount, not a premium.
- C describes *open-end* funds (always transact at NAV); closed-end funds trade at market prices that deviate from NAV.
- D miscomputes NAV (forgets to subtract liabilities: $480M/25M = $19.20) and the percentage.

📌 Exam tip: Closed-end funds trade on an exchange and usually at a *discount* to NAV. Price < NAV = discount; Price > NAV = premium. Only open-end funds and (via arbitrage) ETFs stay at/near NAV.

**Q4.** A trader at a brokerage learns that a large mutual fund will submit a sizable buy order for a thinly traded stock this afternoon, and buys the stock for his own account beforehand, intending to sell into the fund's order. Separately, an investor consistently places fund orders at 5:30 p.m. but is granted that day's 4:00 p.m. NAV. These two practices are, respectively:

- A. Market timing; directed brokerage
- B. Front running; late trading
- C. Late trading; front running
- D. Front running; market timing

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: classify two distinct abusive practices.

1. Trading one's own account *ahead of* a known large client/fund order to profit from the price impact is **front running** (illegal).
2. Placing an order *after* the 4:00 p.m. cutoff yet receiving that day's NAV — exploiting post-close information — is **late trading** (illegal).

Why B is correct: the first is front running; the second is late trading.

Distractor traps:
- A mislabels both: market timing exploits *stale prices* in the NAV (and trades before the cutoff); directed brokerage is a kickback for pushing fund shares.
- C swaps the two labels.
- D correctly identifies front running but calls the late-trade scenario market timing — market timing happens *before* the cutoff and exploits stale prices, not post-close news.

📌 Exam tip: Late trading = orders *after* 4 p.m. getting today's NAV (post-close info). Market timing = rapid in/out *before* the cutoff exploiting stale prices. Front running = trading ahead of a known order. Directed brokerage = kickback for selling the fund.

**Q5.** A hedge fund database reports an average annual return of 14% for its constituent funds. An analyst notes that (i) funds that failed and shut down were removed from the database, and (ii) when a fund first begins reporting, its prior favorable track record is added to the database. The two biases described, in order, are:

- A. Backfill bias; survivorship bias
- B. Survivorship bias; backfill bias
- C. Measurement bias; survivorship bias
- D. Survivorship bias; look-ahead bias

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: match each described data problem to the correct named bias.

1. Removing failed/dead funds so only winners remain in the average is **survivorship bias** (overstates returns).
2. Importing a fund's self-selected, favorable *pre-entry* history when it starts reporting is **backfill bias** (overstates returns).

Why B is correct: (i) is survivorship, (ii) is backfill, in that order.

Distractor traps:
- A reverses the order.
- C calls (i) measurement bias — but measurement bias is the *smoothing* of illiquid, manager-marked valuations, understating volatility/correlation, not the dropout of dead funds.
- D mislabels (ii) as look-ahead bias; the textbook term for importing pre-entry history is backfill (a.k.a. instant-history) bias.

📌 Exam tip: Survivorship = losers drop out. Backfill (instant history) = winners' past returns are added on entry. Measurement/smoothing = stale, manager marks on illiquid assets understate risk. All three *inflate* reported hedge-fund performance.

**Q6.** Which statement most accurately distinguishes an ETF from an open-end mutual fund and a closed-end fund?

- A. Like a closed-end fund, an ETF has a fixed share count after its IPO, which is why it can trade at large discounts to NAV.
- B. An ETF trades intraday on an exchange and, unlike a closed-end fund, stays very close to NAV because authorized participants can create/redeem shares in large blocks, arbitraging away deviations.
- C. An ETF can be bought and sold only once per day at the 4 p.m. NAV, exactly like an open-end fund.
- D. An ETF discloses holdings only quarterly, less frequently than an open-end mutual fund.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify the defining ETF features versus the other two fund types.

1. ETFs trade **intraday on an exchange** (like closed-end funds, unlike open-end funds).
2. Their share count is **variable**: large institutions (authorized participants) **create/redeem** shares in big blocks. This creation/redemption **arbitrage** keeps the market price very close to NAV — unlike closed-end funds, which can trade at persistent discounts/premiums.
3. ETFs typically disclose holdings **daily**.

Why B is correct: intraday trading + creation/redemption arbitrage keeping price near NAV is the ETF's hallmark.

Distractor traps:
- A wrongly gives ETFs a fixed share count (that is the closed-end feature) and predicts large discounts — the arbitrage prevents them.
- C describes the *open-end* once-daily-NAV mechanism; ETFs trade intraday.
- D reverses disclosure frequency — ETFs typically disclose *daily*, more often than mutual funds.

📌 Exam tip: ETF = intraday exchange trading + variable shares via create/redeem arbitrage → price hugs NAV, daily holdings disclosure. Closed-end = fixed shares → can trade at a discount/premium. Open-end = once-daily at NAV.

**Q7.** A prime broker to a hedge fund provides which of the following, and is positioned to do what?

- A. Sets the fund's incentive fee and high-water mark on behalf of investors.
- B. Provides trade clearing/settlement, custody, margin financing (leverage), and securities lending for shorts — and, holding the fund's collateral and monitoring positions, is often the first to see the fund in distress.
- C. Guarantees the fund's returns to investors in exchange for a portion of the management fee.
- D. Acts as the central counterparty for all the fund's OTC trades, eliminating counterparty risk.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify the prime broker's services and informational position.

1. Prime brokers (large banks) provide: trade **clearing and settlement**, **custody**, **margin financing** (leverage), **securities lending** for short sales, and **capital introduction**.
2. Because the prime broker holds collateral and monitors the fund's positions, it is **often the first to detect distress**.

Why B is correct: it lists the standard services and the early-warning position.

Distractor traps:
- A confuses the prime broker with the fund's own fee structure/governance.
- C invents a return guarantee — prime brokers do not guarantee fund performance.
- D overstates the role: a prime broker is not a CCP and does not eliminate counterparty risk (it manages its own exposure via collateral).

📌 Exam tip: Prime broker = clearing, custody, margin/leverage, securities lending, capital introduction. Its collateral-holding/monitoring role makes it the first to spot a fund unraveling.
