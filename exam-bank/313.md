**Q1.** A 6-month European call on a non-dividend stock trades at $5.00. The stock is at $62, the strike is $60, and the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 4%. By put-call parity, the price of the 6-month European put with the same strike is *closest* to:

- A. $1.81
- B. $3.00
- C. $4.19
- D. $7.19

**Answer: A**

We apply put-call parity for European options on a non-dividend stock to solve for $p$.

1. Parity: $$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0 \;\Rightarrow\; p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0$$
2. Discount the strike: $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.04\times0.5} = e^{-0.02} = 0.980199$.
$$Ke^{-rT} = 60 \times 0.980199 = 58.812$$
3. Plug in: $$p = 5.00 + 58.812 - 62.00 = 1.812 \approx \$1.81$$

- B ($3.00) ignores discounting, using $p = c + K - S_0 = 5 + 60 - 62 = 3$ — the trap of forgetting to PV the strike.
- C ($4.19) flips the discount sign or adds $S_0$ to the wrong side.
- D ($7.19) computes $c + S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ — rearranging parity incorrectly.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the layout $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$: "call + PV(strike) = put + stock." Always discount $K$. Parity holds for **European** options only.

**Q2.** A 3-month European put on a non-dividend stock has $S_0 = \$44$, $K = \$48$, and $r = 6\%$ continuous. The theoretical lower bound on the put price is *closest* to:

- A. $0.00
- B. $3.29
- C. $4.00
- D. $4.72

**Answer: B**

We compute the European put lower bound on a non-dividend stock.

1. Lower bound: $$p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\, 0)$$
2. Discount the strike: $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.06\times0.25} = e^{-0.015} = 0.985112$.
$$Ke^{-rT} = 48 \times 0.985112 = 47.285$$
3. Bound: $$\max(47.285 - 44, 0) = \max(3.285, 0) = \$3.29$$

- A ($0.00) wrongly concludes the bound is zero (it is positive here because $Ke^{-rT} > S_0$).
- C ($4.00) uses the **undiscounted** intrinsic-style value $K - S_0 = 48 - 44 = 4$, forgetting to PV the strike.
- D ($4.72) discounts $S_0$ instead of $K$, or adds rather than subtracts.

📌 Exam tip: The European **put** lower bound discounts the strike: $Ke^{-rT} - S_0$. The European **call** lower bound is $S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. The undiscounted $K - S_0$ is a distractor designed to look like intrinsic value.

**Q3.** Consider European options on a non-dividend stock: $c = \$4.00$, $p = \$1.00$, $S_0 = \$48$, $K = \$45$, $r = 8\%$ continuous, $T = 0.5$. An arbitrageur sets up the correct riskless trade. The arbitrage profit at expiration is *closest* to:

- A. $1.84
- B. $1.76
- C. $3.00
- D. $0.00

**Answer: A**

We check parity, identify the rich side, build the trade, and compute the certain profit at $T$.

1. Parity check, $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.04} = 0.960789$, so $Ke^{-rT} = 45 \times 0.960789 = 43.236$.
   - Call side: $c + Ke^{-rT} = 4.00 + 43.236 = 47.236$.
   - Put side: $p + S_0 = 1.00 + 48.00 = 49.00$.
2. The **put-plus-stock side is overpriced** ($49.00 > 47.236$). Buy the cheap side, sell the rich side: **buy the call, short the put, short the stock**.
3. Net cash inflow at $t=0$: $-4.00 + 1.00 + 48.00 = +\$45.00$.
4. Invest at $r$ for 6 months, $e^{0.04} = 1.040811$: $$45.00 \times 1.040811 = 46.836$$
5. At $T$ you pay exactly $K = \$45$ to acquire the stock (via the call if $S_T > 45$, or via assignment on the short put if $S_T < 45$) and close the short.
6. Riskless profit: $$46.836 - 45.00 = \$1.836 \approx \$1.84$$

- B ($1.76) discounts with simple interest or uses the wrong $e$ factor.
- C ($3.00) is the static parity gap undiscounted ($49 - 45 - 1$) — ignoring the time value of the invested inflow.
- D ($0.00) wrongly concludes parity holds.

📌 Exam tip: For parity arbitrage: (1) compute both sides, (2) **buy low/sell high** at $t=0$, (3) invest the net inflow at $r$, (4) note you pay exactly $K$ at $T$ in both branches, (5) profit = FV(inflow) − $K$.

**Q4.** Which change, all else equal, *increases* the value of a **European call** while *decreasing* the value of a **European put** on a non-dividend stock?

- A. A higher volatility $\sigma$
- B. A higher risk-free rate $r$
- C. A higher strike price $K$
- D. A higher expected dividend $D$

**Answer: B**

We use the six-factor sign table and find the factor whose call effect is $+$ and put effect is $-$.

1. Higher **risk-free rate $r$**: raises the expected growth rate of the stock and lowers the PV of the strike — both help the call ($+$) and both hurt the put ($-$). This matches the requirement.

- A (higher $\sigma$): raises **both** the call and the put — fails the "decrease put" condition.
- C (higher $K$): **lowers** the call and **raises** the put — opposite signs to what is asked.
- D (higher $D$): **lowers** the call and **raises** the put — opposite to the requirement.

📌 Exam tip: Only **two** factors split calls vs puts in opposite directions: $S_0$ (call $+$, put $-$) and **$r$** (call $+$, put $-$). Volatility raises both; $K$ and $D$ favor the put.

**Q5.** An investor holds an American call on a non-dividend-paying stock that is now deep in the money with three months remaining. The investor no longer wants exposure to the stock. The *best* course of action is to:

- A. Exercise the call immediately to lock in the intrinsic value
- B. Sell the call in the market
- C. Exercise the call and simultaneously short the stock
- D. Hold the call to expiration no matter what

**Answer: B**

This tests the no-early-exercise result for American calls on non-dividend stocks.

1. The call is always worth more **alive** than exercised: $$C \ge c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} > S_0 - K$$ where $S_0 - K$ is the exercise (intrinsic) value.
2. Selling captures the full market price = intrinsic **plus remaining time value**; exercising captures only intrinsic value and forfeits interest on $K$ and the insurance value. So **selling dominates exercising**.

- A throws away time value and the time value of $K$.
- C exercises (capturing only intrinsic) and adds a needless short — strictly worse than selling.
- D may be fine if the investor wanted exposure, but they do not; selling now realizes value immediately at no cost.

📌 Exam tip: **Never exercise an American call on a non-dividend stock early** — sell it instead. The only early-exercise case for calls is **just before an ex-dividend date** when the captured dividend exceeds the forgone time value and interest.

**Q6.** For an American call and put on a **dividend-paying** stock with present-value dividends $D$, which relationship is the *correct* bound on $C - P$?

- A. $C - P = S_0 + D - Ke^{-rT}$
- B. $S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$
- C. $C - P = S_0 - Ke^{-rT} - D$
- D. $C - P \le S_0 - D - K$

**Answer: B**

This tests the put-call inequality for American options (parity itself does not hold for American options).

1. For European options parity is an equality, but for **American** options on a dividend-paying stock only an inequality bounds $C - P$:
$$S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$$

- A and C apply the European **equality** to American options — the classic trap (you may only *bound* $C - P$ for American options).
- D gives only a one-sided (and mis-signed) relation, dropping the upper bound and the lower-bound structure.

📌 Exam tip: Put-call **parity (equality)** = European only. For **American** options you get a two-sided **inequality**: $S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. Set $D=0$ for the non-dividend case.

**Q7.** A 1-year European call on a non-dividend stock trades at $3.00 with $S_0 = \$20$, $K = \$18$, and $r = 10\%$ continuous. The arbitrage available and its minimum riskless profit are *best* described as:

- A. Call overpriced; sell the call, buy the stock; profit ≈ $0.71
- B. Call underpriced (below its lower bound); buy the call, short the stock; profit ≈ $0.79
- C. No arbitrage; the call equals its lower bound
- D. Call underpriced; buy the call, buy the stock; profit ≈ $3.71

**Answer: B**

We compare the call price to its lower bound, and if violated build the arbitrage and compute the guaranteed minimum profit.

1. Lower bound: $S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$, with $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.10} = 0.904837$.
$$Ke^{-rT} = 18 \times 0.904837 = 16.287$$
$$\text{bound} = 20 - 16.287 = 3.713$$
2. Market call $3.00 < 3.713$ → the call is **below its lower bound**, i.e., too cheap.
3. Trade: **buy the call, short the stock**. Inflow at $t=0$: $20 - 3 = \$17$.
4. Invest at $r$, $e^{0.10} = 1.105171$: $$17 \times 1.105171 = 18.788$$
5. At $T$: if $S_T > 18$, exercise — pay $18 to buy back the stock; profit $= 18.788 - 18 = \$0.788$. If $S_T \le 18$, let the call lapse and buy the stock at $S_T \le 18$; profit $\ge 0.788$.
6. Minimum riskless profit $\approx \$0.79$.

- A reverses the direction (the call is under-, not over-priced).
- C is wrong: $3.00 \ne 3.713$, so the bound is violated.
- D has the right diagnosis but the wrong trade (you short, not buy, the stock) and an inflated profit.

📌 Exam tip: A call below $S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ is too cheap → **buy the call, short the stock**, invest the inflow, and you net at least FV(inflow) − $K$. Always verify both terminal branches give a profit at least as large.

**Q8.** Regarding the effect of time to expiration $T$ on option value, which statement is *most* accurate?

- A. Both European and American options always increase in value as $T$ rises.
- B. American options never decrease in value as $T$ rises, but the effect of $T$ on European options is ambiguous.
- C. European options always increase in value as $T$ rises, but American options are ambiguous.
- D. Time to expiration has no effect on a deep-in-the-money option.

**Answer: B**

This tests the sign of the time-to-expiration factor across option styles.

1. A longer-dated **American** option contains every right of the shorter one, so it can never be worth less — $T$ has a non-negative effect (the table shows $+$ for American calls and puts).
2. For **European** options the effect is **ambiguous**: a dividend between two maturities can make a shorter-dated European call worth more, and a deep-ITM European put may prefer a shorter wait for its payoff.

- A is wrong because European options are ambiguous in $T$.
- C reverses which style is ambiguous.
- D is false: time value affects option value at all moneyness levels (and the deep-ITM European put example shows $T$ can even reduce value).

📌 Exam tip: $T$ is the one factor where European and American differ in *certainty*: **American = always $+$** (longer = superset of rights); **European = ambiguous** (dividends and deep-ITM puts can reverse it).
