**Q1.** A trader writes 10 naked call contracts with strike $K = \$70$ when the stock trades at $S = \$66$. The call premium is $1.80 per share. Under the standard exchange margin rules, the required margin is *closest* to:

- A. $7,200
- B. $9,800
- C. $13,200
- D. $14,200

**Answer: B**

We write **naked calls** and must take the greater of the two standard per-share margin formulas, then scale by 100 shares and 10 contracts. The call is **out of the money** ($S = \$66 < K = \$70$), OTM by $K - S = \$4$.

1. **Formula 1**: premium + 20% of stock price − amount OTM.
$$1.80 + 0.20(66) - 4.00 = 1.80 + 13.20 - 4.00 = 11.00$$
2. **Formula 2** (naked call → 10% of stock price): $$1.80 + 0.10(66) = 1.80 + 6.60 = 8.40$$
3. Greater of the two: $\max(11.00, 8.40) = 11.00$ per share.
4. Scale: $$11.00 \times 100 \times 10 = \$11,000$$

The required margin is **$11,000**. (The closest listed value reflecting the correct method is B; the exact figure is $11,000 — see tip.)

Correcting the keyed choice to the exact arithmetic, B should read $11,000. Distractors:
- A ($7,200) uses only Formula 2 logic but with $0.10 \times 66 = 6.60 → 8.40 \times 100 \times$ a wrong contract count.
- C ($13,200) forgets to subtract the $4 OTM amount in Formula 1: $1.80 + 13.20 = 15.00 → 15.00 \times$ wrong scale.
- D ($14,200) uses 10% of the **strike** (a put rule) instead of the stock price.

📌 Exam tip: For a **naked call** Formula 2 uses 10% of the **stock price**; for a **naked put** it uses 10% of the **strike**. Always subtract the OTM amount in Formula 1, then take the maximum, then multiply by 100 × contracts.

**Q2.** On June 1 you buy 4 put contracts on a stock, strike $K = \$80$, premium $4.20 per share. On June 15 the company executes a 5-for-4 stock dividend (equivalent to a 5-for-4 split). After the adjustment, and given the stock finishes at $S_T = \$50$ per (post-adjustment) share at expiration, your total payoff (ignoring premium) is *closest* to:

- A. $0
- B. $5,000
- C. $7,000
- D. $12,000

**Answer: C**

A 25% stock dividend is a 5-for-4 split. We must adjust the strike and share count, then compute the payoff on the post-adjustment terms.

1. For an n-for-m split with n = 5, m = 4: strike is multiplied by $m/n = 4/5$ and shares per contract by $n/m = 5/4$.
2. New strike: $$80 \times \tfrac{4}{5} = \$64$$
3. New shares per contract: $$100 \times \tfrac{5}{4} = 125 \text{ shares}$$
4. Put payoff per share at $S_T = \$50$: $$\max(64 - 50, 0) = \$14$$
5. Total over 4 contracts × 125 shares: $$14 \times 125 \times 4 = 14 \times 500 = \$7,000$$

- A ($0) wrongly assumes a cash-dividend rule (no adjustment) and uses old $K=80$ vs $S_T=50$... which actually pays — so it is just wrong.
- B ($5,000) uses the new strike $64 but forgets to raise the share count to 125 (uses 100): $14 \times 100 \times 4 = 5,600$ then mis-rounds, or uses $10 \times 500$.
- D ($12,000) forgets to lower the strike, using $K=80$: $(80-50)\times 100 \times 4 = 12,000$.

📌 Exam tip: For an n-for-m split, **strike × m/n** and **shares × n/m** — the economics are preserved, so check that (new strike × new shares) ≈ (old strike × old shares): $64 × 125 = 8,000 = 80 × 100$. ✓

**Q3.** A stock pays a $1.50 ordinary cash dividend on its ex-date. An investor holds a long exchange-traded call with $K = \$45$ on 100 shares. Immediately after the ex-date, the option's contractual terms are:

- A. Strike reduced to $43.50; still 100 shares
- B. Strike reduced to $43.50; shares raised to 103.33
- C. Unchanged; the call holder simply bears the stock's ex-date price drop
- D. Strike raised to $46.50 to compensate the call holder

**Answer: C**

This tests the corporate-action adjustment rules: ordinary cash dividends do **not** adjust option terms.

1. Exchange-traded options are adjusted for **stock splits and stock dividends**, not for **ordinary cash dividends**.
2. On the ex-date the stock price falls by roughly the dividend, and the call holder simply bears it — the expected dividend was already priced into the premium when the option was bought.

- A and B apply a "strike reduced by the dividend" rule that does not exist for ordinary cash dividends (that is a discretionary adjustment reserved for special/large distributions).
- D invents a strike increase that benefits the holder — no such rule.

📌 Exam tip: Cash dividend → **no adjustment** (stock drops, holder bears it). Split/stock dividend → adjust strike and share count. Only **special/large** cash distributions trigger discretionary OCC adjustments.

**Q4.** An investor buys a call for $5.50 and simultaneously the same-strike same-maturity put for $3.00, both with $K = \$100$ on a non-dividend stock (this is a long straddle, but consider only the call leg's properties). For the call alone, when the stock is at $S = \$103$, the time value of the call is *closest* to:

- A. $0
- B. $2.50
- C. $3.00
- D. $5.50

**Answer: B**

We must separate the call premium into intrinsic value and time value at the current stock price.

1. Intrinsic value of a call = $\max(S - K, 0) = \max(103 - 100, 0) = \$3.00$.
2. Time value = premium − intrinsic value: $$5.50 - 3.00 = \$2.50$$

- A ($0) wrongly assumes an ATM/OTM call (no time value) — but the call here is ITM with leftover time value.
- C ($3.00) reports the **intrinsic** value, not the time value (the classic swap).
- D ($5.50) reports the full premium, ignoring that part of it is intrinsic.

📌 Exam tip: Premium = intrinsic + time value. Compute intrinsic first ($\max(S-K,0)$ for a call), then subtract from the premium. Time value is largest **at the money** and shrinks deep ITM/OTM.

**Q5.** Which statement about exchange-traded options, the OCC, and early exercise is *most* accurate?

- A. Exercising an in-the-money American call on a non-dividend stock is generally better than selling it, because exercise captures intrinsic value immediately.
- B. The OCC becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, guaranteeing performance, and on exercise assigns a short-position member firm.
- C. Option buyers may always buy any option on 50% margin because options embed leverage.
- D. A covered call writer must post the same naked-writer margin as an uncovered call writer.

**Answer: B**

This tests the role of the OCC, exercise/assignment mechanics, and margin rules.

1. The **OCC** is the central counterparty: it becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, guarantees performance, nets positions, and on exercise selects a short-position member firm, which assigns a customer (random or FIFO). Choice B is correct.

- A is wrong: selling an ITM American call captures intrinsic value **plus remaining time value**, while exercise captures only intrinsic value — selling is better. Early exercise of a call on a non-dividend stock is never optimal.
- C is wrong: buyers pay the premium **in full**; only options with maturity over **9 months** may be bought on 50% margin.
- D is wrong: a covered call (long stock + short call) needs **no margin** beyond the stock — the stock collateralizes the obligation.

📌 Exam tip: The OCC = central counterparty (novation + guarantee + assignment). Buyers pay premium in full (margin only if maturity > 9 months). Covered calls need no extra margin.

**Q6.** You buy 1 put contract (100 shares), $K = \$50$, premium $3.50 per share. The stock finishes at $S_T = \$42$. Which of the following correctly states your profit per contract, breakeven, and maximum possible loss?

- A. Profit $450; breakeven $46.50; max loss $350
- B. Profit $800; breakeven $53.50; max loss unlimited
- C. Profit $450; breakeven $53.50; max loss $350
- D. Profit $350; breakeven $46.50; max loss $4,650

**Answer: A**

We must compute the long put's profit at expiration, its breakeven, and its maximum loss.

1. Payoff per share = $\max(K - S_T, 0) = \max(50 - 42, 0) = \$8.00$.
2. Profit per share = payoff − premium = $8.00 - 3.50 = \$4.50$; per contract: $4.50 \times 100 = \$450$.
3. Breakeven (long put): $S_T = K - p = 50 - 3.50 = \$46.50$.
4. Maximum loss = the premium paid = $3.50 \times 100 = \$350$ (occurs if $S_T \ge K$ and the put expires worthless).

- B uses the payoff ($800) as profit and a call-style breakeven ($K + p$); a long put's loss is capped, not unlimited.
- C has the right profit and loss but a call-style breakeven $K + p = 53.50$ instead of $K - p$.
- D mislabels the gross payoff as $350 and treats max loss as $K - p$ per share × 100.

📌 Exam tip: Long put breakeven = $K - p$ (the price must fall *below* the strike by the premium). Max loss for any long option = the premium. Profit = payoff − premium; do not report the payoff as the profit.

**Q7.** A market maker quotes a call at a bid of $4.10 and an ask of $4.40. A customer buys 6 contracts at the ask and later sells all 6 at the (unchanged) bid via an offsetting order, paying $0.05 per share in commissions on each side. The customer's total round-trip cost (spread plus commissions) is *closest* to:

- A. $180
- B. $240
- C. $360
- D. $420

**Answer: B**

We must compute the cost of crossing the bid-ask spread plus commissions, scaled to 6 contracts (100 shares each).

1. Spread cost per share = ask − bid = $4.40 - 4.10 = \$0.30$.
2. Commissions per share = $0.05 buy + $0.05 sell = $0.10$.
3. Total cost per share = $0.30 + 0.10 = \$0.40$.
4. Scale: $0.40 \times 100 \times 6 = 0.40 \times 600 = \$240$.

- A ($180) counts only the spread ($0.30 \times 600$), omitting commissions.
- C ($360) double-counts the spread (charges $0.30 on both the buy and the sell) plus omits handling commissions correctly.
- D ($420) adds the full ask and bid incorrectly or applies commission per contract not per share.

📌 Exam tip: A round trip pays the spread **once** (buy at ask, sell at bid) plus commissions **both ways**. Per-share cost = spread + total commissions, then × 100 × contracts.

**Q8.** An employee receives company stock options (ESOs) as compensation. Compared with exchange-traded call options on the same stock, the ESOs *most likely*:

- A. Are freely transferable and cleared by the OCC
- B. Create new shares on exercise, diluting existing shareholders, and are nontransferable with a vesting period
- C. Have unlimited supply determined by open interest
- D. Adjust their strike for ordinary cash dividends but not for splits

**Answer: B**

This contrasts employee stock options (and warrants/convertibles) with exchange-traded options.

1. ESOs are **issued by the company itself**; exercise causes the company to issue **new shares**, diluting existing shareholders.
2. ESOs are typically **nontransferable** and subject to a **vesting** period, unlike standardized, liquid exchange-traded options.

- A is wrong: ESOs are nontransferable and are not OCC-cleared instruments.
- C describes exchange-traded options (supply effectively unlimited, tracked by open interest); ESOs are fixed at grant.
- D inverts the adjustment rules and applies them to an instrument whose terms are set in the grant contract.

📌 Exam tip: Company-issued equity claims (warrants, ESOs, convertibles) → **new shares + dilution**, custom/nontransferable terms. Exchange-traded options → written by investors, OCC-cleared, deliver existing shares, no dilution.
