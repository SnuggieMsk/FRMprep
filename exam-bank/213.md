**Q1.** A Monte Carlo simulation of a credit portfolio's annual loss uses 4,900 trials and reports a mean loss of $\$8.0$M with an across-trial standard deviation of $\$28$M. The risk team needs the standard error of the mean loss reduced to $\$0.10$M. The number of trials required is closest to:

- A. 19,600
- B. 78,400
- C. 156,800
- D. 160,000

**Answer: B**

We compute the current standard error, the required reduction factor, and scale trials by its square.

1. Current standard error: $$SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{28}{\sqrt{4{,}900}} = \frac{28}{70} = \$0.40\text{M}.$$
2. Target $SE = \$0.10$M. The SE must fall by a factor of $$\frac{0.40}{0.10} = 4.$$
3. Because $SE \propto 1/\sqrt{N}$, reducing SE by a factor of 4 requires multiplying $N$ by $4^2 = 16$.
4. Required trials: $$N = 16 \times 4{,}900 = 78{,}400.$$

B is correct. Distractor A ($4 \times 4{,}900 = 19{,}600$) scales trials linearly with the SE-reduction factor instead of by its square — the most common error. Distractor D (160,000) comes from squaring the SE-reduction factor but applying it to a wrongly assumed round base of 10,000 trials ($16 \times 10{,}000$) instead of the actual 4,900. Distractor C (156,800) doubles the correct answer ($2 \times 78{,}400$), a stray factor-of-2 slip. 

📌 Exam tip: Monte Carlo precision improves only at rate $\sqrt{N}$ — to cut the SE by a factor $k$, you need $k^2$ times as many trials. "10× more accuracy needs 100× more trials." Always compute the current SE first.

**Q2.** An analyst estimates a quantity by Monte Carlo. Independent sampling gives a per-trial variance of $\sigma^2 = 900$. She switches to antithetic variates with a correlation of $\rho = -0.7$ between paired estimates. The variance of the average of one antithetic *pair* is closest to:

- A. 450
- B. 135
- C. 765
- D. 270

**Answer: B**

We apply the variance formula for the average of two antithetic draws.

1. Formula for the variance of the average of two paired estimates: $$\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{\hat\theta_1 + \hat\theta_2}{2}\right) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1 + \rho).$$
2. Plug in $\sigma^2 = 900$ and $\rho = -0.7$: $$\frac{900}{2}(1 + (-0.7)) = 450 \times 0.3.$$
3. Compute: $$= 135.$$

B is correct. Distractor A (450) is the variance under *independent* pairing ($\rho = 0$): $\frac{900}{2}(1+0) = 450$ — it ignores the negative correlation benefit. Distractor C (765) uses $(1 + 0.7)$ with a sign error on $\rho$ ($450 \times 1.7 = 765$), which would *increase* variance — the opposite of what antithetic variates do. Distractor D (270) uses $\frac{900}{2}(1-0.4)$ or a similar partial-credit miscalculation.

📌 Exam tip: Antithetic variates work *because* $\rho < 0$: the factor $(1+\rho)$ is below 1, shrinking variance, and you reuse the random numbers (halving generation cost). A sign slip on $\rho$ flips the conclusion entirely — antithetic pairing should always *reduce* variance.

**Q3.** A risk manager must choose between Monte Carlo simulation (assuming normally distributed returns) and historical bootstrapping to estimate the 99% 1-day VaR of a portfolio whose returns exhibit pronounced fat tails. Which statement is the most accurate comparison?

- A. Monte Carlo is superior because it can generate losses worse than any in the historical sample, while the bootstrap cannot.
- B. The bootstrap requires no distributional assumption and captures the observed fat tails directly, but it cannot produce a loss worse than the worst observation in the sample.
- C. The bootstrap eliminates sampling error, whereas Monte Carlo does not.
- D. Both methods are equivalent here because both ultimately resample from the same data.

**Answer: B**

The question contrasts the core strengths and weaknesses of the two simulation approaches.

1. **Bootstrap** resamples *with replacement from observed data* — it makes **no distributional assumption** and automatically reflects the empirical fat tails and skew. This avoids the model risk of a misspecified DGP.
2. Its key limitation: it can **never generate a loss worse than the worst observed value**, because every resampled point is an actual historical observation. This is a real problem for extreme-quantile (tail) risk.
3. Normal-based Monte Carlo, by contrast, *assumes* a distribution (here normal), which would *understate* fat-tailed risk — its weakness is model misspecification, not tail extrapolation.

B is correct. A reverses the tail-extrapolation property: it is *Monte Carlo* (drawing from a parametric model) that can generate losses beyond the historical sample; the bootstrap cannot. C is false — both methods carry sampling error; nothing eliminates it. D is wrong: Monte Carlo draws from an *assumed model*, not from the observed data, so they are not equivalent.

📌 Exam tip: MC draws from an *assumed model* (weakness = model misspecification, but it *can* extrapolate beyond data). Bootstrap draws from *observed data* (weakness = cannot exceed the worst observation, and needs a representative sample). GARP asks this contrast almost verbatim.

**Q4.** A risk team applies an iid (single-observation) bootstrap to a series of daily returns that displays strong volatility clustering, to estimate a 10-day VaR. Which statement best identifies the problem and the fix?

- A. There is no problem; the iid bootstrap preserves all dependence structure automatically.
- B. The iid bootstrap destroys the autocorrelation/volatility clustering; a circular block bootstrap should be used to preserve time-series dependence.
- C. The iid bootstrap overstates risk because it double-counts clustered losses; switch to Monte Carlo.
- D. The problem is the seed; fixing the seed will restore the clustering.

**Answer: B**

The question tests when the bootstrap fails and the appropriate remedy.

1. Volatility clustering is a form of **time-series dependence**: high-volatility days tend to follow high-volatility days.
2. The **iid bootstrap** draws single observations independently with replacement, which **shuffles the data and destroys** any autocorrelation or clustering — leading to misestimated multi-day risk.
3. The fix is the **circular block bootstrap**: draw consecutive *blocks* of observations (wrapping around the end of the series), which preserves the dependence structure within each block.

B is correct. A is false — iid resampling specifically *breaks* dependence. C invents an "overstatement/double-count" mechanism that does not exist; the issue is destroyed structure, and switching to Monte Carlo would require assuming a model (defeating the bootstrap's purpose). D confuses the seed (which only controls reproducibility of the random sequence) with the resampling scheme — fixing the seed does nothing to preserve clustering.

📌 Exam tip: For dependent / clustered time series, use a *block* bootstrap, not the iid bootstrap. Memorize the bootstrap failure modes: structural breaks/non-stationarity, naive handling of dependence, and inability to extrapolate beyond observed extremes.

**Q5.** Regarding pseudo-random number generation and seeds in simulation, which statement is correct?

- A. Pseudo-random sequences are truly random and cannot be reproduced.
- B. Setting the seed reproduces the identical sequence of draws, enabling auditability and fair comparison of competing models on identical shocks.
- C. Fixing the seed eliminates Monte Carlo sampling error.
- D. A fixed seed is required for the standard error formula $SE = s/\sqrt{N}$ to hold.

**Answer: B**

The question tests the role of seeds in pseudo-random generation.

1. Computers generate **pseudo-random** numbers via deterministic algorithms that pass statistical tests for randomness — they are not truly random.
2. The **seed** sets the algorithm's starting state. The *same seed reproduces the identical sequence* of draws.
3. Benefits: **reproducibility** (auditing/debugging) and **fair comparison** — two competing models can be run on the *same* set of shocks, isolating model differences from sampling noise.

B is correct. A is false on two counts: the sequence is pseudo-random (deterministic), and it *is* reproducible. C is false — fixing the seed does not reduce sampling error at all; only more trials do that. The seed just makes the (still error-laden) result reproducible. D is fabricated: the SE formula holds for valid random draws regardless of whether a seed was fixed.

📌 Exam tip: Seeds give *reproducibility*, not *accuracy*. They let you audit results and compare models on identical shocks, but they do nothing to shrink Monte Carlo sampling error — that still requires more trials ($\sqrt{N}$ convergence).

**Q6.** A 10,000-trial Monte Carlo simulation of a portfolio's 1-year P&L produces a mean of $\$5.0$M and an across-trial standard deviation of $\$40$M. The 95% confidence interval for the *true expected* P&L is closest to:

- A. $(\$4.22\text{M},\ \$5.78\text{M})$
- B. $(-\$73.4\text{M},\ \$83.4\text{M})$
- C. $(\$4.61\text{M},\ \$5.39\text{M})$
- D. $(\$4.92\text{M},\ \$5.08\text{M})$

**Answer: A**

We build a confidence interval for the *mean* using the standard error (not the raw standard deviation).

1. Standard error of the mean: $$SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{40}{\sqrt{10{,}000}} = \frac{40}{100} = \$0.40\text{M}.$$
2. 95% CI for the mean: $$\bar X \pm 1.96\,SE = 5.0 \pm 1.96 \times 0.40 = 5.0 \pm 0.784.$$
3. Bounds: $$(5.0 - 0.784,\ 5.0 + 0.784) = (\$4.216\text{M},\ \$5.784\text{M}).$$

A is correct. Distractor B uses the raw standard deviation $s = \$40$M instead of the SE ($5.0 \pm 1.96 \times 40 = 5.0 \pm 78.4$) — the classic confusion between the dispersion of *individual trials* and the precision of their *mean*. Distractor C uses $SE = 0.40$ but applies $1.0$ (or a mis-scaled multiplier) instead of 1.96 (giving $\pm 0.40$... actually $\pm 0.39$). Distractor D divides the SE by an extra factor (e.g., uses $SE/10$), grossly understating the interval.

📌 Exam tip: A confidence interval for the *mean* uses the *standard error* $s/\sqrt{N}$, not the raw standard deviation $s$. Using $s$ directly inflates the interval enormously — a favorite trap when $N$ is large.

**Q7.** Which of the following is a genuine disadvantage of the simulation approach (Monte Carlo) to financial problem solving, as opposed to a strength?

- A. It can incorporate complex, path-dependent payoffs that lack closed-form solutions.
- B. Its results are only as good as the assumed data-generating process, and it provides less general insight than an analytic solution where one exists.
- C. It produces an empirical distribution from which quantiles such as VaR can be read.
- D. Variance-reduction techniques can improve precision without proportionally more trials.

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to distinguish a true *disadvantage* from the method's *strengths*.

1. Monte Carlo's central weakness is **model risk**: the answer is only as reliable as the assumed DGP (distributions and parameters). A misspecified model produces a confidently wrong answer.
2. Additionally, a simulated result is **specific to the chosen parameters** and gives **less general insight** than a closed-form analytic solution. Where an analytic solution exists, it should generally be preferred.
3. These are genuine disadvantages of simulation.

B is correct. A, C, and D all describe *strengths* of simulation: handling path-dependent/intractable problems (A), generating a full empirical distribution for quantile/VaR estimation (C), and improving efficiency via variance-reduction techniques like antithetic and control variates (D). Only B captures a true drawback (model risk plus lack of general insight relative to analytic solutions).

📌 Exam tip: Know the disadvantages of simulation cold — sampling error (slow $\sqrt{N}$ convergence), dependence on the assumed DGP (model risk), computational cost, and less general insight than analytic solutions. Strengths (flexibility, full distribution, variance reduction) are common distractors dressed up as "disadvantages."
