**Q1.** An investor constructs a bull call spread: buy a 40-strike call for $4.00 and sell a 45-strike call for $1.50 (same expiry). The maximum profit, maximum loss, and breakeven are *closest* to:

- A. Max profit $2.50; max loss $2.50; breakeven $42.50
- B. Max profit $5.00; max loss $2.50; breakeven $42.50
- C. Max profit $2.50; max loss $5.00; breakeven $44.50
- D. Max profit unlimited; max loss $2.50; breakeven $44.00

**Answer: A**

We have a debit bull call spread with $K_1 = 40$, $K_2 = 45$. Compute the net debit, then max profit, max loss, and breakeven.

1. Net debit: $$D = 4.00 - 1.50 = \$2.50$$
2. Maximum profit (stock above $K_2$, both calls in the money): $$(K_2 - K_1) - D = (45 - 40) - 2.50 = 5 - 2.50 = \$2.50$$
3. Maximum loss (stock below $K_1$, both expire worthless): the net debit $= \$2.50$.
4. Breakeven (long call covers the debit): $$K_1 + D = 40 + 2.50 = \$42.50$$

- B uses the gross spread width $(K_2 - K_1) = 5$ as the max profit, forgetting to subtract the debit.
- C swaps max profit and max loss, and uses $K_2 - D$ for the breakeven (a bear-put formula).
- D treats it like a naked long call (unlimited upside) — the short $K_2$ call caps the profit.

📌 Exam tip: Debit vertical spreads → max profit = (strike width − debit), max loss = debit, breakeven = lower strike + debit (for calls). The width caps the payoff at $K_2 - K_1$.

**Q2.** A box spread is built from European options with strikes $K_1 = 60$ and $K_2 = 70$, expiring in 2 years; the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 5%. Its no-arbitrage price today is *closest* to:

- A. $9.05
- B. $9.51
- C. $10.00
- D. $10.52

**Answer: A**

A box spread (bull call spread + bear put spread on the same strikes) pays $K_2 - K_1$ with certainty at $T$, so its fair price is the present value of that fixed payoff.

1. Certain payoff at $T$: $$K_2 - K_1 = 70 - 60 = \$10$$
2. Discount at the risk-free rate: $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.05\times2} = e^{-0.10} = 0.904837$.
3. Fair price: $$(K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT} = 10 \times 0.904837 = \$9.05$$

- B ($9.51) uses $T = 1$ ($e^{-0.05} = 0.9512$) instead of $T = 2$.
- C ($10.00) forgets to discount — quotes the undiscounted certain payoff.
- D ($10.52) discounts with the wrong sign ($e^{+0.05}$), inflating the price.

📌 Exam tip: A box's payoff is the **riskless** amount $K_2 - K_1$; its value is its PV $(K_2-K_1)e^{-rT}$. Any market price away from PV is arbitrage — but only with **European** options (American early exercise breaks the box).

**Q3.** A trader buys a 80-strike call for $7.00 and a 80-strike put for $5.00 (same expiry). At expiration the stock is at $60. The trader's profit is *closest* to, and the position is best described as a:

- A. Profit $8.00; long straddle
- B. Profit $20.00; long straddle
- C. Profit $8.00; long strangle
- D. Profit −$12.00; short straddle

**Answer: A**

A call and a put at the same strike and expiry is a **long straddle**. Compute the total premium and the profit at $S_T = 60$.

1. Total premium paid: $$7.00 + 5.00 = \$12.00$$
2. At $S_T = 60$: call payoff $= \max(60 - 80, 0) = 0$; put payoff $= \max(80 - 60, 0) = \$20.00$.
3. Total payoff $= 0 + 20 = \$20.00$.
4. Profit $= \text{payoff} - \text{premium} = 20.00 - 12.00 = \$8.00$.
5. Same strike for both legs → **long straddle** (a long volatility bet).

- B reports the **payoff** ($20) as the profit, forgetting to subtract the $12 premium.
- C mislabels it a strangle (a strangle uses **different** strikes for the call and put).
- D has the wrong sign/structure — the trader is long, not short, and is profitable here.

📌 Exam tip: Straddle = same strike; strangle = different strikes. Profit = payoff − **total** premium. Breakevens of this straddle are $K \pm \text{premium} = 80 \pm 12 = 68$ and $92$; at $S_T = 60$ (below 68) the position profits.

**Q4.** A bear put spread is built by buying a 55-strike put for $6.00 and selling a 50-strike put for $2.50 (same expiry). The maximum profit, maximum loss, and breakeven are *closest* to:

- A. Max profit $1.50; max loss $3.50; breakeven $51.50
- B. Max profit $3.50; max loss $1.50; breakeven $53.50
- C. Max profit $5.00; max loss $3.50; breakeven $51.50
- D. Max profit $1.50; max loss unlimited; breakeven $48.50

**Answer: A**

A bear put spread buys the higher-strike put ($K_2 = 55$) and sells the lower-strike put ($K_1 = 50$); it is a debit spread.

1. Net debit: $$D = 6.00 - 2.50 = \$3.50$$
2. Maximum profit (stock below $K_1 = 50$, both puts in the money): $$(K_2 - K_1) - D = (55 - 50) - 3.50 = 5 - 3.50 = \$1.50$$
3. Maximum loss (stock above $K_2 = 55$, both expire worthless): the net debit $= \$3.50$.
4. Breakeven (long higher-strike put recovers the debit): $$K_2 - D = 55 - 3.50 = \$51.50$$

- B swaps max profit and max loss and uses $K_1 + D$ for the breakeven (call-spread logic).
- C uses the gross width $5$ as max profit (forgets the debit).
- D treats it like a naked long put — the short $K_1$ put caps the downside profit, so loss is **not** unlimited.

📌 Exam tip: Bear put spread (debit) → max profit = (width − debit), max loss = debit, breakeven = **higher** strike − debit. Mirror image of the bull call spread.

**Q5.** A long call butterfly is built by buying a 90-strike call and a 110-strike call and selling two 100-strike calls, for a net debit of $3.00. Which statement about this position is *most* accurate?

- A. Maximum profit is $10.00, achieved if the stock finishes below $90.
- B. Maximum profit is $7.00, achieved if the stock finishes exactly at $100.
- C. Maximum loss is unlimited if the stock rises far above $110.
- D. It is a long-volatility bet that profits most from a large move in either direction.

**Answer: B**

A long call butterfly with $K_1 = 90$, $K_2 = 100$, $K_3 = 110$ bets the stock finishes **near the middle strike** $K_2$.

1. Maximum profit occurs at $S_T = K_2 = 100$: $$(K_2 - K_1) - D = (100 - 90) - 3.00 = 10 - 3.00 = \$7.00$$
2. Maximum loss is the net debit $D = \$3.00$, occurring if $S_T \le 90$ or $S_T \ge 110$.

- A uses the gross width $10$ (forgets the debit) and places the max profit at the wrong stock price (it is at the middle strike, not below the lowest).
- C is wrong: the bought 110 call caps the loss; max loss is the $3.00 debit.
- D reverses the view — a butterfly is a **short-volatility** (low-movement) bet, profiting when the stock stays near $K_2$.

📌 Exam tip: Long butterfly = **short volatility**, peaks at the middle strike with profit (width − debit) and capped loss = debit. Breakevens are $K_1 + D = 93$ and $K_3 - D = 107$.

**Q6.** An investor owns 100 shares purchased at $S_0 = \$50$ and writes one covered call with $K = \$55$, collecting a $2.00 premium. Ignoring time value of money, the maximum profit and maximum loss on the combined position are *closest* to:

- A. Max profit $7.00/share; max loss $48.00/share
- B. Max profit $5.00/share; max loss $50.00/share
- C. Max profit $2.00/share; max loss $48.00/share
- D. Max profit unlimited; max loss $48.00/share

**Answer: A**

A covered call is long stock + short call. Compute max profit (stock called away at $K$) and max loss (stock falls to zero).

1. Maximum profit (at $S_T \ge K$, stock called away at $55$): $$K - S_0 + c = 55 - 50 + 2.00 = \$7.00 \text{ per share}$$
2. Maximum loss (at $S_T = 0$, stock worthless, keep the premium): $$S_0 - c = 50 - 2.00 = \$48.00 \text{ per share}$$

- B forgets the premium in both figures (uses $K - S_0 = 5$ and $S_0 = 50$).
- C reports only the premium as max profit, ignoring the $5 capital gain up to the strike.
- D is wrong: the short call **caps** the upside at $K$, so profit is not unlimited.

📌 Exam tip: Covered call max profit = $K - S_0 + c$ (gain to the strike plus premium); max loss = $S_0 - c$ (stock to zero, cushioned by the premium). By parity its profit profile equals a **short put**.

**Q7.** A trader expects a large move in a stock and believes a **downward** move is more likely than an upward one. The strategy *best* matching this view is a:

- A. Strap (2 calls + 1 put)
- B. Strip (1 call + 2 puts)
- C. Bull put spread
- D. Long strangle with the call struck far above the put

**Answer: B**

We match a "big move, down more likely" view to the correct combination.

1. A **strip** = 1 call + 2 puts (same $K$, $T$). It profits from a big move in either direction but carries **double weight on the downside** — exactly a bearish-tilted volatility bet.

- A (strap = 2 calls + 1 put) tilts toward the **upside** — the opposite tilt.
- C (bull put spread) is a **moderately bullish, limited-range** play, not a big-move bet.
- D (strangle) is symmetric — it expresses no directional tilt.

📌 Exam tip: **Stri-P → P-uts (down tilt); stra-P → P... no — anchor it as "strip has more puts (pessimistic, down), strap has more calls (up)."** Both are long-volatility (big move) bets; the extra leg sets the directional lean.

**Q8.** An investor holds 100 shares at $S_0 = \$40$ and wants downside protection at no net cash outlay. She buys a 36-strike put for $1.50 and sells a 46-strike call for $1.50 (a zero-cost collar). Ignoring time value of money, her profit if the stock finishes at $S_T = \$30$, and her maximum possible profit, are *closest* to:

- A. Profit −$4.00/share at $S_T=30$; max profit $6.00/share
- B. Profit −$10.00/share at $S_T=30$; max profit $6.00/share
- C. Profit −$4.00/share at $S_T=30$; max profit unlimited
- D. Profit +$6.00/share at $S_T=30$; max profit $10.00/share

**Answer: A**

A zero-cost collar = long stock + long put ($K_1 = 36$) + short call ($K_2 = 46$), with premiums netting to zero. The put floors losses; the call caps gains.

1. Net premium = $-1.50 + 1.50 = \$0$ (zero-cost), so profit = stock P&L plus option payoffs only.
2. At $S_T = 30$: the 36-put is exercised, so the effective sale price is the floor $K_1 = 36$. Loss from $S_0$: $$36 - 40 = -\$4.00 \text{ per share}$$ (the put limits the loss; without it the loss would be $-\$10$).
3. Maximum profit (stock at or above the call strike $K_2 = 46$, called away): $$K_2 - S_0 = 46 - 40 = \$6.00 \text{ per share}$$

- B ignores the protective put, reporting the unhedged $-\$10$ loss.
- C correctly floors the downside but wrongly leaves the upside unlimited — the short call caps it at $K_2$.
- D inverts the downside (a $30 stock cannot yield a $+6$ profit) and mislabels the max.

📌 Exam tip: A collar locks the outcome into the band $[K_1, K_2]$. Below $K_1$ the put protects (effective price = $K_1$); above $K_2$ the call caps (effective price = $K_2$). Profit floor = $K_1 - S_0$; profit cap = $K_2 - S_0$ (zero-cost case).
