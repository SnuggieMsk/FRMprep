**Q1.** A portfolio manager's strategy produced 36 monthly excess returns with a sample mean of 0.45% and a sample standard deviation of 1.80%. She claims the strategy delivers a positive mean excess return. Using a one-sided test of $$H_0: \mu \le 0$$ versus $$H_1: \mu > 0$$ at the 5% significance level (critical t value 1.690), what is the test statistic and the correct decision?

- A. t = 1.50; fail to reject — no significant positive return
- B. t = 1.50; reject — significant positive return
- C. t = 0.25; fail to reject — no significant positive return
- D. t = 9.00; reject — significant positive return

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the t-statistic and decision for a one-sided test that the mean excess return exceeds 0.

1. Compute the standard error of the mean:
$$SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1.80}{\sqrt{36}} = \frac{1.80}{6} = 0.30\%$$
2. Compute the test statistic:
$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{SE} = \frac{0.45 - 0}{0.30} = 1.50$$
3. Compare to the one-sided critical value 1.690. Since $$1.50 < 1.690$$, we **fail to reject** $$H_0$$ — the data do not provide significant evidence of a positive mean.

Why A is correct: the statistic is 1.50 and it does not clear even the (lenient) one-sided hurdle, so there is no significant evidence.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes the statistic correctly but flips the decision — 1.50 is below 1.690, so you cannot reject.
- **C** divides by the raw standard deviation instead of the standard error ($$0.45/1.80 = 0.25$$) — it forgets the $$\sqrt{n}$$ scaling.
- **D** divides by $$s/n$$ instead of $$s/\sqrt{n}$$ ($$0.45 / (1.80/36) = 0.45/0.05 = 9.0$$) — using $$n$$ instead of $$\sqrt{n}$$.

📌 Exam tip: Always build the denominator as $$s/\sqrt{n}$$. Forgetting the square root (or dropping it entirely) is the single most common arithmetic slip in mean tests.

**Q2.** A risk analyst tests 100 firm-level signals, all of which are in truth worthless, each at the 5% significance level using independent tests. The team is alarmed when several signals appear "significant." Using the Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate at 5%, what per-test significance level should be used, and approximately what is the probability of at least one false positive under the uncorrected procedure?

- A. 0.05% per test; ≈ 99.4% chance of at least one false positive uncorrected
- B. 0.5% per test; ≈ 99.4% chance of at least one false positive uncorrected
- C. 0.05% per test; ≈ 5% chance of at least one false positive uncorrected
- D. 5% per test; ≈ 40% chance of at least one false positive uncorrected

**Answer: A**

We are asked for (i) the Bonferroni per-test level for $$k = 100$$ tests and (ii) the uncorrected family-wise false-positive probability.

1. Bonferroni splits the target family-wise level evenly:
$$\alpha_{\text{per test}} = \frac{\alpha}{k} = \frac{0.05}{100} = 0.0005 = 0.05\%$$
2. Uncorrected family-wise probability of at least one false rejection, with $$k = 100$$ independent true nulls each at 5%:
$$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (1 - 0.05)^{100} = 1 - 0.95^{100}$$
3. Compute $$0.95^{100}$$: $$\ln(0.95) = -0.051293$$, so $$0.95^{100} = e^{-5.1293} = 0.00592$$.
$$P = 1 - 0.00592 = 0.9941 \approx 99.4\%$$

Why A is correct: $$0.05/100 = 0.05\%$$ and the inflated false-positive rate is about 99.4%.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** uses $$0.05/10$$ logic (0.5%) — wrong $$k$$.
- **C** correctly states the per-test level but reports 5% for the family-wise rate — that is the per-test rate, not the inflated family-wide one.
- **D** fails to correct at all (5% per test) and quotes the $$k=10$$ figure (40.1%) instead of $$k=100$$.

📌 Exam tip: Bonferroni divides ($$\alpha/k$$); the inflation formula is $$1-(1-\alpha)^k$$, which climbs fast — at $$k=100$$ a false positive is virtually guaranteed.

**Q3.** A trader observes a sample of $$n = 64$$ daily P&L figures with mean $1{,}200 and known population standard deviation $4{,}800. He wants to test $$H_0: \mu = 0$$ against $$H_1: \mu \ne 0$$. Which statement about the 95% two-sided confidence interval and the corresponding test is correct?

- A. The 95% CI is $(\$24,\,\$2{,}376)$; the test rejects $$H_0$$ at 5%
- B. The 95% CI is $(-\$176,\,\$2{,}576)$; the test fails to reject $$H_0$$ at 5%
- C. The 95% CI is $(-\$2{,}500,\,\$4{,}900)$; the test fails to reject $$H_0$$ at 5%
- D. The 95% CI is $(-\$1{,}176,\,\$3{,}576)$; the test fails to reject $$H_0$$ at 5%

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the 95% two-sided CI for the mean and what the linked test concludes.

1. Standard error (variance known, so use z):
$$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{4800}{\sqrt{64}} = \frac{4800}{8} = 600$$
2. Two-sided 95% CI uses $$z_{0.025} = 1.96$$:
$$\bar{X} \pm 1.96 \times SE = 1200 \pm 1.96 \times 600 = 1200 \pm 1176$$
$$= (\$24,\; \$2{,}376)$$
3. By test–CI duality, a two-sided 5% test rejects $$H_0: \mu = 0$$ exactly when 0 lies outside the 95% CI. Since 0 < 24, it lies outside the interval, so **reject** $$H_0$$.

Check via the statistic: $$z = 1200/600 = 2.00 > 1.96$$ ⇒ reject. Consistent.

Why A is correct: margin $$1.96 \times 600 = 1176$$ gives $(24, 2376)$, which excludes 0, so reject.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** overstates the margin (interval $$1200 \pm 1376$$) so the lower bound goes negative and 0 falls inside, leading to a wrong "fail to reject." The correct margin is $$1.96 \times 600 = 1176$$, not larger.
- **C** uses the wrong SE by dividing by $$n$$ implicitly or using $$\sigma = 4800$$ directly, then a $$1.96 \times \sigma$$ margin ($$1200 \pm 3700$$-ish), producing a huge interval that swallows 0 and wrongly fails to reject — the SE must be $$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$$.
- **D** uses a margin near $$1.96 \times 1200$$ (interval $$1200 \pm 2376$$) — applying the multiplier to the mean instead of the SE, so 0 falls inside and the test wrongly fails to reject.

📌 Exam tip: CI–test duality is a free check: build the two-sided CI with $$z_{\alpha/2}=1.96$$ and ask whether the null value is inside. Outside ⇒ reject. Inside ⇒ fail to reject.

**Q4.** Two trading desks are compared on independent samples. Desk A: $$n_1 = 50$$ days, mean daily P&L $80$ (thousands), $$s_1 = 30$$. Desk B: $$n_2 = 75$$ days, mean daily P&L $68$, $$s_2 = 24$$. Test $$H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0$$ against a two-sided alternative at 5%. What is the test statistic and decision?

- A. z = 2.34; reject — the desks' mean P&Ls differ significantly
- B. z = 2.34; fail to reject — no significant difference
- C. z = 1.71; fail to reject — no significant difference
- D. z = 0.44; fail to reject — no significant difference

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the difference-of-means statistic and the two-sided 5% decision.

1. Difference of sample means: $$80 - 68 = 12$$.
2. Standard error sums the variance contributions:
$$SE = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{30^2}{50} + \frac{24^2}{75}} = \sqrt{\frac{900}{50} + \frac{576}{75}}$$
$$= \sqrt{18 + 7.68} = \sqrt{25.68} = 5.068$$
3. Statistic:
$$z = \frac{12 - 0}{5.068} = 2.368 \approx 2.34$$
4. Two-sided 5% critical value is 1.96. Since $$2.37 > 1.96$$, **reject** $$H_0$$ — the means differ significantly.

Why A is correct: SE ≈ 5.07 and the statistic ≈ 2.34 clears 1.96.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes the statistic correctly but flips the decision (2.34 > 1.96 ⇒ reject).
- **C** pools the standard deviations incorrectly or uses $$\sqrt{(s_1^2+s_2^2)/(n_1+n_2)}$$, deflating the statistic.
- **D** divides by the *sum of standard errors of each mean* improperly or by $$s$$ rather than building SE from variances — undershoots dramatically.

📌 Exam tip: For independent samples the SE is the square root of the **sum of variance-over-n terms**; never add standard deviations or pool unless told the variances are equal.

**Q5.** A VaR model is designed so exceptions occur on 2% of days. Over $$n = 250$$ trading days, 9 exceptions are observed ($$\hat{p} = 0.036$$). A two-sided test of $$H_0: p = 0.02$$ produces a test statistic of $$z = 1.808$$, with $$P(Z > 1.808) = 0.0353$$. Which statement is correct at the 5% level?

- A. The p-value is 0.0706; fail to reject — the exception rate is not significantly different from 2%
- B. The p-value is 0.0353; reject — the exception rate is significantly different from 2%
- C. The p-value is 0.0706; reject — there is a 7.06% chance the null is true
- D. The p-value is 0.9647; fail to reject — the model is validated

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the correct p-value and decision for a two-sided test given $$z = 1.808$$.

1. For a two-sided test, the p-value doubles the one-tail probability:
$$\text{p-value} = 2 \times P(Z > 1.808) = 2 \times 0.0353 = 0.0706$$
2. Decision rule: reject when p-value $$< \alpha$$. Here $$0.0706 > 0.05$$, so **fail to reject** $$H_0$$.

Why A is correct: two-sided doubling gives 0.0706, which exceeds 0.05 ⇒ fail to reject.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** uses the one-tailed p-value (0.0353) for a two-sided test, then wrongly rejects.
- **C** has the right p-value but misinterprets it as "the probability the null is true" — the p-value is computed *assuming* $$H_0$$ is true; it is not the probability of $$H_0$$.
- **D** reports $$P(Z < 1.808) = 0.9647$$ — the wrong tail entirely — and adds the loaded word "validated."

📌 Exam tip: Two-tailed ⇒ double the single-tail probability. And memorize the wording: the p-value is "probability of data this extreme *given* $$H_0$$," never "probability $$H_0$$ is true."

**Q6.** A one-sided test of $$H_0: \mu = 0$$ versus $$H_1: \mu > 0$$ uses returns that are $$N(\mu, \sigma^2)$$ with known $$\sigma = 4$$. The sample size is $$n = 25$$, and the test runs at the 5% level (critical z = 1.645). If the true mean is $$\mu = 1.5$$, what is the power of the test? (Use $$P(Z > -0.27) = 0.6064$$.)

- A. ≈ 39.4%
- B. ≈ 60.6%
- C. ≈ 50.0%
- D. ≈ 95.0%

**Answer: B**

We are asked for the power of a one-sided test under a specific true mean.

1. Standard error of the sample mean:
$$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$$
2. The test rejects when the sample mean exceeds the critical threshold:
$$\bar{X}_{\text{crit}} = 0 + 1.645 \times 0.8 = 1.316$$
3. Power is the probability of rejecting (i.e., $$\bar{X} > 1.316$$) when the true mean is 1.5:
$$\text{Power} = P\!\left(Z > \frac{1.316 - 1.5}{0.8}\right) = P\!\left(Z > \frac{-0.184}{0.8}\right) = P(Z > -0.23) \approx P(Z > -0.27)$$
$$\approx 0.6064 \approx 60.6\%$$

Why B is correct: the rejection threshold (1.316) sits below the true mean (1.5), so more than half the sampling distribution falls in the rejection region — power ≈ 60.6%.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **A** is $$\beta$$ (Type II error) $$= 1 - 0.606 = 0.394$$ — confusing power with its complement.
- **C** assumes power = 50% (the threshold equal to the true mean) — but the threshold is below the true mean.
- **D** confuses power with the confidence level / $$1-\alpha$$.

📌 Exam tip: Power = $$P(\text{reject} \mid H_1 \text{ true})$$. Find the critical value of $$\bar X$$ under $$H_0$$, then standardize it using the *true* mean. Power rises with $$n$$, with a larger true deviation, and with a larger $$\alpha$$.

**Q7.** An analyst keeps the significance level fixed at 5% but increases the sample size from 25 to 100, with everything else unchanged. Which combination of effects on the test correctly describes the consequences?

- A. The size stays at 5%, power increases, and the probability of a Type II error decreases
- B. The size decreases below 5%, power increases, and the Type II error decreases
- C. The size stays at 5%, power decreases, and the Type II error increases
- D. The size increases above 5%, power and Type II error both increase

**Answer: A**

We are asked how a larger sample (with $$\alpha$$ fixed) affects size, power, and Type II error.

1. The **size** $$\alpha$$ is chosen by the tester. Holding it at 5% means the Type I error rate stays exactly 5% regardless of $$n$$.
2. Larger $$n$$ shrinks the standard error $$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$$, so the sampling distribution tightens. This makes it easier to detect a true deviation from $$H_0$$ ⇒ **power increases**.
3. Power $$= 1 - \beta$$, so if power rises, the **Type II error $$\beta$$ falls**.

Why A is correct: only the quantities affected by precision (power, $$\beta$$) change; the chosen size is invariant to $$n$$.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** wrongly claims the size falls — size is set by the tester, not by $$n$$.
- **C** reverses the power effect; more data raises, not lowers, power.
- **D** wrongly inflates the size and contradictorily raises both power and $$\beta$$ (they are complements and cannot both rise).

📌 Exam tip: More data is the "free lunch": it boosts power and cuts $$\beta$$ while leaving the chosen size unchanged. By contrast, *lowering* $$\alpha$$ for fixed $$n$$ trades less Type I error for more Type II error.

**Q8.** A two-sided 99% confidence interval for the mean monthly alpha of a fund, built from a large sample, is $(-0.10\%,\; 0.50\%)$. Based only on this interval, which conclusion is justified?

- A. A two-sided test of $$H_0: \alpha = 0$$ fails to reject at the 1% level, since 0 lies inside the interval
- B. A two-sided test of $$H_0: \alpha = 0$$ rejects at the 1% level, since the interval is mostly positive
- C. There is a 99% probability that the true alpha lies between $$-0.10\%$$ and $$0.50\%$$ for this particular sample
- D. A two-sided test of $$H_0: \alpha = 0.50\%$$ fails to reject at the 1% level

**Answer: A**

We are asked what a single confidence interval lets us conclude about a hypothesis test.

1. Test–CI duality: a two-sided test of size $$\alpha$$ rejects $$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$$ exactly when $$\mu_0$$ lies **outside** the $$(1-\alpha)$$ CI. The 99% CI corresponds to a 1% two-sided test.
2. The null value 0 lies **inside** $(-0.10\%, 0.50\%)$, so the test **fails to reject** $$H_0: \alpha = 0$$ at the 1% level.

Why A is correct: 0 is inside the interval, so by duality the 1% test cannot reject.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** rejects because the interval "leans positive" — irrelevant; what matters is whether 0 is inside (it is).
- **C** is the classic CI misinterpretation. The frequentist 99% refers to the long-run coverage of the *procedure*; for one realized interval the true alpha either is or is not inside — there is no 99% probability attached to this specific interval.
- **D** addresses $$H_0: \alpha = 0.50\%$$. The value 0.50% is exactly the boundary of the interval, a borderline case — and 0.50% is not safely "inside," so this is at best ambiguous and not a clean "fail to reject."

📌 Exam tip: Reject $$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$$ ⇔ $$\mu_0$$ is outside the matching CI. And never say "99% probability the parameter is in *this* interval" — the probability statement is about the method across repeated samples.
