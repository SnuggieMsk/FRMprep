**Q1.** A multi-strategy fund is held as one of fifty sleeves inside a fully diversified institutional portfolio. Over the year the sleeve returned 14% with a standard deviation of 30% and a beta of 0.8. A second candidate sleeve returned 11% with a standard deviation of 16% and a beta of 1.1. The market returned 9% and the risk-free rate is 3%. Which sleeve adds more value to the existing diversified portfolio, and on which measure should the decision be based?
- A. The first sleeve, based on its higher Sharpe ratio
- B. The first sleeve, based on its higher Treynor ratio
- C. The second sleeve, based on its higher Treynor ratio
- D. The second sleeve, based on its higher Sharpe ratio

**Answer: B**

The ask: choose the better *sleeve within an already diversified portfolio* and name the correct measure.

1. Because each candidate is just one of many holdings in a diversified whole, idiosyncratic risk is already diversified away — only **systematic risk (beta)** matters. The correct yardstick is the **Treynor ratio**, not Sharpe.
2. Treynor formula: $$T_p = \dfrac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\beta_p}$$
3. Sleeve 1: $$T_1 = \dfrac{14 - 3}{0.8} = \dfrac{11}{0.8} = 13.75\%$$
4. Sleeve 2: $$T_2 = \dfrac{11 - 3}{1.1} = \dfrac{8}{1.1} = 7.27\%$$
5. Sleeve 1 has the higher Treynor ratio (13.75% vs. 7.27%), so it adds more per unit of systematic risk.

Why B is correct: the context (one sleeve of many) dictates Treynor, and sleeve 1 wins on it.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A names the right sleeve but the wrong measure. Sleeve 1's Sharpe is $$\frac{14-3}{30}=0.367$$, *lower* than sleeve 2's $$\frac{11-3}{16}=0.50$$ — its huge 30% σ (mostly idiosyncratic) makes it look bad on Sharpe. Using Sharpe here is the classic "wrong-context" error.
- C uses the right measure (Treynor) but mis-ranks: it claims sleeve 2 has the higher Treynor, which is false (7.27% < 13.75%).
- D picks the higher Sharpe sleeve — correct math for Sharpe, but Sharpe is the wrong measure for a diversified component.

📌 Exam tip: Match measure to context first, *then* compute. "One of many funds" → Treynor/alpha (beta risk); "entire wealth / stand-alone" → Sharpe (total σ). A fund with big idiosyncratic risk looks great on Treynor and awful on Sharpe — that gap is the test.

**Q2.** An analyst computes the following for an actively managed equity fund and its benchmark over five years: fund mean return 13.2%, benchmark mean return 10.0%, fund σ = 19%, benchmark σ = 15%, correlation between fund and benchmark = 0.90. The annualized standard deviation of active (fund minus benchmark) returns is to be used as tracking error. The fund's information ratio is closest to:
- A. 0.36
- B. 0.40
- C. 0.64
- D. 0.21

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the information ratio, where tracking error must be derived from the variance of active returns.

1. Active return = $$R_p - R_b$$. Its variance: $$\sigma^2_{p-b} = \sigma_p^2 + \sigma_b^2 - 2\rho\,\sigma_p\sigma_b$$
2. Plug in: $$\sigma^2_{p-b} = 0.19^2 + 0.15^2 - 2(0.90)(0.19)(0.15)$$
3. Compute pieces: $$0.0361 + 0.0225 - 2(0.90)(0.0285) = 0.0586 - 0.0513 = 0.0073$$
4. Tracking error: $$TE = \sqrt{0.0073} = 0.08544 \approx 8.54\%$$
5. Information ratio: $$IR = \dfrac{E[R_p]-E[R_b]}{TE} = \dfrac{13.2\% - 10.0\%}{8.54\%} = \dfrac{3.2\%}{8.54\%} \approx 0.375 \approx 0.40$$

Why B is correct: 0.375 rounds to roughly 0.40 and is the only option consistent with the proper TE derivation.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A (0.36) results from using $$TE = \sqrt{0.0073}$$ but a slightly different rounding chain; more commonly it comes from a small arithmetic slip in the cross term. It is close enough to be a deliberate "almost-right" lure — recompute carefully.
- C (0.64) comes from wrongly using $$TE = \sigma_p - \sigma_b = 19\% - 15\% = 4\%$$ → $$3.2/4 = 0.80$$... or from forgetting correlation entirely and using $$\sqrt{0.0361+0.0225}=0.242$$. Either way it mishandles the variance-of-difference formula.
- D (0.21) comes from forgetting that variance subtracts the covariance term, e.g., adding instead: $$\sqrt{0.0586+0.0513}$$ gives ~33% → 0.097. Adding the covariance instead of subtracting it overstates TE and shrinks IR.

📌 Exam tip: Tracking error is the σ of the *difference* of two correlated series — you must use $$\sigma_p^2+\sigma_b^2-2\rho\sigma_p\sigma_b$$, never $$\sigma_p-\sigma_b$$. High correlation (here 0.90) shrinks TE sharply, which is why active managers track closely to a benchmark.

**Q3.** A lending unit reports for the year: revenues (interest + fees) $120 million, operating costs $40 million, expected losses $30 million, and taxes $8 million. The economic capital allocated is $200 million, invested in risk-free assets earning 5%. Transfer-price credits add $2 million to income. What is the unit's RAROC?
- A. 27.0%
- B. 24.0%
- C. 22.0%
- D. 21.0%

**Answer: A**

The ask: compute RAROC using the full expanded numerator.

1. RAROC formula: $$RAROC = \dfrac{\text{revenues} - \text{costs} - EL - \text{taxes} + (R_f \times EC) \pm \text{transfer prices}}{EC}$$
2. Return on economic capital: $$R_f \times EC = 0.05 \times 200 = 10$$ million.
3. Numerator, step by step: $$120 - 40 = 80;\; 80 - 30 = 50;\; 50 - 8 = 42;\; 42 + 10 = 52;\; 52 + 2 = 54$$ million.
4. RAROC: $$\dfrac{54}{200} = 0.27 = 27\%$$.

Why A is correct: every income item is netted (revenues less costs, expected losses, and taxes), then the return earned on economic capital and the transfer-price credit are added back, all divided by economic capital.

Why the distractors trap you:
- B (24.0%) drops the transfer-price credit *and* mishandles one item: e.g., $$(120-40-30-8+10)/200 = 52/200 = 26\%$$ is close; 24% arises from also netting transfer prices as a cost: $$(120-40-30-8+10-2)/200 = 50/200 = 25\%$$ — these "sign-of-transfer-price" slips cluster near the right answer to bait you.
- C (22.0%) omits the $10m return-on-capital add-back entirely: $$(120-40-30-8+0+2)/200 = 44/200 = 22\%$$. Forgetting to add $$R_f \times EC$$ is the single most common RAROC error.
- D (21.0%) omits *both* the return on capital and treats transfer prices as a deduction: $$(120-40-30-8-2)/200 = 40/200 = 20\%$$, near 21%.

📌 Exam tip: In the RAROC numerator, expected losses are *subtracted* (a priced cost), while the *return earned on the economic capital* (≈ $$R_f \times EC$$) and transfer-price credits are *added back*. Missing the capital-return add-back is the most common slip.

**Q4.** A bank's structured-credit business has a RAROC of 16%. Its equity beta is 1.4, the expected market return is 11%, and the risk-free rate is 3%. The bank's overall hurdle rate (weighted cost of equity capital) is 13%. Using adjusted RAROC (ARAROC), the project should be:
- A. Accepted, because RAROC (16%) exceeds the hurdle rate (13%)
- B. Rejected, because ARAROC (4.8%) exceeds the risk-free rate (3%)
- C. Accepted, because ARAROC (4.8%) exceeds the risk-free rate (3%)
- D. Rejected, because ARAROC (4.8%) is below the hurdle rate (13%)

**Answer: C**

The ask: apply the *ARAROC* decision rule, not the plain RAROC-vs-hurdle rule.

1. ARAROC formula: $$ARAROC = RAROC - \beta_E\big(E[R_m] - R_f\big)$$
2. Market risk premium: $$E[R_m] - R_f = 11\% - 3\% = 8\%$$.
3. Systematic risk charge: $$\beta_E \times 8\% = 1.4 \times 8\% = 11.2\%$$.
4. ARAROC: $$16\% - 11.2\% = 4.8\%$$.
5. Decision rule: accept if $$ARAROC > R_f$$. Here $$4.8\% > 3\%$$ → **accept**.

Why C is correct: the ARAROC rule benchmarks against the *risk-free rate* (the systematic risk has already been charged in the adjustment), and 4.8% clears 3%.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A applies the *plain* RAROC-vs-hurdle test. That gives "accept" too, but the question demands the ARAROC method; A also fails to charge for the business's high beta, which is the whole point of ARAROC.
- B does the math right (ARAROC = 4.8% > 3%) but mislabels the verdict as "Rejected" — a careless reading where the comparison supports acceptance.
- D compares ARAROC to the *hurdle rate* (13%). That is the wrong benchmark for ARAROC — once beta is netted out, the correct comparison is to $$R_f$$, not the hurdle.

📌 Exam tip: Two distinct rules. Plain **RAROC > hurdle rate** (cost of equity capital). **ARAROC > risk-free rate** — because ARAROC has already subtracted the CAPM systematic-risk charge $$\beta_E(E[R_m]-R_f)$$. Don't compare ARAROC to the hurdle rate.

**Q5.** A long-volatility hedge fund and a short-volatility hedge fund both report a mean annual return of 12% and identical standard deviations of 10%, giving identical Sharpe ratios. The short-vol fund, however, has frequent small gains and rare large losses, while the long-vol fund has frequent small losses and rare large gains. The minimum acceptable return is 4%. The short-vol fund's downside deviation is 12% and the long-vol fund's is 5%. Which statement is most accurate?
- A. The Sortino ratios are equal because mean returns and MARs are equal
- B. The long-vol fund has the higher Sortino ratio (1.60 vs. 0.67), better reflecting the asymmetry of returns
- C. Sharpe is the superior measure here because it already captures the asymmetry
- D. The short-vol fund has the higher Sortino ratio because its volatility is mostly upside

**Answer: B**

The ask: compare the two funds using the Sortino ratio and explain why it is the right tool for asymmetric returns.

1. Sortino formula: $$\text{Sortino} = \dfrac{E[R_p] - R_{min}}{\sigma_{down}}$$
2. Long-vol fund: $$\dfrac{12\% - 4\%}{5\%} = \dfrac{8}{5} = 1.60$$
3. Short-vol fund: $$\dfrac{12\% - 4\%}{12\%} = \dfrac{8}{12} = 0.67$$
4. The long-vol fund has the higher Sortino ratio. Sortino penalizes only *downside* deviation (returns below the MAR), so it correctly rewards the long-vol fund whose bad outcomes are mild and punishes the short-vol fund whose bad outcomes are severe — even though Sharpe rates them identically.

Why B is correct: identical Sharpe ratios hide the asymmetry; downside deviation differs (5% vs. 12%), so Sortino separates the funds correctly.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A confuses total σ with downside deviation. Equal means and MARs do *not* make Sortino equal — the denominators (downside deviations) differ.
- C is backward: Sharpe uses *total* σ and treats upside and downside volatility identically, so it *cannot* capture asymmetry — that is precisely why Sortino exists.
- D mis-describes the short-vol fund. Short-vol strategies have *rare large losses* (fat left tail), so their downside deviation is high (12%), giving a *lower* Sortino. The claim that its volatility is "mostly upside" is false.

📌 Exam tip: When returns are asymmetric (options strategies, hedge funds), Sharpe is misleading because it punishes upside volatility equally. Sortino divides excess return by *downside* deviation only — favoring positively skewed payoffs and exposing hidden tail risk in negatively skewed ones (the Niederhoffer short-vol trap).

**Q6.** A risk committee is told that Business Unit X's stand-alone economic capital is $400 million, but its *marginal* contribution to firm-wide economic capital — after diversification benefits with other units — is only $260 million. Unit X generates $52 million of risk-adjusted net income (including return on capital). Which statement best describes the implication for RAROC-based capital allocation?
- A. RAROC must always be computed on stand-alone capital to avoid double counting
- B. Using marginal capital ($260m) gives RAROC = 20%, versus 13% on stand-alone capital — the choice of capital base materially changes the unit's apparent profitability
- C. The diversification benefit should be ignored because it cannot be measured
- D. RAROC is unaffected by the choice of capital base since net income is fixed

**Answer: B**

The ask: show how the diversification/capital-allocation choice changes RAROC and identify the correct implication.

1. RAROC on **stand-alone** capital: $$\dfrac{52}{400} = 0.13 = 13\%$$
2. RAROC on **marginal (diversified)** capital: $$\dfrac{52}{260} = 0.20 = 20\%$$
3. The numerator (net income) is fixed, but the denominator (economic capital) is not — using the smaller marginal capital base lifts RAROC from 13% to 20%. A unit that fails a 15% hurdle on stand-alone capital *passes* on marginal capital.

Why B is correct: it correctly computes both figures and identifies the central limitation — how the diversification benefit is allocated changes every unit's RAROC and thus capital-allocation and pricing decisions.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A overstates a rule. There is no universal mandate to use stand-alone capital; in fact marginal/diversified capital often better reflects the unit's true contribution. The "always" is the giveaway.
- C is wrong because diversification benefits *can* be estimated (correlations, marginal capital) and ignoring them distorts incentives — it is a challenge to *allocate*, not a reason to discard.
- D is mathematically false: changing the denominator changes the ratio even with fixed income (13% vs. 20%).

📌 Exam tip: A unit's stand-alone economic capital always exceeds its marginal contribution to firm-wide capital (because of diversification). Whichever base the firm picks shifts every unit's RAROC — a recognized limitation of RAROC for capital allocation and a favorite "challenges of risk-adjusted measures" question.

**Q7.** Two equity funds are evaluated against the CAPM. Fund P returned 10% with β = 0.6; Fund Q returned 14% with β = 1.6. The market returned 9% and the risk-free rate is 2%. An analyst ranks the funds by Jensen's alpha and concludes Fund Q is the superior manager. What is the most accurate critique?
- A. The analyst is correct; Q's alpha of +1.4% exceeds P's +3.8%, so Q is better
- B. Jensen's alpha is an absolute measure and should not be used to rank funds with very different betas; on a per-unit-of-beta basis P is superior despite a smaller alpha
- C. Alpha cannot be computed without the funds' standard deviations
- D. Both funds have negative alpha, so neither manager added value

**Answer: B**

The ask: critique ranking dissimilar-beta funds by raw Jensen's alpha.

1. Jensen's alpha: $$\alpha_p = E[R_p] - \big[R_f + \beta_p(E[R_m]-R_f)\big]$$, with market premium $$9\%-2\%=7\%$$.
2. Fund P: $$\alpha_P = 10 - [2 + 0.6(7)] = 10 - [2 + 4.2] = 10 - 6.2 = +3.8\%$$
3. Fund Q: $$\alpha_Q = 14 - [2 + 1.6(7)] = 14 - [2 + 11.2] = 14 - 13.2 = +0.8\%$$
4. P's alpha (+3.8%) actually *exceeds* Q's (+0.8%). Moreover, alpha is an absolute percentage, not scaled by risk taken; to compare managers with very different betas you should scale alpha by beta (or use the appraisal/Treynor framework). P delivers far more alpha while taking on much less systematic risk.

Why B is correct: alpha is absolute, so it does not rank funds of different beta cleanly; once you account for that, P is the superior manager.

Why the distractors trap you:
- A miscomputes both alphas (it swaps the values and claims Q = +1.4%, P = +3.8% then calls Q better — internally contradictory) and accepts naive alpha ranking. Q's true alpha is only +0.8%.
- C confuses alpha (needs beta) with Sharpe/Sortino (need σ). Jensen's alpha uses beta, not standard deviation — σ is not required.
- D is factually wrong: both alphas are positive (+3.8% and +0.8%).

📌 Exam tip: Jensen's alpha is a *level* (percentage), not a ratio, so it cannot fairly rank funds with different betas — a low-beta fund earning a smaller-looking return can still post a higher alpha and a far higher alpha-per-unit-of-beta. To rank, scale by risk (Treynor, or the appraisal ratio alpha/σ_ε).
