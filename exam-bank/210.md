**Q1.** A short-rate model is estimated as $$r_t = 0.45 + 0.90\,r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$ with $$\sigma_\epsilon = 0.5$$ (% points). The current rate is $$r_t = 6.0\%$$. What are the mean-reverting level and the two-step-ahead forecast?

- A. Mean = 4.5%; $$\hat r_{t+2} = 5.715\%$$
- B. Mean = 0.5%; $$\hat r_{t+2} = 5.715\%$$
- C. Mean = 4.5%; $$\hat r_{t+2} = 5.85\%$$
- D. Mean = 4.95%; $$\hat r_{t+2} = 5.55\%$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the long-run mean and the 2-step forecast of an AR(1).

1. Mean-reverting level:
$$\mu = \frac{\delta}{1 - \phi} = \frac{0.45}{1 - 0.90} = \frac{0.45}{0.10} = 4.5\%$$
2. One-step forecast:
$$\hat r_{t+1} = 0.45 + 0.90 \times 6.0 = 0.45 + 5.40 = 5.85\%$$
3. Two-step forecast (iterate, using the forecast as the input):
$$\hat r_{t+2} = 0.45 + 0.90 \times 5.85 = 0.45 + 5.265 = 5.715\%$$
Check with the mean-reversion form: $$\hat r_{t+2} = \mu + \phi^2 (r_t - \mu) = 4.5 + 0.81 \times (6.0 - 4.5) = 4.5 + 0.81 \times 1.5 = 4.5 + 1.215 = 5.715\%$$. ✓

Why A is correct: mean 4.5% and $$\hat r_{t+2} = 5.715\%$$, consistent both by iteration and by the closed form.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** uses $$\sigma_\epsilon$$ (0.5) as the mean — confusing the shock SD with the long-run level, or computing $$\delta - \phi$$ nonsense.
- **C** reports the *one-step* forecast (5.85%) as if it were two-step — stops iterating too early.
- **D** mis-computes the mean ($$0.45/(1-0.90)$$ misread) and forecasts inconsistently.

📌 Exam tip: Mean = $$\delta/(1-\phi)$$, not $$\delta$$. For multi-step forecasts, either iterate (feeding each forecast back in) or use $$\hat Y_{t+h} = \mu + \phi^h(Y_t - \mu)$$ — they must agree.

**Q2.** An MA(1) process is $$Y_t = 5 + 0.8\,\epsilon_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$ with $$\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 9$$. What are the unconditional variance of $$Y$$ and the lag-1 autocorrelation $$\rho_1$$?

- A. Var = 14.76; $$\rho_1 = 0.488$$
- B. Var = 9.00; $$\rho_1 = 0.800$$
- C. Var = 14.76; $$\rho_1 = 0.800$$
- D. Var = 11.88; $$\rho_1 = 0.488$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the variance and lag-1 ACF of an MA(1).

1. Unconditional variance:
$$\mathrm{Var}(Y) = \sigma^2 (1 + \theta^2) = 9 \times (1 + 0.8^2) = 9 \times (1 + 0.64) = 9 \times 1.64 = 14.76$$
2. Lag-1 autocorrelation:
$$\rho_1 = \frac{\theta}{1 + \theta^2} = \frac{0.8}{1 + 0.64} = \frac{0.8}{1.64} = 0.4878 \approx 0.488$$
(All higher-lag autocorrelations are zero — MA(1) memory is one period.)

Why A is correct: $$9 \times 1.64 = 14.76$$ and $$0.8/1.64 = 0.488$$.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** forgets the $$(1+\theta^2)$$ factor for the variance (uses $$\sigma^2 = 9$$) and reports $$\rho_1 = \theta = 0.8$$ — confusing the MA coefficient with its autocorrelation.
- **C** has the right variance but again reports $$\rho_1 = \theta = 0.8$$ instead of $$\theta/(1+\theta^2)$$.
- **D** uses $$\theta = 0.6$$-style numbers ($$9 \times 1.32 = 11.88$$) — wrong coefficient in the variance.

📌 Exam tip: For MA(1): variance $$= \sigma^2(1+\theta^2)$$ and $$\rho_1 = \theta/(1+\theta^2)$$ (NOT $$\theta$$ itself). The autocorrelation is always smaller in magnitude than $$\theta$$.

**Q3.** A model's residuals over $$T = 144$$ observations have sample autocorrelations $$\hat\rho_1 = 0.15$$, $$\hat\rho_2 = 0.10$$, $$\hat\rho_3 = 0.08$$. Using the Box–Pierce statistic with $$m = 3$$, test for white noise at 5% (critical $$\chi^2_3 = 7.81$$).

- A. $$Q_{BP} = 5.60$$; fail to reject — residuals consistent with white noise
- B. $$Q_{BP} = 5.60$$; reject — residuals are serially correlated
- C. $$Q_{BP} = 0.039$$; fail to reject — residuals consistent with white noise
- D. $$Q_{BP} = 12.4$$; reject — residuals are serially correlated

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the Box–Pierce Q-statistic and decision.

1. Box–Pierce formula:
$$Q_{BP} = T \sum_{h=1}^{m} \hat\rho_h^2$$
2. Sum the squared autocorrelations:
$$\hat\rho_1^2 + \hat\rho_2^2 + \hat\rho_3^2 = 0.15^2 + 0.10^2 + 0.08^2 = 0.0225 + 0.0100 + 0.0064 = 0.0389$$
3. Multiply by $$T = 144$$:
$$Q_{BP} = 144 \times 0.0389 = 5.60$$
Compare to $$\chi^2_3 = 7.81$$. Since $$5.60 < 7.81$$, **fail to reject** — the residuals are consistent with white noise.

Why A is correct: $$Q_{BP} = T\sum\hat\rho_h^2 = 5.60 < 7.81$$ ⇒ fail to reject (the statistic is well below the critical value).

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes the statistic correctly but flips the decision — it is below 7.81, so cannot reject.
- **C** forgets to multiply by $$T$$, reporting just $$\sum\hat\rho_h^2 = 0.0389 \approx 0.039$$.
- **D** squares incorrectly or applies the Ljung–Box $$T(T+2)$$ weighting without the $$1/(T-h)$$ denominators, grossly inflating the statistic past the critical value.

📌 Exam tip: Box–Pierce is $$T\sum\hat\rho_h^2$$; Ljung–Box is $$T(T+2)\sum \hat\rho_h^2/(T-h)$$. Both are $$\chi^2_m$$ and reject for *large* values. Don't forget to multiply by $$T$$.

**Q4.** The same residuals as Q3 ($$T = 144$$, $$\hat\rho_1 = 0.15$$, $$\hat\rho_2 = 0.10$$, $$\hat\rho_3 = 0.08$$) are tested with the Ljung–Box statistic ($$m = 3$$, critical $$\chi^2_3 = 7.81$$). What is $$Q_{LB}$$ and the decision?

- A. $$Q_{LB} \approx 5.74$$; fail to reject — consistent with white noise
- B. $$Q_{LB} \approx 5.74$$; reject — serially correlated
- C. $$Q_{LB} \approx 5.60$$; reject — serially correlated
- D. $$Q_{LB} \approx 0.040$$; fail to reject — consistent with white noise

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the Ljung–Box statistic and decision.

1. Ljung–Box formula:
$$Q_{LB} = T(T+2)\sum_{h=1}^{m} \frac{\hat\rho_h^2}{T - h}$$
2. Compute each term ($$T = 144$$, so $$T+2 = 146$$, $$T(T+2) = 144 \times 146 = 21{,}024$$):
$$\frac{\hat\rho_1^2}{T-1} = \frac{0.0225}{143} = 0.00015734$$
$$\frac{\hat\rho_2^2}{T-2} = \frac{0.0100}{142} = 0.00007042$$
$$\frac{\hat\rho_3^2}{T-3} = \frac{0.0064}{141} = 0.00004539$$
Sum $$= 0.00027315$$.
3. Multiply:
$$Q_{LB} = 21{,}024 \times 0.00027315 = 5.743 \approx 5.78$$
Compare to $$\chi^2_3 = 7.81$$. Since $$5.74 < 7.81$$, **fail to reject** — residuals are consistent with white noise. (Ljung–Box is slightly larger than Box–Pierce, as expected in finite samples.)

Why A is correct: $$Q_{LB} \approx 5.7$$–$$5.8 < 7.81$$ ⇒ fail to reject.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes correctly but flips the decision.
- **C** reports the Box–Pierce value (≈5.60) and additionally flips the decision — confusing the two statistics and over-rejecting.
- **D** forgets to multiply by $$T(T+2)$$, leaving just the weighted sum.

📌 Exam tip: Ljung–Box ($$T(T+2)\sum\hat\rho_h^2/(T-h)$$) is always slightly larger than Box–Pierce and is preferred in small samples. Both compare to $$\chi^2_m$$ and reject only for large values.

**Q5.** For an AR(1) process $$Y_t = \delta + \phi Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$ with $$\phi = 0.7$$, which combination of theoretical ACF and PACF behavior is correct?

- A. ACF decays geometrically as $$\rho_h = 0.7^h$$; PACF cuts off after lag 1
- B. ACF cuts off after lag 1; PACF decays geometrically
- C. Both ACF and PACF decay geometrically
- D. ACF cuts off after lag 1; PACF cuts off after lag 1

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the ACF/PACF signature of an AR(1).

1. For an AR(1), the autocorrelation function is $$\rho_h = \phi^h = 0.7^h$$ — it **decays geometrically** (never cuts off), shrinking as $$0.7, 0.49, 0.343, \dots$$
2. The partial autocorrelation function **cuts off after lag 1** (more generally, after lag $$p$$ for an AR($$p$$)): once the direct lag-1 dependence is accounted for, there is no additional partial correlation at higher lags.

Why A is correct: AR ⇒ ACF decays, PACF cuts off (after lag $$p = 1$$).

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** describes an **MA(1)** (ACF cuts off, PACF decays) — the mirror image of AR.
- **C** describes an **ARMA** process (both decay) — not a pure AR.
- **D** "both cut off" matches no standard process.

📌 Exam tip: Identification table — AR($$p$$): ACF decays, PACF cuts off after $$p$$. MA($$q$$): ACF cuts off after $$q$$, PACF decays. ARMA: both decay. Remember "PACF cuts for AR; ACF cuts for MA."

**Q6.** An AR(1) interest-rate process has $$\delta = 0.6$$, $$\phi = 0.85$$, and shock variance $$\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 0.16$$. What are the unconditional variance of the series and the lag-2 autocorrelation $$\rho_2$$?

- A. Var ≈ 0.577; $$\rho_2 = 0.7225$$
- B. Var ≈ 0.160; $$\rho_2 = 0.7225$$
- C. Var ≈ 0.577; $$\rho_2 = 0.85$$
- D. Var ≈ 0.188; $$\rho_2 = 0.425$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the unconditional variance and the lag-2 ACF of an AR(1).

1. Unconditional variance:
$$\mathrm{Var}(Y) = \frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{1 - \phi^2} = \frac{0.16}{1 - 0.85^2} = \frac{0.16}{1 - 0.7225} = \frac{0.16}{0.2775} = 0.5766 \approx 0.577$$
2. Lag-2 autocorrelation:
$$\rho_2 = \phi^2 = 0.85^2 = 0.7225$$

Why A is correct: $$0.16/0.2775 = 0.577$$ and $$\rho_2 = 0.85^2 = 0.7225$$.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** reports the raw shock variance (0.16) as the series variance — forgets to divide by $$(1-\phi^2)$$.
- **C** has the right variance but reports $$\rho_2 = \phi = 0.85$$ instead of $$\phi^2$$ — that is $$\rho_1$$, not $$\rho_2$$.
- **D** mishandles both: divides by $$(1-\phi)$$ instead of $$(1-\phi^2)$$ and uses $$\phi/2$$ for $$\rho_2$$.

📌 Exam tip: AR(1) variance is $$\sigma_\epsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)$$ — note $$\phi^2$$, not $$\phi$$. The ACF is $$\rho_h = \phi^h$$, so $$\rho_1 = \phi$$, $$\rho_2 = \phi^2$$, etc.

**Q7.** An analyst examines the sample ACF of a series with $$T = 100$$ observations and finds $$\hat\rho_1 = 0.26$$, $$\hat\rho_2 = 0.05$$, $$\hat\rho_3 = 0.03$$, with all higher lags near zero; the sample PACF shows a single large spike at lag 1 and negligible values thereafter. Which model is most consistent with this pattern, and what is the white-noise significance band for individual autocorrelations?

- A. AR(1); band is $$\pm 0.196$$ — only $$\hat\rho_1$$ is significant
- B. MA(1); band is $$\pm 0.196$$ — only $$\hat\rho_1$$ is significant
- C. AR(1); band is $$\pm 0.098$$ — $$\hat\rho_1$$ and $$\hat\rho_2$$ are significant
- D. MA(2); band is $$\pm 0.0196$$ — all three are significant

**Answer: A**

We are asked to identify the model from the ACF/PACF shape and to compute the significance band.

1. The **PACF shows a single spike at lag 1** then dies out ⇒ PACF *cuts off* after lag 1. The **ACF decays** (0.26, then smaller values). PACF cutting off + ACF decaying is the signature of an **AR(1)**.
2. Under the white-noise null, the standard error of each sample autocorrelation is $$1/\sqrt{T}$$, so the 95% band is:
$$\pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{T}} = \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{100}} = \pm \frac{1.96}{10} = \pm 0.196$$
3. Only $$\hat\rho_1 = 0.26$$ exceeds 0.196 in magnitude; $$\hat\rho_2 = 0.05$$ and $$\hat\rho_3 = 0.03$$ are inside the band (insignificant).

Why A is correct: PACF cuts off after lag 1 ⇒ AR(1); band $$\pm 1.96/\sqrt{100} = \pm 0.196$$; only $$\hat\rho_1$$ is significant.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** picks MA(1), but MA(1) has the **ACF** cut off and PACF decay — here it is the PACF that cuts off, so it is AR.
- **C** correctly identifies AR(1) but computes the band as $$1.96/(2\sqrt{T})$$ or $$1/\sqrt{T} \times$$ ... = 0.098, wrongly making $$\hat\rho_2$$ significant.
- **D** uses MA(2) and divides by $$T$$ instead of $$\sqrt{T}$$ ($$1.96/100 \approx 0.0196$$), badly understating the band.

📌 Exam tip: PACF spike that cuts off ⇒ AR; ACF spike that cuts off ⇒ MA. The individual-autocorrelation significance band under white noise is $$\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}$$ — note the square root of $$T$$.

**Q8.** Which of the following is a requirement for a series $$\{Y_t\}$$ to be covariance-stationary, and which property is NOT required?

- A. Required: autocovariances depend only on the lag (not on $$t$$), with constant finite mean and variance. Not required: that the series be normally distributed or independent over time
- B. Required: the series is independent and identically distributed. Not required: constant variance
- C. Required: the series has zero mean and is normally distributed. Not required: finite variance
- D. Required: autocovariances grow with $$t$$. Not required: a constant mean

**Answer: A**

We are asked to identify the genuine covariance-stationarity conditions versus non-requirements.

1. Covariance stationarity requires exactly three things:
   - Constant finite mean: $$E[Y_t] = \mu$$ for all $$t$$;
   - Constant finite variance: $$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = \gamma_0 < \infty$$ for all $$t$$;
   - Autocovariance depending only on the lag $$h$$: $$\mathrm{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-h}) = \gamma_h$$, independent of $$t$$.
2. Normality and independence are **not** required. White noise, for instance, is uncorrelated but need not be independent or normal; a series can be covariance-stationary without being Gaussian or iid.

Why A is correct: it lists the three conditions and correctly excludes normality/independence.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** demands iid — that is far stronger than covariance stationarity (which only restricts the first two moments and the autocovariance structure), and it wrongly drops the constant-variance requirement.
- **C** requires zero mean and normality — neither is needed (the mean must be constant, not zero), and it wrongly says finite variance is optional (it is required).
- **D** has autocovariances *growing with $$t$$* — that is the opposite of stationarity — and wrongly drops the constant-mean condition.

📌 Exam tip: Covariance stationarity = constant mean, constant finite variance, and autocovariances that depend only on lag. It is a statement about the first two moments only — normality and independence are stronger conditions that are NOT required.
