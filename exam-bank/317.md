**Q1.** A 1-year corporate bond (face $100) pays a 10% annual coupon and trades at $98. The 1-year probability of default is 6%; in default the bond recovers 35% of **face value** and pays no coupon. The bond's expected return over the year is closest to:
- A. 12.24%
- B. 7.65%
- C. 5.61%
- D. 9.69%

**Answer: B**

Restate: compute the probability-weighted expected return, not the promised yield.

1. **Payoff if no default:** principal + coupon $$= 100 + 10 = 110.$$
2. **Payoff if default:** $$0.35 \times 100 = 35$$ (recovery on **face**, no coupon).
3. **Expected payoff:**
$$E[\text{payoff}] = 0.94 \times 110 + 0.06 \times 35 = 103.40 + 2.10 = 105.50.$$
4. **Expected return** (divide by current **price**, not face):
$$E[r] = \frac{105.50}{98} - 1 = 1.076531 - 1 = 0.07653 = 7.65\%.$$

Why B is correct: the expected return weights both states and divides by the $98 paid today, giving 7.65% — well below the promised yield.

- A (12.24%) is the **promised yield**, $$110/98 - 1 = 12.24\%$$ — the headline trap; it assumes no default.
- D (9.69%) divides the expected payoff by **face** ($105.50/100 − 1 ≈ 5.5%) or mishandles the coupon — a common base-value slip.
- C (5.61%) recovers on **price** instead of face, understating the default payoff and the expected return.

📌 Exam tip: Expected return weights *all* states (default recovers a fraction of **face**, no coupon) and divides by the **price paid**. It is always **below** the promised yield (12.24% here).

**Q2.** A 5-year bond pays an 8% annual coupon (face $100) and trades at $112. Which ordering of its yield measures is correct?
- A. Coupon rate < current yield < yield to maturity
- B. Yield to maturity < current yield < coupon rate
- C. Current yield < yield to maturity < coupon rate
- D. Yield to maturity < coupon rate < current yield

**Answer: B**

Restate: rank coupon rate, current yield, and YTM for a **premium** bond (price > face).

1. **Coupon rate** $$= 8\%$$ (fixed, on face).
2. **Current yield** $$= \frac{\text{annual coupon}}{\text{price}} = \frac{8}{112} = 7.14\%.$$
3. **YTM** must be **below** the current yield for a premium bond, because the investor also suffers a capital **loss** as the price pulls to par (100) at maturity. Approximate:
$$y \approx \frac{C + (F-P)/n}{(F+P)/2} = \frac{8 + (100-112)/5}{(100+112)/2} = \frac{8 - 2.4}{106} = \frac{5.6}{106} = 5.28\%.$$
4. Ordering: $$\text{YTM }(5.28\%) < \text{current yield }(7.14\%) < \text{coupon }(8\%).$$

Why B is correct: for a **premium** bond the relationship reverses the discount-bond ordering; the pull-to-par loss drags YTM below both.

- A is the **discount-bond** ordering (coupon < CY < YTM) — wrong direction for a premium bond.
- C and D scramble the ranks.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize — discount bond: coupon < CY < YTM; **par**: all equal; **premium**: YTM < CY < coupon. The capital gain/loss to par drives the ranking.

**Q3.** An issuer wants to redeem an outstanding bond issue early but minimize the windfall it captures from falling rates, so it sets the early-redemption price equal to the present value of the bond's remaining cash flows discounted at the relevant Treasury rate plus a small spread. This provision is a:
- A. Sinking fund (doubling option)
- B. Make-whole call
- C. Standard fixed-price call
- D. Defeasance

**Answer: B**

Restate: identify the retirement mechanism described by a PV-based, Treasury-plus-spread redemption price.

Why B is correct: a **make-whole call** sets the call price as the PV of remaining cash flows at the Treasury rate plus a small spread. Because that price tracks rates, the issuer captures little refunding gain when rates fall — exactly the feature described.

- A (sinking fund/doubling option) retires a *portion* periodically at par, not the whole issue at a PV-based price.
- C (standard fixed-price call) uses a stated call price (often starting above par, declining toward par) — independent of current Treasury rates, giving the issuer a real refunding windfall when rates drop.
- D (defeasance) deposits Treasuries in trust to replicate cash flows and extinguish the obligation; it does not "redeem" the bond at a price.

📌 Exam tip: "PV of remaining cash flows at Treasury + spread" is the signature of a **make-whole** call — designed so the issuer rarely profits from refunding.

**Q4.** Which statement about default rates and recovery rates across the credit cycle is **correct**?
- A. Recovery rates are positively correlated with default rates, so losses in recessions are limited.
- B. For low-rated bonds, the marginal (hazard) default rate tends to increase with time since issue, while for investment-grade bonds it tends to decrease.
- C. The default loss rate is approximately the default rate multiplied by (1 − recovery rate).
- D. Recovery rates are independent of seniority because all creditors share pari passu.

**Answer: C**

Restate: identify the accurate empirical/definitional statement.

Why C is correct: $$\text{default loss rate} \approx \text{default rate} \times (1 - \text{recovery rate}).$$ This is the standard decomposition of expected credit loss into frequency and severity.

- A reverses the empirical sign: recovery rates are **negatively** correlated with default rates — in recessions defaults spike *and* recoveries fall, compounding losses.
- B reverses the two hazard patterns: for **investment-grade** issuers the marginal default rate tends to **rise** with age (they can drift down); for **low-rated** issuers it tends to **fall** (survivors prove themselves).
- D is false: recovery depends heavily on **seniority and security** — first-mortgage/senior secured recover most, subordinated least.

📌 Exam tip: Two regularities to lock in: (1) recovery and default rates are *negatively* correlated; (2) marginal default rates *rise* with age for high grades, *fall* with age for low grades.

**Q5.** A speculative-grade issuer is structured so it pays **no cash coupons** for the first three years; instead it may issue additional bonds to satisfy coupon obligations during that period. This bond is best described as a:
- A. Step-up bond
- B. Deferred-interest (zero-coupon) bond
- C. Payment-in-kind (PIK) bond
- D. Equipment trust certificate

**Answer: C**

Restate: identify the high-yield structure where coupons can be paid **in more bonds** rather than cash.

Why C is correct: a **payment-in-kind (PIK)** bond lets the issuer pay coupons by issuing additional bonds instead of cash — preserving cash early in a leveraged deal.

- A (step-up) pays cash coupons that start low and **increase on a schedule** — it does pay cash, just rising amounts.
- B (deferred-interest/zero-coupon) pays **no coupons at all** for an initial period and is sold at a deep discount — it does not satisfy coupons by issuing new bonds.
- D (equipment trust certificate) is a *secured* investment-grade-style structure (title to equipment held by trustee), unrelated to coupon deferral.

📌 Exam tip: PIK = "pay in more bonds"; step-up = "rising cash coupons"; deferred-interest/zero = "no coupons, deep discount." The phrase "issue additional bonds to pay coupons" uniquely flags PIK.

**Q6.** A weak company's **first-mortgage bond** and a financially strong company's **subordinated debenture** are compared. Which statement is correct?
- A. The first-mortgage bond is necessarily safer because collateral always outranks an unsecured claim.
- B. Collateral type ranks claims *within* an issuer; the strong company's debenture can still be safer than the weak company's secured bond.
- C. A debenture is a secured claim and therefore comparable in safety to a mortgage bond.
- D. Subordinated debentures recover most in default because they carry the highest coupons.

**Answer: B**

Restate: assess cross-issuer comparisons of secured vs. unsecured debt.

Why B is correct: collateral and seniority rank claims **within a single issuer's** capital structure — they do not transfer safety across issuers. A debenture of a strong issuer can easily be safer than a secured bond of a weak issuer, because the strong issuer is far less likely to default in the first place.

- A overgeneralizes — security helps only *given* a default; it doesn't lower the *probability* of default, which depends on the issuer's overall credit.
- C is factually wrong: a **debenture is unsecured** (backed only by the issuer's general credit).
- D is wrong: high coupons do not raise recovery; recovery rises with **seniority and security**, and subordinated debt recovers **least**.

📌 Exam tip: "Debenture = unsecured" in US usage. Collateral ranks claims *within* an issuer, not *across* issuers — issuer credit quality dominates the default-probability comparison.

**Q7.** A B-rated bond is quoted as "yielding 9%." An analyst tells a client this means the expected return is 9%. Why is this **incorrect**?
- A. The 9% is the current yield, which always equals the expected return.
- B. The 9% is a promised yield conditional on full payment; expected return is lower because it weights in default with recovery, and part of the spread compensates for systematic credit risk.
- C. The 9% is the expected return, but the promised yield is even lower.
- D. Expected return exceeds promised yield for high-yield bonds because of the higher coupons.

**Answer: B**

Restate: explain why a quoted (promised) yield overstates the expected return on a defaultable bond.

Why B is correct: the quoted 9% is the **promised yield (YTM)** — the return *if* all promised cash flows arrive. The **expected return** probability-weights the payoffs including default with recovery, so it is **below** the promised yield. Moreover, part of the spread over Treasuries compensates for **systematic (non-diversifiable) credit risk**, not merely expected loss.

- A is wrong: 9% is the promised yield, not the current yield, and neither equals expected return for a risky bond.
- C inverts the relationship — promised yield is **higher** than expected return, not lower.
- D is backwards: for any defaultable bond, expected return is **below** promised yield; higher coupons don't reverse this.

📌 Exam tip: Promised yield ≥ expected return always for risky debt. The gap is expected loss; the *remaining* spread over Treasuries is partly a premium for systematic credit risk — never quote promised yield as expected return.
