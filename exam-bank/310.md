**Q1.** A non-dividend-paying stock trades at $80. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is 5% for all maturities. A 9-month forward on the stock is quoted in the market at $84.50. Identify the arbitrage and the riskless profit per share (using cost-of-carry pricing).
- A. No arbitrage; the forward is fairly priced
- B. Cash-and-carry; profit ≈ $1.45 per share
- C. Reverse cash-and-carry; profit ≈ $1.45 per share
- D. Cash-and-carry; profit ≈ $4.50 per share

**Answer: B**

The ask: price the no-income forward, compare to the market quote, and find the arbitrage profit per share.

1. No-income forward price:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{rT} = 80\,e^{0.05 \times 0.75} = 80\,e^{0.0375} = 80 \times 1.038211 = 83.0569$$

2. Market forward $84.50 > fair $83.0569 → the forward is *over-priced*. The arbitrage is **cash-and-carry**: borrow $80, buy the stock, short the forward at 84.50.

3. At T (9 months): deliver the share, receive $84.50; repay the loan 80 × e^{0.0375} = $83.0569.
   Profit = 84.50 − 83.0569 = **$1.4431 ≈ $1.45 per share**.

- **A** is wrong: 84.50 ≠ 83.06, so arbitrage exists.
- **C** (reverse cash-and-carry) is the wrong direction — you short the asset and buy the forward only when the forward is *under*-priced (F_mkt < F_0). Here the forward is too high.
- **D** ($4.50) just takes (84.50 − 80) ignoring the cost of carry — it forgets to grow the spot at the risk-free rate.

📌 Exam tip: F_0 = S_0·e^{rT} (no income). If market F > fair F ⇒ cash-and-carry (borrow, buy spot, short forward). If market F < fair F ⇒ reverse cash-and-carry. Profit = |F_mkt − F_0|.

**Q2.** A stock trades at $60 and will pay dividends of $1.20 in 4 months and $1.20 in 10 months. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is 6%. Price a 1-year forward on the stock.
- A. $59.45
- B. $61.26
- C. $61.71
- D. $63.71

**Answer: B**

The ask: price a forward with known dollar income — subtract PV of dividends, then grow at r.

1. PV of dividends:
$$I = 1.20\,e^{-0.06 \times (4/12)} + 1.20\,e^{-0.06 \times (10/12)}$$
$$= 1.20\,e^{-0.02} + 1.20\,e^{-0.05} = 1.20(0.980199) + 1.20(0.951229)$$
$$= 1.176239 + 1.141475 = 2.317714$$

2. Forward price:
$$F_0 = (S_0 - I)\,e^{rT} = (60 - 2.317714)\,e^{0.06 \times 1} = 57.682286 \times 1.061837 = 61.256 \approx \$61.26$$

So F₀ ≈ **$61.26**, option **B**.

- **A** ($59.45) subtracts the dividends but forgets to grow the net at e^{rT} (or grows the wrong base).
- **C** ($61.71) discounts the dividends incorrectly (subtracts a smaller PV, e.g., one dividend only), nudging the price up.
- **D** ($63.71) ignores dividends entirely: 60 × e^{0.06} = 63.71 — the classic "no-income" trap on an income-paying stock.

📌 Exam tip: With known cash income, subtract the *present value* of dividends from S₀ first, THEN grow at e^{rT}. F₀ = (S₀ − I)e^{rT}. Forgetting to discount dividends, or ignoring them, are the two standard distractors.

**Q3.** A stock index stands at 2,500 with a continuous dividend yield of 2.5%; the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 4.5%. The fair 6-month index futures price is closest to:
- A. 2,474.4
- B. 2,500.0
- C. 2,525.1
- D. 2,556.8

**Answer: C**

The ask: price index futures using the known-yield formula.

1. Known-yield (dividend yield q) forward/futures price:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - q)T} = 2500\,e^{(0.045 - 0.025)\times 0.5} = 2500\,e^{0.01}$$

2. Compute: e^{0.01} = 1.010050. F₀ = 2500 × 1.010050 = **2525.13 ≈ 2,525.1**.

- **A** (2,474.4) uses e^{−0.01} — flips the sign of (r − q), the direction trap.
- **B** (2,500) ignores the net carry entirely.
- **D** (2,556.8) uses e^{(r + q)T} = e^{0.035×... } or adds the yield instead of subtracting — overstates carry.

📌 Exam tip: Index futures: F₀ = S₀·e^{(r − q)T}. Subtract the dividend yield from r. Quick check: (r − q)T = 0.01 ⇒ ~1% above spot, so ≈ 2,525.

**Q4.** Eight months ago an investor went **long** a 1-year forward on a non-dividend stock when the spot was $50 and r = 4% (continuous), so the delivery price K was locked in. Today the stock trades at $54, r is unchanged, and 4 months remain. The value of the long forward position today is closest to:
- A. $2.00
- B. $2.65
- C. $2.68
- D. $4.00

**Answer: B**

The ask: find K from inception, the current forward price, then value the long position.

1. Delivery price set at inception (original term = 1 year):
$$K = 50\,e^{0.04 \times 1} = 50 \times 1.040811 = 52.0405$$

2. Current forward price (4 months = 1/3 year remain):
$$F = 54\,e^{0.04 \times (1/3)} = 54\,e^{0.013333} = 54 \times 1.013423 = 54.7248$$

3. Value of the long position:
$$f = (F - K)e^{-rT} = (54.7248 - 52.0405)\,e^{-0.04 \times (1/3)} = 2.6843 \times 0.986756 = 2.6488 \approx \$2.65$$

Check via spot: f = S₀ − K·e^{−rT} = 54 − 52.0405 × 0.986756 = 54 − 51.3514 = $2.6486. ✓ So f ≈ **$2.65**, option **B**.

- **A** ($2.00) discounts K over the full original year instead of the remaining 4 months (mis-timed discount).
- **C** ($2.68) is the *undiscounted* difference F − K = 54.7248 − 52.0405 = 2.6843 — it forgets the e^{−rT} factor on the remaining maturity.
- **D** ($4.00) just takes S₀ − S₀_initial = 54 − 50, ignoring the cost of carry embedded in K.

📌 Exam tip: Value of a long forward = (F − K)·e^{−rT}, where K is the *locked-in* delivery price (from inception) and F is today's forward for the *remaining* maturity. Equivalent: f = S₀ − K·e^{−rT} for a no-income asset. Discount over the *remaining* time, not the original term.

**Q5.** You are **short** a forward contract with delivery price K = $145 on a non-dividend-paying asset. The current forward price for the same remaining maturity is F = $150; r = 5% (continuous) and 6 months remain. The value of your short position today is closest to:
- A. +$4.88
- B. −$4.88
- C. +$5.00
- D. −$5.00

**Answer: B**

The ask: value a *short* forward position.

1. Long-position value: f_long = (F − K)e^{−rT} = (150 − 145)e^{−0.05×0.5} = 5 × e^{−0.025} = 5 × 0.975310 = $4.8765.

2. A short position is worth the negative of the long: f_short = −(F − K)e^{−rT} = −$4.88.

3. Intuition: you agreed to *sell* at $145, but the forward price has risen to $150 — selling cheap is a loss, so the short position has negative value. Answer: **B (−$4.88)**.

- **A** (+$4.88) is the *long* value, not the short — the sign trap.
- **D** (−$5.00) forgets to discount (uses F − K = $5 undiscounted).
- **C** (+$5.00) is both undiscounted and wrong-signed.

📌 Exam tip: Short forward value = −(F − K)·e^{−rT}. When the forward price rises above your locked-in K, the short loses (negative value). Always discount (F − K) over the remaining maturity.

**Q6.** Which statement about the relationship between forward and futures prices is correct?
- A. With deterministic interest rates, futures prices always exceed forward prices
- B. When the underlying asset price is strongly positively correlated with interest rates, futures prices tend to exceed forward prices
- C. When the underlying asset price is strongly positively correlated with interest rates, forward prices tend to exceed futures prices
- D. The forward-futures difference is largest for very short-dated contracts

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct forward-vs-futures relationship.

1. With *deterministic* rates, forward and futures prices are **equal** (so A is false).
2. With strong *positive* correlation between the asset price and interest rates: when the asset rises, the futures long receives daily settlement gains *and* rates are high, so those gains are reinvested at high rates; when the asset falls, losses are financed at low rates. This daily-settlement timing benefit makes **futures > forwards**. Answer: **B**.

- **A** is false: equal under deterministic rates, not "always exceed."
- **C** has the correlation right but the inequality backwards (positive correlation favors *futures*, not forwards).
- **D** is false: the gap is *small* for short maturities and matters for *long*-dated contracts.

📌 Exam tip: Deterministic rates ⇒ forward = futures. Positive asset-rate correlation ⇒ futures > forwards (reinvest gains at high rates, finance losses at low rates). Negative correlation reverses it. Effect grows with maturity.

**Q7.** A trader observes a 1-year futures on a stock index at 4,120. The index is at 4,000 with a dividend yield of 1.5%; the risk-free rate is 4% (both continuous). Is index arbitrage available, and what is the correct trade?
- A. No arbitrage; the futures is fairly priced
- B. Yes; buy the index basket and short the futures
- C. Yes; short the index basket and buy the futures
- D. Yes; buy both the index basket and the futures

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the fair index futures price, compare, and specify the index-arbitrage trade.

1. Fair futures price:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - q)T} = 4000\,e^{(0.04 - 0.015)\times 1} = 4000\,e^{0.025} = 4000 \times 1.025315 = 4101.26$$

2. Market futures 4,120 > fair 4,101.26 → the futures is *over-priced*. Index arbitrage sells the rich side and buys the cheap side: **short the futures and buy the index basket** (a cash-and-carry on the index). Answer: **B**.

- **A** is wrong: 4,120 ≠ 4,101.3, so arbitrage exists.
- **C** (short basket, buy futures) is the trade for an *under*-priced futures (F_mkt < F_0); here the futures is over-priced, so the direction is reversed.
- **D** (buy both) is not an arbitrage — it doubles directional exposure rather than locking in a riskless spread.

📌 Exam tip: F₀ = S₀·e^{(r−q)T}. If market futures > fair ⇒ buy the basket, short the futures (cash-and-carry the index). If market futures < fair ⇒ short the basket, buy the futures.
