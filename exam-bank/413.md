**Q1.** A portfolio has the following key-rate '01s (KR01s), expressed as the dollar loss per 1 bp rise in each key rate: 2-year = $1,200, 5-year = $2,800, 10-year = $4,500, 30-year = $1,500. Over one day the 2-year rate rises 8 bp, the 5-year rises 4 bp, the 10-year is unchanged, and the 30-year falls 6 bp. The portfolio's approximate P&L is closest to:

- A. −$11,800
- B. −$29,800
- C. +$9,000
- D. −$20,800

**Answer: A**

The ask: compute portfolio P&L as the negative dot product of KR01s and key-rate moves (in bp).

1. Formula: $$\Delta P \approx -\sum_k \text{KR01}_k \times \Delta y_k^{(bp)}$$
2. Compute term by term (a positive KR01 means a LOSS when that rate rises):
   - 2-year: $$-1{,}200 \times 8 = -9{,}600$$
   - 5-year: $$-2{,}800 \times 4 = -11{,}200$$
   - 10-year: $$-4{,}500 \times 0 = 0$$
   - 30-year: $$-1{,}500 \times (-6) = +9{,}000$$ (rate FELL, so a gain)
3. Sum:
   $$-9{,}600 - 11{,}200 + 0 + 9{,}000 = -11{,}800$$

The portfolio loses about **$11,800**: the losses on the rising 2- and 5-year exposures dominate the gain from the falling 30-year.

- B (−$29,800) treats the 30-year move as a rise too, turning its +$9,000 gain into a −$9,000 loss (sign error on the falling rate) and also mishandles signs.
- C (+$9,000) keeps only the 30-year gain and drops the dominant 2- and 5-year losses.
- D (−$20,800) ignores the offsetting 30-year gain entirely (treats it as zero).

📌 Exam tip: P&L from a set of key-rate moves is a dot product: $$-\sum \text{KR01}_k \Delta y_k$$. A rate that FALLS flips the sign of its term to a gain. Track signs rate by rate.

**Q2.** A portfolio has key-rate durations of 0.6 (2-year), 1.4 (5-year), 3.0 (10-year), and 2.2 (30-year). Holding all else equal, its effective (parallel-shift) duration is approximately:

- A. 3.0
- B. 7.2
- C. 1.8
- D. Cannot be determined from key-rate durations

**Answer: B**

The ask: recover effective duration from a set of key-rate durations.

1. Key-rate shifts are constructed so their sum reproduces a parallel 1 bp shift. Therefore the key-rate durations sum to the effective duration:
   $$\sum_k D_k \approx D_{Eff}$$
2. Add them:
   $$0.6 + 1.4 + 3.0 + 2.2 = 7.2$$

- A (3.0) takes only the largest single KRD (the 10-year) — that captures one segment, not the parallel response.
- C (1.8) is the average of the four KRDs — averaging is wrong; they ADD.
- D is incorrect: the sum-to-total property makes the parallel duration fully determined by the KRDs.

📌 Exam tip: Key-rate durations SUM to the effective (parallel-shift) duration because the individual tent-shaped shifts add up to a parallel shift. Don't average them.

**Q3.** In the standard four-point (2/5/10/30-year) key-rate framework, the 5-year key-rate shift is best described as:

- A. A 1 bp parallel move in all rates from 0 to 5 years.
- B. A 1 bp move at the 5-year point, declining linearly to zero at the 2-year and 10-year points.
- C. A 1 bp move at the 5-year point only, with all other maturities unchanged.
- D. A 1 bp move in all forward rates between years 2 and 10.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the shape of a single key-rate shift.

1. Each key-rate shift is "tent-shaped": it peaks at 1 bp at its own key maturity and decays LINEARLY to zero at the two adjacent key rates.
2. For the 5-year shift, the adjacent key rates are the 2-year and the 10-year, so the shift is 1 bp at 5 years, tapering linearly to 0 at both 2 and 10 years, and zero outside that span.
3. The four such tents sum to a parallel 1 bp shift across the curve.

- A describes a block/step shift over a maturity range, not the tent shape.
- C describes a single-point spike (a partial '01 style isolated bump), not the tapered key-rate shift.
- D describes a forward-bucket '01, which shifts forward rates within a bucket — a different decomposition.

📌 Exam tip: Key-rate shifts are tent-shaped — peak at the key maturity, linear decay to zero at neighboring key rates, flat beyond the end points. This shape is exactly why they sum to a parallel shift.

**Q4.** A portfolio has KR01s of $600 at the 5-year key rate and $450 at the 10-year key rate (it loses these amounts per 1 bp rise in each rate). The available hedge bonds have the following KR01s per $1 million of face:

| | 5-year KR01 | 10-year KR01 |
|---|---|---|
| Bond A (5-yr note) | $48 | $6 |
| Bond B (10-yr note) | $0 | $75 |

The face amounts to SHORT to neutralize both key-rate exposures are closest to:

- A. Short $12.5M of A and $5.0M of B
- B. Short $12.5M of A and $6.0M of B
- C. Short $9.4M of A and $5.0M of B
- D. Short $6.0M of A and $5.6M of B

**Answer: A**

The ask: solve the triangular system so the hedge's KR01s match the portfolio's at both key rates.

1. Let $$F_A, F_B$$ be the face amounts (in $M) shorted. The hedge must supply KR01s of $600 (5-yr) and $450 (10-yr):
   - 5-year: $$48 F_A + 0 \cdot F_B = 600$$
   - 10-year: $$6 F_A + 75 F_B = 450$$
2. The first equation has only one unknown:
   $$F_A = \frac{600}{48} = 12.5$$
3. Back-substitute into the second:
   $$6(12.5) + 75 F_B = 450 \;\Rightarrow\; 75 + 75 F_B = 450 \;\Rightarrow\; 75 F_B = 375 \;\Rightarrow\; F_B = 5.0$$
4. Short **$12.5M of Bond A** and **$5.0M of Bond B**.

- B ($6.0M of B) forgets to subtract Bond A's 10-year cross-KR01, solving $$75 F_B = 450 \Rightarrow 6.0$$ instead of $$75 F_B = 375$$.
- C ($9.4M of A) divides $600 by the wrong KR01 (e.g., uses 64 or mishandles units).
- D scrambles both legs, ignoring the triangular structure.

📌 Exam tip: Solve the KR01 hedge system starting with the equation that has only one unknown (the triangular structure), then back-substitute. A positive portfolio KR01 means a loss when the rate rises, so the hedge is a SHORT of matching magnitude.

**Q5.** A trader is long a 10-year bullet bond and has hedged it to zero net DV01 by shorting a duration-matched 2s/30s barbell. The combined position will most likely LOSE money when:

- A. The yield curve shifts up in parallel by 50 bp.
- B. The yield curve shifts down in parallel by 50 bp.
- C. The curve's curvature changes — the 2-year and 30-year rates fall relative to the 10-year.
- D. Nothing — zero net DV01 means the position is fully hedged.

**Answer: C**

The ask: identify which curve move produces P&L on a DV01-neutral bullet-vs-barbell position.

1. By construction the position has zero net DV01, so PARALLEL shifts (A and B) net to approximately zero P&L — eliminating those.
2. A bullet has cash flows concentrated at 10 years; the barbell has them at 2 and 30 years. The position is therefore short the wings (2y, 30y) and long the belly (10y) — a butterfly exposure.
3. If the wings (2-year and 30-year) rally (fall) relative to the belly, the short barbell legs rise in value (a loss on the short) more than the long bullet — the position loses on this curvature/butterfly move.

- A and B are parallel moves, hedged out by the zero net DV01 — no first-order P&L.
- D is the core misconception the chapter warns against: zero DV01 protects only against parallel moves, NOT curve reshaping.

📌 Exam tip: A DV01-neutral position is immune only to parallel shifts. Bullet-vs-barbell trades carry curvature (butterfly) risk that single-factor DV01 cannot see — this is exactly why key-rate metrics exist.

**Q6.** A portfolio has a 30-year KR01 of +$3,000 (it loses $3,000 per 1 bp rise in the 30-year key rate), with all other KR01s approximately zero. Hedge Bond Z has a 30-year KR01 of $75 per $1 million of face (its other KR01s are approximately zero). The appropriate hedge is to:

- A. Buy $40 million face of Z.
- B. Sell $40 million face of Z.
- C. Sell $4 million face of Z.
- D. Buy $22.5 million face of Z.

**Answer: B**

The ask: determine the direction and size of the hedge for a single positive KR01 exposure.

1. The portfolio has a positive 30-year KR01, meaning it LOSES when the 30-year rate rises. The hedge must GAIN when the 30-year rate rises — i.e., a SHORT position (shorting a bond gains when its rate rises).
2. Size: match the magnitude.
   $$F = \frac{3{,}000}{75} = 40 \text{ (in \$M)}$$
3. Therefore SHORT $40 million face of Bond Z.

- A (buy) doubles the exposure — a long bond also loses when rates rise, adding to the portfolio's loss.
- C ($4M) is off by a factor of 10 (a decimal slip in the division).
- D (buy $22.5M) is both the wrong direction and the wrong size.

📌 Exam tip: A positive KR01 = a loss when that rate rises, so it must be hedged with a SHORT of equal magnitude. Get the direction from the sign convention before computing the size.

**Q7.** A trader uses a regression-based (volatility-weighted) hedge instead of a simple DV01 hedge. Historically, when the 30-year yield (the hedge instrument's yield) moves 1 bp, the position's 20-year yield moves 0.85 bp. The position has DV01 of $10,000 and the 30-year hedge bond has DV01 of $80 per contract-equivalent. The number of hedge units (rounded) is closest to:

- A. 147
- B. 125
- C. 106
- D. 100

**Answer: C**

The ask: apply the beta-adjusted (regression) hedge ratio rather than the plain DV01 ratio.

1. Volatility-weighted hedge formula:
   $$N = \frac{\text{DV01}_{position}}{\text{DV01}_{hedge}} \times \beta$$
   where β is the regression slope = change in the POSITION's yield per 1 bp change in the HEDGE's yield = 0.85.
2. Plain DV01 ratio:
   $$\frac{10{,}000}{80} = 125$$
3. Scale by β:
   $$N = 125 \times 0.85 = 106.25 \approx 106$$

- B (125) is the plain DV01 hedge — it ignores the β adjustment, over-hedging because the 20-year moves less than one-for-one with the 30-year.
- A (147) divides by a wrong DV01 or multiplies by 1/β instead of β.
- D (100) is an unmotivated round number.

📌 Exam tip: When the position and hedge yields do not move one-for-one, scale the DV01 hedge by the regression β (position yield change per unit hedge yield change). A β below 1 means you need FEWER hedge units than the plain DV01 ratio suggests.
