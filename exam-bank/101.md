**Q1.** A consumer-lending bank reports the following on a $500 million credit card book: average annual charge-offs over the cycle of 4.0%, with a one-in-twenty bad-year loss rate of 9.5%. The bank prices the average loss into its APR and holds capital against the worst-case stress. Which statement is the most accurate characterization of how these losses should be funded?

- A. Expected loss is $20 million, funded by capital; the additional $27.5 million in a bad year is funded by loan-loss provisions
- B. Expected loss is $20 million, funded by provisions priced into the APR; the additional $27.5 million in a bad year is unexpected loss funded by capital
- C. Expected loss is $47.5 million, funded by provisions; unexpected loss is zero because the worst case is already known
- D. Expected loss is $20 million, funded by capital; unexpected loss is $47.5 million, funded by provisions

**Answer: B**

This problem tests the expected-loss vs. unexpected-loss distinction *and* which funding source matches each. It is a near-universal exam trap to swap "capital" and "provisions."

1. Compute expected loss (EL): the average, anticipated loss.
$$EL = 4.0\% \times \$500\text{m} = \$20\text{m}$$
2. EL is a *predictable cost of doing business* — it is priced into the APR and covered by **reserves/provisions**, not capital.
3. Compute the bad-year loss: $$9.5\% \times \$500\text{m} = \$47.5\text{m}$$.
4. Unexpected loss (UL) is the *variability around* the expected level — the amount the bad year exceeds the average:
$$UL = \$47.5\text{m} - \$20\text{m} = \$27.5\text{m}$$
5. UL is the genuine risk and is absorbed by **capital**, the buffer for losses worse than expected.

So EL = $20m (provisions, priced in) and the extra $27.5m is UL (capital). That is option B.

- A swaps the funding sources (EL by capital, UL by provisions) — backwards.
- C wrongly calls the entire $47.5m bad-year figure "expected loss" and claims UL is zero; knowing a worst case exists does not make it expected, and the *variability* is precisely the risk.
- D both swaps funding sources and treats the full $47.5m as UL (it forgot to subtract the EL already covered).

📌 Exam tip: EL = cost = priced in = provisions/reserves; UL = risk = capital. UL is the *excess over* EL, so always subtract EL from the stress loss — never use the gross stress number as UL.

**Q2.** A money-center bank holds a large position in a single emerging-market corporate bond. During a regional crisis the following chain occurs: the bond's market price drops sharply; the bank is unable to sell the position without accepting a large discount; the mark-to-market loss triggers a margin call from a repo counterparty; the bank cannot raise enough cash to meet the call and is forced to liquidate other assets at depressed prices. Which sequence correctly identifies the primary risk type at each stage?

- A. Market risk → funding liquidity risk → market liquidity risk → credit risk
- B. Market risk → market liquidity risk → funding liquidity risk → market liquidity risk
- C. Credit risk → market liquidity risk → operational risk → market risk
- D. Market risk → credit risk → funding liquidity risk → reputation risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to map each stage of a 2008-style loss spiral to the correct risk class, distinguishing the two liquidity risks.

1. "The bond's market price drops sharply" — a loss from a move in market price = **market risk**.
2. "Unable to sell without a large discount" — the inability to exit a position without moving the price = **market (trading) liquidity risk**.
3. "Cannot raise enough cash to meet the [margin] call" — the inability to roll/raise cash to meet obligations = **funding liquidity risk**.
4. "Forced to liquidate other assets at depressed prices" — again being forced to sell into a thin market at fire-sale prices = **market liquidity risk**.

That ordering is market → market liquidity → funding liquidity → market liquidity, which is B.

- A mislabels stage 2 as funding and stage 3 as market liquidity (the two are swapped) and tacks on credit risk that never appears.
- C calls a price move "credit risk" and the forced sale "market risk," and invents operational risk.
- D introduces credit risk (no counterparty has defaulted — the loss is from price moves) and reputation risk (not the primary driver of the forced sale).

📌 Exam tip: funding liquidity = *can't get cash*; market/trading liquidity = *can't sell without moving the price*. In a loss spiral the two feed each other repeatedly — read each clause and match the verb ("raise cash" vs. "sell at a discount").

**Q3.** A bank sells deposit-insured retail deposits and uses the funds to write out-of-the-money options to corporate clients. Traders are paid a percentage of annual P&L with no clawback and no charge for the risk capital they consume. A risk officer warns that this structure simultaneously embeds two distinct agency problems. Which pair is present, and through which channel?

- A. Adverse selection (deposit insurance) and basis risk (the options) — both pre-contract pricing problems
- B. Moral hazard (deposit insurance shields the bank from depositor discipline) and moral hazard (uncapped bonus encourages excess risk after the contract is set)
- C. Adverse selection (uncapped bonus attracts the riskiest traders) and moral hazard (deposit insurance)
- D. Moral hazard (deposit insurance) and adverse selection (option buyers are the most informed counterparties)

**Answer: B**

The question asks which agency problems the structure creates and the channel for each. The key distinction: *adverse selection* is a pre-contract problem (the riskiest types self-select before the deal); *moral hazard* is a post-contract behavior change (the protected party takes more risk once protected).

1. Deposit insurance: once deposits are insured, depositors no longer monitor or discipline the bank, so the bank can take more risk than it otherwise could. The risk-taking change happens *after* protection is in place → **moral hazard**.
2. Uncapped bonus with no clawback: the trader keeps the upside but bears none of the downside, so *after* the comp contract is signed the trader takes excessive risk → **moral hazard**.

Both channels are moral hazard, which is option B.

- A wrongly calls deposit insurance "adverse selection" and reframes the comp problem as basis risk (a hedging-mismatch concept, not an agency problem here).
- C and D each correctly tag one channel but mislabel the other as adverse selection. The uncapped bonus is not primarily about *which* traders apply (selection); it changes the *behavior* of whoever holds it — that is hazard, not selection.

📌 Exam tip: ask "did the bad outcome come from *who* entered the deal (selection) or from *how they behaved after* (hazard)?" Both deposit insurance and asymmetric-payoff comp are classic moral-hazard examples because they alter behavior post-protection.

**Q4.** An enterprise risk team aggregates firm-wide VaR by summing the standalone VaRs of its trading, banking, and insurance units, citing diversification as the reason the true figure should be lower. During the next crisis the realized firm-wide loss exceeds even the simple sum of standalone VaRs. Which explanation is most consistent with the curriculum's treatment of risk aggregation?

- A. VaR is subadditive, so the sum of standalone VaRs is always an upper bound that cannot be exceeded
- B. Diversification benefits assumed in calm markets shrink as cross-unit correlations rise toward 1 in a crisis, so the realized loss can exceed even the undiversified sum
- C. The units measured VaR on the same horizon and confidence level, so summation must have understated the figure
- D. Expected loss was double-counted, inflating the realized number above the sum of VaRs

**Answer: B**

The question asks why a realized crisis loss can blow through even the *un*-diversified sum of standalone VaRs.

1. In normal times, units' losses are imperfectly correlated, so combined VaR is *below* the sum of standalone VaRs — the diversification benefit. The team was counting on this.
2. The curriculum stresses that **correlations are unstable and rise toward 1 in crises**. As correlations spike, the assumed diversification evaporates.
3. If tail dependence is strong enough, all units lose together *and* by more than the individually-estimated tails (fat tails, model breakdown), so the realized loss can exceed even the simple sum — diversification not only disappears, the joint tail is worse than modeled.

That is option B.

- A claims VaR is always subadditive and the sum is an unbreachable upper bound. VaR is *not* generally subadditive (a known limitation), and in any case a *realized* loss is not bounded by a *modeled* VaR sum.
- C is irrelevant and backwards — using a common horizon would make summation cleaner, not cause an understatement, and it does not explain a crisis breach.
- D invents double-counting of EL; VaR is about unexpected loss and the breach is a correlation/tail story, not an accounting error.

📌 Exam tip: the danger of risk aggregation is precisely that diversification "benefits assumed in calm markets vanish exactly when needed." Treat any answer asserting VaR is "always subadditive" as a trap.

**Q5.** A firm's CRO is building a risk-tool kit and must match each tool to the job it does *best*. Consider: (i) producing a single comparable, aggregable number across desks; (ii) capturing the *severity* of losses in the extreme tail beyond a threshold; (iii) assessing a brand-new risk for which no probability distribution can yet be estimated. Which assignment is correct?

- A. (i) Expected shortfall; (ii) VaR; (iii) stress test
- B. (i) VaR; (ii) expected shortfall; (iii) qualitative scenario analysis
- C. (i) Scenario analysis; (ii) VaR; (iii) expected shortfall
- D. (i) VaR; (ii) scenario analysis; (iii) expected shortfall

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to match three jobs to the tool best suited to each.

1. Job (i) — one comparable, aggregable number across desks: **VaR** states a loss threshold not exceeded at a given confidence over a given horizon. It is the standard single comparable figure.
2. Job (ii) — severity of losses *beyond* the threshold: **expected shortfall** averages the losses in the tail past the VaR cutoff, capturing tail severity that VaR ignores.
3. Job (iii) — a brand-new risk with no usable probability distribution: **qualitative scenario analysis** (a "what if?" tool) handles new/rare risks without requiring probability estimates; quantitative measures are weak at "unknown unknowns."

That is B.

- A swaps VaR and ES (ES is the tail-severity measure, not the single comparable number).
- C puts scenario analysis where VaR belongs and VaR where ES belongs.
- D correctly tags (i) but assigns the tail-severity job to scenario analysis and the new-risk job to ES — both reversed; ES is quantitative and needs a distribution, so it is poorly suited to an unmodelable new risk.

📌 Exam tip: VaR = the threshold; ES = the *average loss beyond* the threshold (so ES ≥ VaR and captures tail shape). Qualitative tools (scenario analysis, expert judgment) are the answer whenever a question stresses "no probabilities," "novel," or "unprecedented."

**Q6.** A rating agency is paid by the issuers whose securities it rates, and a desk originates loans it intends to sell rather than hold. A junior analyst lists four labels for these arrangements: conflict of interest, moral hazard, adverse selection, and basis risk. Which single concept best unifies *both* arrangements as described, and why?

- A. Adverse selection — in both cases the most informed party withdraws from the market
- B. Conflict of interest — in both cases the party making the risk judgment is rewarded in a way misaligned with the user of that judgment
- C. Basis risk — in both cases the hedge fails to track the underlying exposure
- D. Funding liquidity risk — in both cases the firm cannot raise cash when needed

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the *single* concept that best unifies the issuer-paid rating arrangement and the originate-to-distribute desk.

1. Rating agency paid by issuers: the agency's revenue depends on pleasing the issuer, whose interest (a high rating) diverges from the investor relying on the rating. The judgment-maker's incentives are misaligned with the judgment's user → **conflict of interest**.
2. Originate-to-distribute desk: the originator profits from volume and fees but will not bear the loss it is selling on, so its incentive to underwrite carefully diverges from the buyer's interest → again a **conflict of interest** (with a moral-hazard flavor in the eroded screening incentive).
3. The label that fits *both* cleanly, exactly as the curriculum lists them ("rating agencies paid by issuers; originators selling loans they will not hold"), is **conflict of interest**.

That is B.

- A (adverse selection) does not describe either arrangement — neither says the informed party exits a market at a given price.
- C (basis risk) is a hedging-mismatch concept and has nothing to do with either incentive problem.
- D (funding liquidity) is unrelated; neither scenario is about raising cash.

📌 Exam tip: the curriculum explicitly groups "rating agencies paid by issuers" and "originate-to-distribute" under conflicts of interest. When the misalignment is *who pays the person making the call*, reach for conflict of interest; reserve adverse selection for self-selection at a price and moral hazard for post-protection behavior.

**Q7.** Which of the following statements about the relationship between risk and reward, as framed in the curriculum, is most accurate?

- A. A firm that consistently reports no losses in any year is demonstrating best-practice risk management
- B. Because higher expected return generally requires bearing more risk, a firm earning its cost of capital must be taking essentially zero risk
- C. The risk–reward relationship is observable ex ante in well-functioning markets but is distorted by asymmetric information, moral hazard, and adverse selection
- D. Risk management aims to minimize the firm's total risk so that reward is maximized per unit of remaining exposure

**Answer: C**

The question tests the conceptual link between risk and reward and the frictions that distort it.

1. The curriculum states higher expected return generally requires bearing more risk, and that this relationship is "observable only *ex ante* in well-functioning markets."
2. It then lists the distortions: asymmetric information, moral hazard, adverse selection, and conflicts of interest.
3. Option C states exactly this — ex-ante observability plus the named distortions — so it is correct.

- A is the classic trap the notes flag: "a firm that 'never loses money' is probably not taking enough risk to earn its cost of capital." Zero losses is a warning sign, not best practice.
- B inverts the logic: earning the cost of capital *requires* taking risk, not zero risk.
- D restates the "risk management = minimization" misconception the curriculum explicitly rejects; risk management is deliberate risk *selection* within appetite, not minimization.

📌 Exam tip: two recurring traps live together here — "no losses = good" (false; under-risked) and "risk management = minimize risk" (false; it is selecting and sizing the right risks within appetite). The correct framing always pairs ex-ante risk–reward with the agency/information distortions.
