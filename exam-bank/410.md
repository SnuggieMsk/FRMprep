**Q1.** A rate of 6.00% is quoted with semiannual compounding. The equivalent continuously compounded rate is closest to:

- A. 5.83%
- B. 5.91%
- C. 6.00%
- D. 6.09%

**Answer: B**

The ask: convert a semiannually compounded rate to its continuous-compounding equivalent.

1. Formula: $$r_c = m\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right), \quad m = 2.$$
2. Inner term: $$1 + \frac{0.06}{2} = 1.03.$$
3. Log: $$\ln(1.03) = 0.0295588.$$
4. Multiply: $$r_c = 2 \times 0.0295588 = 0.0591177 = 5.91\%.$$

Why B is correct: more frequent compounding requires a *lower* quoted rate for the same terminal wealth, so the continuous equivalent is below 6.00%.

Distractor traps:
- A (5.83%) over-shrinks the rate (e.g., uses $$m = 4$$ or a log error).
- C (6.00%) leaves the rate unchanged — ignores that the convention matters.
- D (6.09%) is the *annual effective* rate, $$(1.03)^2 - 1 = 6.09\%$$ — the conversion in the wrong direction (toward less frequent compounding).

📌 Exam tip: Ordering for the same wealth: $$r_{annual} > r_{semi} > r_{monthly} > r_{cont}.$$ Continuous is the smallest. If your "continuous" answer exceeds the semiannual rate, you converted the wrong way.

**Q2.** A rate of 5.00% is quoted with monthly compounding. Its continuously compounded equivalent is closest to:

- A. 4.99%
- B. 5.00%
- C. 5.12%
- D. 4.88%

**Answer: A**

The ask: convert a monthly-compounded rate to continuous.

1. Formula: $$r_c = m\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right), \quad m = 12.$$
2. Inner term: $$1 + \frac{0.05}{12} = 1.0041667.$$
3. Log: $$\ln(1.0041667) = 0.0041580.$$
4. Multiply: $$r_c = 12 \times 0.0041580 = 0.049896 = 4.99\%.$$

Why A is correct: continuous compounding sits just below the monthly rate (monthly is already close to continuous).

Distractor traps:
- B (5.00%) ignores the convention change.
- C (5.12%) is the *annual effective* rate, $$(1 + 0.05/12)^{12} - 1 = 5.12\%$$ — wrong direction.
- D (4.88%) over-shrinks the rate.

📌 Exam tip: The more frequent the starting compounding, the closer the continuous equivalent. Monthly → continuous moves the rate down only slightly (5.00% → 4.99%).

**Q3.** Continuously compounded spot rates are $$z(1) = 2.50\%$$ and $$z(2) = 3.00\%$$. The forward rate for the period from year 1 to year 2 is closest to:

- A. 2.75%
- B. 3.00%
- C. 3.50%
- D. 4.00%

**Answer: C**

The ask: compute the 1-year forward rate starting in 1 year, continuous compounding.

1. Formula: $$f(t_1, t_2) = \frac{z_2 t_2 - z_1 t_1}{t_2 - t_1}.$$
2. Plug in: $$f = \frac{0.03\times 2 - 0.025\times 1}{2 - 1}.$$
3. Numerator: $$0.06 - 0.025 = 0.035.$$
4. Divide by $$(2 - 1) = 1$$: $$f = 0.035 = 3.50\%.$$

Why C is correct: the forward is the *marginal* rate for extending maturity from 1 to 2 years; with a rising spot curve it sits above both spots.

Distractor traps:
- A (2.75%) averages the two spots — the forward is not the average.
- B (3.00%) reports $$z(2)$$ — confuses the spot with the forward.
- D (4.00%) double-counts (e.g., $$2z_2 - z_1$$ without dividing, or mis-scales).

📌 Exam tip: Continuous-compounding forward is a clean weighted difference: $$f = (z_2t_2 - z_1t_1)/(t_2 - t_1).$$ Spots are *averages* of forwards, so a rising spot curve ⟹ forward above spot.

**Q4.** Semiannual-compounding discount factors are $$d(0.5) = 0.985$$ and $$d(1.0) = 0.965$$. The 1-year par rate (semiannual coupon) is closest to:

- A. 3.50%
- B. 3.59%
- C. 7.00%
- D. 3.18%

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the 1-year par rate from discount factors.

1. Formula: $$c(T) = \frac{2\bigl(1 - d(T)\bigr)}{A(T)}, \quad A(T) = \sum_i d(t_i).$$
2. Annuity factor: $$A(1) = d(0.5) + d(1.0) = 0.985 + 0.965 = 1.950.$$
3. Numerator: $$2(1 - d(1.0)) = 2(1 - 0.965) = 2(0.035) = 0.070.$$
4. Par rate: $$c(1) = \frac{0.070}{1.950} = 0.035897 = 3.59\%.$$

Why B is correct: the par rate is the coupon making the bond price exactly 100; the factor of 2 annualizes the semiannual coupon.

Distractor traps:
- C (7.00%) forgets to divide by the annuity factor — leaves $$2(1 - d(T))$$ as a percentage.
- A (3.50%) uses only $$d(1.0)$$ in the denominator (omits $$d(0.5)$$ from the annuity).
- D (3.18%) divides $$(1 - d(T))$$ by $$A(T)$$ without the factor of 2 (forgets to annualize the semiannual coupon).

📌 Exam tip: Par rate $$= \dfrac{2(1 - d(T))}{\sum d(t_i)}.$$ The numerator's factor of 2 annualizes the semiannual coupon; the denominator sums *all* discount factors up to T. Drop either and you get a classic wrong answer.

**Q5.** With an upward-sloping spot-rate curve, the correct ordering of the forward, spot, and par curves at a given maturity T is:

- A. par > spot > forward
- B. forward > spot > par
- C. spot > forward > par
- D. forward > par > spot

**Answer: B**

The ask: order the three curves for a rising term structure.

Why B is correct: forwards are *marginal* rates that lead a rising curve (forward > spot); spots are averages of forwards. Par rates blend in *earlier, lower* spot rates through the bond's coupons, pulling the par rate below the spot rate (spot > par). Hence forward > spot > par.

Distractor traps:
- A reverses the entire ordering (that is the *downward*-sloping case).
- C places spot above forward — wrong for a rising curve (forward leads).
- D misplaces par above spot.

📌 Exam tip: Rising curve ⟹ forward > spot > par. Falling curve ⟹ forward < spot < par. Flat ⟹ all equal. The intuition: forwards lead (marginal), par lags (it averages earlier, lower coupons-weighted rates).

**Q6.** Semiannually compounded spot rates are $$z(1) = 3.00\%$$ and $$z(2) = 4.00\%$$. Using the no-arbitrage relation $$\left(1+\frac{z_2}{2}\right)^{2t_2} = \left(1+\frac{z_1}{2}\right)^{2t_1}\left(1+\frac{f}{2}\right)^{2(t_2 - t_1)},$$ the 1-year forward rate starting in 1 year (semiannual compounding) is closest to:

- A. 4.50%
- B. 5.00%
- C. 5.50%
- D. 4.00%

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute the forward rate under semiannual compounding (note: divide rates by 2, double the periods).

1. Left side ($$t_2 = 2$$): $$\left(1 + \frac{0.04}{2}\right)^{2\times 2} = (1.02)^4 = 1.082432.$$
2. First factor ($$t_1 = 1$$): $$\left(1 + \frac{0.03}{2}\right)^{2\times 1} = (1.015)^2 = 1.030225.$$
3. Isolate the forward factor: $$\left(1 + \frac{f}{2}\right)^{2} = \frac{1.082432}{1.030225} = 1.050675.$$
4. Square root: $$1 + \frac{f}{2} = \sqrt{1.050675} = 1.025025.$$
5. Solve: $$\frac{f}{2} = 0.025025 \Rightarrow f = 0.050049 = 5.00\%.$$

Why B is correct: the semiannual chain requires dividing each rate by 2 and using exponents of $$2t$$; the forward covers the 1→2 year period $$(2(t_2 - t_1) = 2$$ periods$$).$$

Distractor traps:
- A (4.50%) is the simple average of the two spots — ignores compounding/no-arbitrage.
- C (5.50%) over-states (e.g., uses $$2z_2 - z_1 = 5.0\%$$... or mis-handles the exponent).
- D (4.00%) reports $$z(2)$$ — confuses spot with forward.

📌 Exam tip: In semiannual problems, ALWAYS divide rates by 2 and use $$2t$$ as the exponent. Using $$(1+z)^t$$ on a semiannual-quoted rate is the most common error in the spot/forward chain.

**Q7.** $100 is invested for 2 years. Which terminal value correctly pairs with its compounding convention at a 5.00% quoted rate?

- A. Annual compounding gives $110.52.
- B. Continuous compounding gives $110.25.
- C. Semiannual compounding gives $110.52.
- D. Continuous compounding gives $110.52.

**Answer: D**

The ask: match the correct terminal value to its compounding convention.

1. Annual: $$100(1.05)^2 = 100 \times 1.1025 = \$110.25.$$
2. Semiannual: $$100\left(1 + \frac{0.05}{2}\right)^{2\times 2} = 100(1.025)^4 = 100 \times 1.103813 = \$110.38.$$
3. Continuous: $$100\,e^{0.05\times 2} = 100\,e^{0.10} = 100 \times 1.105171 = \$110.52.$$

Why D is correct: continuous compounding grows fastest for the same quoted rate, giving $110.52.

Distractor traps:
- A mislabels $110.52 as annual — annual is $110.25.
- B mislabels $110.25 as continuous — that is the annual figure.
- C mislabels $110.52 as semiannual — semiannual is $110.38.

📌 Exam tip: For the same *quoted* rate, terminal wealth rises with compounding frequency: annual ($110.25) < semiannual ($110.38) < continuous ($110.52). Match the convention in the question — plugging a semiannual rate into $$e^{rT}$$ silently overstates growth.
