**Q1.** During a regulatory review, a bank's risk architecture is described as follows: the FX trading desk monitors and stays within its own VaR limits; the independent risk management function builds the VaR model and challenges the desk's positions; internal audit periodically tests whether both the desk and the risk function operate as designed and reports its findings to the audit committee. An examiner says one role has been assigned to the wrong line of defense. Which assignment, if it were present, would violate the three-lines model?

- A. The risk management function independently building the VaR model — this should be the first line
- B. The trading desk owning and managing the risk it generates — this should be the second line
- C. Internal audit *designing* the desk's risk controls (rather than independently assuring them) — this collapses the third line into the first/second
- D. Internal audit reporting to the audit committee — it should report to the CRO

**Answer: C**

The question asks which assignment would *violate* the three-lines-of-defense model. The described setup is mostly correct; you must spot the impermissible variant among the options.

1. First line = business lines/risk owners: the desk owns and manages the risk it generates and stays within limits — correct as described.
2. Second line = risk management/compliance: sets the framework, measures independently, monitors, and challenges — correct as described.
3. Third line = internal audit: independently *assures* that lines 1 and 2 work as designed, and stays independent of both. If internal audit *designed* the controls it later audits, it could no longer assure them independently — a clear violation. That is C.

- A is wrong: building the independent risk model is precisely a second-line job, not first-line.
- B is wrong: owning and managing generated risk is exactly the first-line role, not second-line.
- D is wrong: internal audit reporting to the **audit committee** is correct best practice; reporting to the CRO would actually *compromise* its independence.

📌 Exam tip: the third line (internal audit) must never *design* or *own* controls — only assure them — and reports to the audit committee, not the CRO. Watch for questions that secretly hand first-line ownership to the CRO or make audit a control designer; both break independence.

**Q2.** A bank's board approves an aggressive three-year growth target (double the loan book) while simultaneously approving a conservative risk appetite statement (no increase in the firm's credit-loss volatility). Traders and lenders are paid on origination volume with no risk adjustment. The CRO warns the structure is internally inconsistent and predicts a specific outcome. Which prediction is most consistent with the curriculum's treatment of appetite, strategy, and incentives?

- A. The conservative appetite will automatically dominate because it is a board-approved policy document
- B. The disconnect will be resolved on the trading floor in favor of growth, because volume-based incentives are the transmission belt and will push the risk profile beyond appetite unless governance intervenes
- C. The two goals are independent, so both can be met without conflict
- D. The growth target will be abandoned because risk capacity is fixed

**Answer: B**

The question tests how strategy, risk appetite, and incentives interact when they are misaligned.

1. The curriculum insists risk appetite and strategy must be set **jointly** — a growth strategy carries risk consequences (more lending, more credit-loss volatility). A conservative appetite paired with an aggressive growth target is a disconnect.
2. **Incentives are the transmission belt.** If pay is on origination volume with no risk adjustment, employees move the risk profile toward whatever the bonus rewards — here, growth.
3. The curriculum states such disconnects "get resolved on the trading floor in favor of growth unless governance intervenes." So the predicted outcome is exactly B.

- A assumes a policy document self-enforces; the whole point is that incentives, not paper, drive behavior.
- C calls the goals independent; doubling the loan book without raising credit-loss volatility is internally contradictory given the incentive design.
- D claims the growth target is auto-abandoned because capacity is fixed; nothing forces that — absent governance, growth wins.

📌 Exam tip: when a question pairs aggressive targets, conservative appetite, and volume-based pay, the answer is almost always that *incentives win and push the profile past appetite unless governance intervenes*. Appetite and strategy must be set together; pay is the transmission mechanism.

**Q3.** A firm is allocating oversight duties between its board risk committee and its audit committee. For each duty below, the firm must pick the *primary* owner: (i) approving and overseeing the firm-wide risk appetite and forward-looking risk; (ii) overseeing the integrity of financial reporting and the external auditor; (iii) approving the internal audit plan and protecting internal audit's independence. Which assignment is correct?

- A. (i) audit committee; (ii) risk committee; (iii) risk committee
- B. (i) risk committee; (ii) audit committee; (iii) audit committee
- C. (i) risk committee; (ii) risk committee; (iii) audit committee
- D. (i) audit committee; (ii) audit committee; (iii) risk committee

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to assign three governance duties to the correct board committee.

1. Duty (i) — risk appetite oversight and forward-looking risk — is the **risk committee's** core mandate.
2. Duty (ii) — integrity of financial reporting and the external audit relationship — belongs to the **audit committee**.
3. Duty (iii) — approving the internal audit plan, receiving its findings, and protecting its independence — also belongs to the **audit committee** (it oversees internal audit but does not run its day-to-day testing).

So (i) risk, (ii) audit, (iii) audit — option B.

- A and D mis-assign the risk-appetite duty to the audit committee.
- C correctly tags (i) but gives financial-reporting integrity to the risk committee — that is the audit committee's signature responsibility.

📌 Exam tip: split the committees cleanly — *risk committee* = appetite + forward-looking risk; *audit committee* = financial-reporting integrity, controls assurance, and oversight (not running) of internal/external audit. Internal audit *reports to* the audit committee.

**Q4.** Which post-crisis governance or regulatory reform is paired correctly with its intended purpose?

- A. The Volcker rule was designed to expand banks' proprietary trading so they could earn higher returns
- B. Compensation clawback and malus provisions were introduced to align pay with long-run, risk-adjusted outcomes
- C. The Basel III leverage ratio was intended to fully replace risk-based capital requirements
- D. Mandatory board risk committees were created to take over the day-to-day running of the risk function

**Answer: B**

The question asks which reform–purpose pairing is accurate.

1. Clawback/malus and deferral let the firm recover or withhold pay when risks materialize later, so compensation reflects multi-period, risk-adjusted performance rather than one good year. That matches the curriculum's "deferral, clawback/malus provisions, risk-adjusted performance metrics." So B is correct.

- A inverts the Volcker rule, which *restricts* proprietary trading, not expands it.
- C is false: the leverage ratio is a *backstop that complements* risk-based capital, not a replacement.
- D is false: board risk committees provide *oversight* — set appetite, engage with the risk appetite statement — not day-to-day management; the board's job is oversight, not management.

📌 Exam tip: memorize the corrected pairings — Volcker = *restrict* prop trading; leverage ratio = *backstop/complement* to risk-based capital; board risk committee = *oversight, not management*; clawback/deferral = align pay with *long-run, risk-adjusted* outcomes. Distractors typically invert exactly one of these.

**Q5.** A new director argues that because the board approves the risk appetite statement, the board is therefore "managing the firm's risk" and should also set desk-level VaR limits, approve individual large loans, and design the daily risk report so that it stays close to the action. Which response correctly states the boundary of the board's role?

- A. The director is right; close board involvement in desk limits and individual loans is best practice
- B. The board's role is oversight, not management: it approves appetite, strategy, and key policies, ensures capable management and sound incentives, and monitors — but it does not run the risk function or set desk limits, approve individual loans, or design daily reports
- C. The board should design the daily risk report but delegate appetite-setting to management
- D. The board should approve individual large loans but leave the appetite statement to the CRO

**Answer: B**

The question tests the line between board *oversight* and management *execution*.

1. The curriculum is explicit: "the board's job is **oversight, not management**: set appetite, approve strategy and key policies, ensure capable management and sound incentive design, and monitor — it does not run the risk function day to day."
2. Setting desk-level VaR limits, approving individual large loans, and designing the daily risk report are operational/management tasks — not board functions.

So the correct boundary is B.

- A endorses the director's overreach into management tasks.
- C and D each hand the board a management task (designing reports / approving individual loans) while stripping it of a genuine board task (approving appetite) — both invert the proper allocation.

📌 Exam tip: board = *approve appetite/strategy/policy, ensure capable management and incentives, monitor*. Anything granular and operational — desk limits, individual deals, report design — is management. Distractors love to drag the board down into execution or push appetite-setting down to management.

**Q6.** A firm experiences a large loss when a trader's positions are mis-booked for months because the back office that should have independently confirmed and settled the trades had been merged under the trading desk's control. A risk officer explains the loss using the concept of functional interdependence. Which explanation is most accurate?

- A. The loss was purely market risk and unrelated to organizational structure
- B. The independent back office is the check that positions are real; merging it under the front office removed that independent confirmation, so reported risk numbers rested on unverified trades — the breakdown that enabled Barings
- C. The CRO should personally confirm every trade; this is a first-line ownership failure by the risk function
- D. The audit committee should have set the desk's trading limits to prevent the mis-booking

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the functional-interdependence explanation of a control breakdown.

1. The curriculum stresses that operations/back office "confirms and settles trades — the independent check that positions are real," and that its absence "enabled Barings."
2. Merging the back office under the trading desk destroyed that independence: the same unit both took and confirmed positions, so mis-booked trades went undetected and any risk number built on those trades was unreliable. "A risk number is only as good as the trade capture, valuation, and data plumbing beneath it." That is B.

- A denies the structural/operational cause; the loss is an operational/control failure, not pure market risk.
- C misassigns first-line ownership to the CRO; the front office and an *independent* back office own trade capture/confirmation, not the CRO personally.
- D miscasts the audit committee as a setter of trading limits — that is a management task, and the failure here is loss of independent confirmation, not the limit level.

📌 Exam tip: independent trade confirmation/settlement (segregation of duties between front and back office) is the control whose absence produced Barings. When a scenario removes that independence, the answer is the loss of the *independent check that positions are real* — and remember every risk number depends on clean trade capture beneath it.

**Q7.** The 2007–09 crisis prompted the elevation of the Chief Risk Officer role. A bank is redesigning its reporting lines. Which CRO arrangement best reflects post-crisis best practice as described in the curriculum?

- A. The CRO reports into the head of the trading business, ensuring close knowledge of front-office activity
- B. The CRO reports to the CFO, consolidating all control functions under finance
- C. The CRO is independent, with direct access and a reporting line to the board or its risk committee, not buried under the business or the CFO
- D. The CRO rotates annually among the business-line heads to maintain breadth

**Answer: C**

The question asks which CRO reporting arrangement matches post-crisis best practice.

1. The curriculum describes "CRO elevation: an independent Chief Risk Officer with direct access/reporting to the board or its risk committee, **not buried under the business or the CFO**." That is exactly C.

- A buries the CRO under the trading business — destroying independence (the second line cannot be controlled by the first line it must challenge).
- B places the CRO under the CFO, the precise arrangement the reforms sought to end.
- D (rotating among business heads) again subordinates the CRO to the lines being overseen and removes a stable independent reporting line.

📌 Exam tip: the headline post-crisis fix is an *independent* CRO with a *direct board/risk-committee reporting line*. Any answer that puts the CRO under the business or the CFO is the trap, because it strips the independence that lets the second line challenge the first.
