**Q1.** A bank holds a $100 million loan to a corporate client. It wants to (i) shed the *default* risk on the client, (ii) preserve the client relationship by not selling or assigning the loan and not notifying the client, and (iii) avoid taking on the client's *market/price* risk that it does not currently bear. The bank is comparing a single-name CDS, a total return swap (TRS) on the loan, and securitizing the loan. Which instrument best meets *all three* conditions?

- A. A total return swap, because it transfers the loan's full economics
- B. A single-name CDS referencing the client, because it transfers default risk only and is executed discreetly without selling the loan
- C. Securitizing the loan through an SPV, because it removes the asset from the balance sheet
- D. A credit-linked note issued to the client, because it is funded upfront

**Answer: B**

The question asks which instrument satisfies all three conditions simultaneously.

1. Condition (i) shed *default* risk: a CDS pays par minus recovery on a credit event of the reference entity — it transfers **default (credit) risk only**.
2. Condition (ii) keep the relationship, no loan sale or notification: buying CDS does not require selling or assigning the loan, so the client is not notified — discreet.
3. Condition (iii) avoid taking on market/price risk: a CDS transfers *only* default risk, so it does not add the loan's market-price risk. 

All three are met by the single-name CDS — option B.

- A (TRS) fails condition (iii): a TRS transfers the *total* return — both credit **and** market risk — which is more than the bank wants.
- C (securitization through an SPV) fails condition (ii): it is "heavier machinery" and a true sale of the asset, not a discreet relationship-preserving hedge of one loan.
- D (CLN issued *to the client*) is nonsensical here — the client cannot fund protection on its own default, and it does not discreetly hedge the bank's exposure to that client.

📌 Exam tip: CDS = default risk only, discreet, relationship preserved. TRS = total return = credit **and** market risk. The phrase "without selling or notifying the client" almost always points to a single-name CDS.

**Q2.** A monoline insurer with a weak balance sheet sells $5 billion of CDS protection on senior tranches of subprime mortgage CDOs. A bank buys this protection to hedge its own subprime CDO holdings. As the housing market collapses, both the reference CDOs and the monoline deteriorate together. Which statement most precisely describes the risk the bank faces and why?

- A. Basis risk, because the CDS does not reference the exact bonds the bank holds
- B. Wrong-way risk: the protection seller's creditworthiness is positively correlated with the reference credit's deterioration, so the CDS is most likely to fail exactly when the bank needs it to pay
- C. Funding liquidity risk, because the bank must post collateral on the CDS
- D. No residual risk, because purchasing a CDS fully eliminates the bank's credit exposure

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the precise risk created when a protection seller's health is correlated with the reference credit it insures.

1. A CDS transfers credit risk to the protection seller but creates **counterparty risk** on that seller — if the seller fails, the protection is worthless.
2. Here the monoline's solvency is **positively correlated** with the very subprime credits it insured: when mortgages sour, both the CDOs *and* the monoline collapse together.
3. That correlation between the protection seller's default and the reference exposure is the definition of **wrong-way risk** — the hedge fails exactly when it is needed. That is B (the AIG/monoline case).

- A (basis risk) is a tracking-mismatch concept; the problem here is the seller's correlated failure, not an index/grade mismatch.
- C (funding liquidity) describes a margin-cash problem, not the failure-of-the-hedge mechanism asked about.
- D is the trap that a CDS "fully eliminates" credit exposure; it merely *replaces* reference-credit risk with counterparty risk on the seller — and that risk just bit.

📌 Exam tip: CDS shifts credit risk but creates counterparty risk on the seller. When the seller's health is *correlated* with the reference credit (monolines, AIG insuring mortgage CDOs), that is wrong-way risk — the protection evaporates precisely when called upon.

**Q3.** Four credit-risk-transfer instruments are under consideration. The treasurer wants the structure that leaves the **protection buyer with no counterparty risk on the protection provider**. Which one, and why?

- A. A single-name CDS, because the determinations committee guarantees settlement
- B. A total return swap, because both legs are exchanged periodically
- C. A credit-linked note, because the investor funds the note upfront so the cash is already held
- D. A bank guarantee, because the guarantor is a regulated institution

**Answer: C**

The question asks which instrument removes the protection buyer's counterparty risk on the provider.

1. A **credit-linked note (CLN)** is a *funded* structure: the investor (protection seller) pays cash upfront for the note, whose principal is written down if the reference entity defaults.
2. Because the protection buyer already holds the investor's cash, there is **no counterparty risk** on the protection — the money is in hand. That is C.

- A (CDS) is *unfunded*; the seller may fail to pay on a credit event (AIG). A determinations committee decides *whether* a credit event occurred — it does not guarantee the seller's solvency.
- B (TRS) is also unfunded and carries counterparty risk on the total-return payer.
- D (bank guarantee) replaces obligor risk with the **guarantor's** credit risk — still counterparty risk (and wrong-way risk if correlated).

📌 Exam tip: "funded upfront → no counterparty risk on the protection" = credit-linked note (and CDO tranches are also funded). CDS, TRS, and guarantees are unfunded promises and leave the buyer exposed to the provider's default.

**Q4.** A securitization SPV issues three classes of notes against a $1,000 million pool: a senior tranche of $800 million, a mezzanine tranche of $150 million, and an equity (first-loss) tranche of $50 million. The pool experiences cumulative losses of $180 million. Assuming a strict subordination waterfall with no other credit enhancement, what is the loss borne by each tranche?

- A. Equity loses $50m, mezzanine loses $130m, senior loses $0m
- B. Equity loses $50m, mezzanine loses $150m, senior loses $0m
- C. Equity loses $60m, mezzanine loses $120m, senior loses $0m
- D. Losses are shared pro rata: senior $144m, mezzanine $27m, equity $9m

**Answer: A**

The question asks how a $180m loss is allocated across tranches under bottom-up subordination.

1. Losses flow **bottom-up**: the equity (first-loss) tranche absorbs losses first.
   - Equity capacity = $50m. Loss applied: $50m. Remaining loss to allocate: $180m − $50m = $130m.
2. Next, the mezzanine tranche absorbs losses.
   - Mezzanine capacity = $150m. Remaining loss $130m < $150m, so mezzanine absorbs all **$130m** and is not wiped out ($20m of mezzanine survives).
3. Senior tranche: no loss reaches it because the $180m total is fully absorbed by equity + mezzanine. Senior loss = **$0m**.

So equity $50m, mezzanine $130m, senior $0m — option A.

- B over-allocates the mezzanine to its full $150m; only $130m of loss remains after equity, so mezzanine cannot lose more than $130m.
- C wrongly inflates the equity loss to $60m (above its $50m capacity) and mis-splits the rest.
- D applies *pro rata* sharing — exactly the trap. Tranched securitizations use **subordination**, not pro rata loss sharing; losses hit the junior tranche fully before any senior tranche is touched.

📌 Exam tip: tranche losses are sequential, bottom-up — fill the junior tranche to its cap, then spill the remainder up. A tranche never absorbs more than its own size, and the trap answer is pro-rata sharing (which would be wrong for standard subordination).

**Q5.** Compared with a buy-and-hold lender, an originate-to-distribute (OTD) originator that sells loans into securitizations faces a *different* primary risk profile. Which risk is most distinctively associated with the OTD model, and what governance reform was introduced to address its core incentive flaw?

- A. Concentration risk; addressed by the leverage ratio
- B. Pipeline/warehouse risk — loans stuck on the balance sheet when the securitization market closes — and the core incentive flaw (weak screening) was addressed by risk-retention ("skin in the game") rules
- C. Counterparty risk on the SPV; addressed by central clearing
- D. Translation risk; addressed by hedge accounting

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the risk distinctive to OTD and the reform targeting its incentive flaw.

1. OTD is capital-light and fee-driven: the originator plans to *distribute* loans, so it holds them only briefly. If markets freeze, loans pile up unsold on the balance sheet — **pipeline/warehouse risk** — a risk a buy-and-hold lender does not have in the same form.
2. The deeper flaw is **weak screening/monitoring incentives** ("skin in the game" problem): an originator passing on the risk has little reason to underwrite carefully, a core cause of the subprime debacle.
3. The post-crisis fix was **risk-retention ("skin in the game") rules** requiring originators/sponsors to keep a slice (e.g., 5%) of each deal. That is B.

- A names concentration risk (more a buy-and-hold issue) and an unrelated reform (the leverage ratio).
- C invents counterparty risk on the SPV and pairs it with central clearing (a derivatives reform, not securitization).
- D names translation risk and hedge accounting — unrelated to OTD.

📌 Exam tip: OTD signature risks = pipeline/warehouse risk + eroded underwriting incentives (moral hazard). The targeted reform is *risk retention / skin in the game* (~5%). Don't confuse this with derivatives-market reforms (clearing, CVA) which address CDS counterparty risk.

**Q6.** Which statement about how the credit derivatives market changed *after* the 2007–09 crisis is accurate?

- A. Standardized CDS moved to central clearing through CCPs, trades were reported to repositories, and single-name liquidity declined while activity concentrated in index products
- B. Central clearing was abolished in favor of purely bilateral OTC trading to preserve customization
- C. Margin requirements for non-cleared derivatives were removed to reduce funding strains
- D. CVA capital charges were eliminated because counterparty risk was deemed fully mitigated by clearing

**Answer: A**

The question asks which post-crisis change to the credit derivatives market is correctly stated.

1. The reforms introduced **central clearing** of standardized CDS through CCPs and **trade reporting to repositories** for transparency.
2. The Big Bang protocol brought standardization (fixed coupons, auction settlement, determinations committees).
3. The market **shrank and concentrated in index products**, while **single-name liquidity declined**. Option A captures all of this accurately.

- B reverses the direction — clearing was *introduced/expanded*, not abolished in favor of bilateral trading.
- C reverses the margin reform — margin requirements for *non-cleared* derivatives were *imposed*, not removed.
- D is false — **CVA charges** (higher capital for counterparty risk) were *added*, not eliminated; clearing does not make counterparty risk vanish.

📌 Exam tip: post-crisis CDS reforms = central clearing + trade reporting + standardization (Big Bang) + margin on non-cleared trades + CVA capital charges, with the market concentrating in indices and single-name liquidity falling. Distractors typically *reverse the direction* of one real reform.

**Q7.** Several traditional credit-risk mitigants are listed. A risk manager wants the mechanism that, by *periodically settling* gains and losses, resets the outstanding exposure toward zero — but recognizes it imposes a specific secondary risk. Which mechanism and secondary risk are correctly paired?

- A. Netting; secondary risk is that recovery rates fall in default
- B. Marking to market; secondary risk is that the party must have the liquidity to pay the variation amounts
- C. Purchasing a guarantee; secondary risk is wrong-way risk on the guarantor
- D. Collateralization; secondary risk is legal enforceability across jurisdictions

**Answer: B**

The question asks which mitigant *periodically settles gains/losses to reset exposure toward zero*, and what secondary risk that creates.

1. **Marking to market** periodically settles gains and losses, so the running exposure is reset toward zero each period (variation margin/settlement). That matches the described mechanism.
2. Its watch-out per the curriculum: it "requires liquidity to pay variation amounts" — the party owing must have cash on hand to settle. That secondary risk is funding liquidity. Pairing matches B.

- A pairs netting with falling recovery rates; netting's mechanism is offsetting mutual obligations (not periodic gain/loss settlement) and its watch-out is *legal enforceability*, not recovery.
- C pairs guarantees with wrong-way risk — that pairing is itself valid, but a guarantee does not *periodically settle gains/losses to reset exposure*, so it fails the stem's defining clause.
- D pairs collateralization with legal enforceability — but the curriculum ties enforceability to *netting*; collateral's watch-outs are falling collateral value, haircuts, rehypothecation, and margin-call liquidity. The defining mechanism (periodic settlement resetting exposure) also points to MTM, not collateral.

📌 Exam tip: match the *mechanism* clause first. "Periodic settlement resetting exposure toward zero" = marking to market, whose secondary risk is needing liquidity to pay variation. Netting = offsetting obligations (watch-out: legal enforceability); collateral = posting assets (watch-outs: value declines, haircuts, margin-call liquidity).
