**Q1.** A multiple regression with $$k = 6$$ slope regressors is estimated on $$n = 80$$ observations and reports $$R^2 = 0.45$$. What is the adjusted $$R^2$$?

- A. 0.405
- B. 0.450
- C. 0.378
- D. 0.495

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the adjusted $$R^2$$ given $$R^2$$, $$n$$, and $$k$$.

1. The formula penalizes for the number of slope coefficients:
$$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1}$$
2. Plug in $$n = 80$$, $$k = 6$$:
$$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - 0.45)\,\frac{80 - 1}{80 - 6 - 1} = 1 - 0.55 \times \frac{79}{73}$$
3. Compute the ratio and product:
$$\frac{79}{73} = 1.0822, \qquad 0.55 \times 1.0822 = 0.5952$$
$$\bar R^2 = 1 - 0.5952 = 0.4048 \approx 0.405$$

Why A is correct: $$\bar R^2 = 1 - 0.55(79/73) = 0.405$$.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** just restates $$R^2$$ — forgetting to adjust at all.
- **C** uses $$n - k - 1$$ in *both* numerator and denominator (or off-by-one degrees of freedom), e.g. $$1 - 0.55(79/74) = 0.413$$... a common df slip producing too low a value.
- **D** mistakenly *adds* the penalty term, pushing the adjusted figure above $$R^2$$ — impossible, since $$\bar R^2 \le R^2$$ always.

📌 Exam tip: Use $$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}$$. The numerator df is $$n-1$$, the denominator df is $$n-k-1$$ (k = slope count). Adjusted $$R^2$$ can only be ≤ $$R^2$$ — if you get a higher number, you flipped a term.

**Q2.** A model with $$k = 4$$ regressors estimated on $$n = 65$$ observations has $$R^2 = 0.28$$. Test the overall null $$H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3 = \beta_4 = 0$$ at 5% (critical $$F_{4,60} \approx 2.53$$). What is the F-statistic and decision?

- A. F = 5.83; reject — the regressors are jointly significant
- B. F = 5.83; fail to reject — the regressors are not jointly significant
- C. F = 0.097; fail to reject — the regressors are not jointly significant
- D. F = 4.20; reject — the regressors are jointly significant

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the overall F-statistic and decision.

1. The all-slopes-zero F uses $$R^2$$ directly:
$$F = \frac{R^2 / k}{(1 - R^2)/(n - k - 1)}$$
2. Plug in $$R^2 = 0.28$$, $$k = 4$$, $$n = 65$$:
$$F = \frac{0.28 / 4}{(1 - 0.28)/(65 - 4 - 1)} = \frac{0.07}{0.72/60} = \frac{0.07}{0.012} = 5.83$$
3. Compare to $$F_{4,60} \approx 2.53$$. Since $$5.83 > 2.53$$, **reject** $$H_0$$ — the regressors are jointly significant.

Why A is correct: numerator $$0.07$$, denominator $$0.012$$, ratio 5.83, which exceeds 2.53.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes F correctly but flips the (always upper-tail) decision.
- **C** inverts the ratio ($$0.012/0.07 \times$$ ... ≈ 0.097) — puts $$(1-R^2)$$ on top.
- **D** uses the wrong denominator df, e.g. $$n - k = 61$$ combined with a miscalculation, deflating F.

📌 Exam tip: The overall F is always an upper-tail test — large F ⇒ reject. The shortcut form $$\frac{R^2/k}{(1-R^2)/(n-k-1)}$$ saves you from needing the raw sums of squares.

**Q3.** An unrestricted model with $$k_u = 5$$ regressors on $$n = 70$$ observations has $$RSS_u = 320$$. Dropping 3 of the regressors gives a restricted model with $$RSS_r = 380$$. Test whether those 3 coefficients are jointly zero at 5% (critical $$F_{3,64} \approx 2.75$$). What is the F-statistic and decision?

- A. F = 4.00; reject — the three dropped regressors jointly matter
- B. F = 4.00; fail to reject — the three dropped regressors do not matter
- C. F = 1.33; fail to reject — the three dropped regressors do not matter
- D. F = 12.0; reject — the three dropped regressors jointly matter

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the restricted-versus-unrestricted F and decision, with $$q = 3$$ restrictions.

1. The F-statistic compares the rise in RSS from imposing the restrictions to the unrestricted residual variance:
$$F = \frac{(RSS_r - RSS_u)/q}{RSS_u/(n - k_u - 1)}$$
2. Plug in $$RSS_r = 380$$, $$RSS_u = 320$$, $$q = 3$$, $$n - k_u - 1 = 70 - 5 - 1 = 64$$:
$$F = \frac{(380 - 320)/3}{320/64} = \frac{60/3}{5} = \frac{20}{5} = 4.00$$
3. Compare to $$F_{3,64} \approx 2.75$$. Since $$4.00 > 2.75$$, **reject** — the three restrictions are rejected, so the dropped regressors jointly belong in the model.

Why A is correct: numerator $$20$$, denominator $$5$$, F = 4.00 > 2.75.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** computes F correctly but flips the decision.
- **C** forgets to divide the numerator by $$q$$ ($$60/320 \times 64/... $$) or uses $$60/(something)$$ giving ≈1.33 — dropping the $$q$$ scaling.
- **D** forgets to divide the numerator by $$q$$ entirely: $$60/5 = 12$$ — treats $$q = 1$$.

📌 Exam tip: In the partial F-test, the numerator is $$(RSS_r - RSS_u)/q$$ with $$q$$ = number of restrictions, and the denominator is $$RSS_u/(n-k_u-1)$$. Forgetting to divide by $$q$$ is the classic error.

**Q4.** A wage regression includes a constant (intercept) and the analyst wants to control for region. There are four regions. The analyst adds a separate dummy variable for **every** region in addition to the intercept. What happens and what is the fix?

- A. Perfect multicollinearity — OLS cannot be estimated; omit one region as the base category
- B. Omitted variable bias — the region effect is absorbed by the intercept; add an interaction term
- C. Heteroskedasticity — the regional groups have different variances; use robust standard errors
- D. Imperfect multicollinearity — standard errors inflate but OLS still runs; do nothing

**Answer: A**

We are asked to diagnose the consequence of including all category dummies plus an intercept.

1. With four regional dummies $$D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4$$ that each equal 1 for exactly one region, every observation falls in exactly one region, so $$D_1 + D_2 + D_3 + D_4 = 1$$ for all observations.
2. The intercept column is also a column of 1s. Thus the four dummies sum exactly to the intercept — one regressor is an exact linear combination of the others.
3. This is **perfect (exact) multicollinearity** (the "dummy variable trap"): OLS cannot invert the regressor matrix and cannot be computed.
4. Fix: **drop one dummy** (the base category); its level is captured by the intercept, and the remaining dummies measure differences relative to it.

Why A is correct: exact linear dependence ⇒ OLS undefined ⇒ omit one category.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** misnames it omitted variable bias — nothing relevant is omitted; the problem is redundancy, not omission.
- **C** invokes heteroskedasticity, which concerns error *variance*, not collinearity among regressors.
- **D** calls it imperfect multicollinearity and says "do nothing" — but exact collinearity makes estimation impossible, so a regressor *must* be dropped.

📌 Exam tip: Number of dummies should be (number of categories − 1) when an intercept is present. Including all categories plus the constant is the dummy variable trap: perfect collinearity, not merely high correlation.

**Q5.** A regression of corporate bond spreads on five regressors reports a highly significant overall F-statistic ($$p < 0.001$$), a high $$R^2$$ of 0.82, yet not one of the five individual t-statistics is significant at 5%. What is the most likely explanation?

- A. Multicollinearity — the regressors are jointly informative but individually imprecise
- B. Omitted variable bias — a key driver of spreads is missing
- C. Heteroskedasticity — the error variance depends on the regressors
- D. The model is misspecified and should be discarded

**Answer: A**

We are asked to identify the signature pattern: significant F, insignificant individual t's, high $$R^2$$.

1. A significant overall F says the regressors **as a group** explain the dependent variable.
2. Insignificant individual t's say no single coefficient is estimated precisely enough to stand out.
3. This combination — group matters, individuals don't, with high $$R^2$$ — is the **textbook signature of multicollinearity**. When regressors are highly correlated, OLS cannot disentangle their separate effects, so each standard error balloons and individual t's collapse, even though the joint fit is strong.

Why A is correct: it is the precise multicollinearity fingerprint.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** omitted variable bias biases coefficients but does not produce the "significant F, insignificant t's" pattern.
- **C** heteroskedasticity corrupts standard errors but does not specifically generate a strong joint fit with weak individual significance.
- **D** discarding the model is wrong — the variables clearly matter jointly; the issue is precision of separate effects, often addressed by combining/dropping collinear regressors or getting more data.

📌 Exam tip: "Significant F but no significant t's" ⇒ think multicollinearity. The variables matter as a bundle; their individual effects just can't be separated.

**Q6.** A multiple regression of portfolio return on three factors reports $$\hat\beta_2 = 1.30$$ for the value factor with $$SE(\hat\beta_2) = 0.50$$, on a large sample. Construct the 95% confidence interval and assess $$H_0: \beta_2 = 0$$.

- A. CI = $$(0.32, 2.28)$$; reject $$H_0$$ — the value factor is significant
- B. CI = $$(0.80, 1.80)$$; reject $$H_0$$ — the value factor is significant
- C. CI = $$(0.32, 2.28)$$; fail to reject $$H_0$$ — the value factor is insignificant
- D. CI = $$(-0.18, 2.78)$$; fail to reject $$H_0$$ — the value factor is insignificant

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the 95% CI for a single coefficient in a multiple regression and the test of significance.

1. Individual-coefficient CI uses the t-multiplier; on a large sample $$t \approx 1.96$$:
$$\hat\beta_2 \pm 1.96 \times SE(\hat\beta_2) = 1.30 \pm 1.96 \times 0.50 = 1.30 \pm 0.98$$
$$= (0.32,\; 2.28)$$
2. By duality, the null value 0 lies **outside** $$(0.32, 2.28)$$, so **reject** $$H_0: \beta_2 = 0$$ — the value factor is significant. (Check: $$t = 1.30/0.50 = 2.60 > 1.96$$ ⇒ reject. Consistent.)

Why A is correct: margin $$1.96 \times 0.50 = 0.98$$ gives $$(0.32, 2.28)$$, which excludes 0.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** uses the bare SE as the margin ($$\pm 0.50$$) instead of $$1.96 \times SE$$.
- **C** has the right interval but flips the decision — 0 is outside, so reject.
- **D** doubles the margin ($$\pm 1.48$$, perhaps using $$2.96$$ or mis-multiplying) so the interval spans 0 and wrongly fails to reject.

📌 Exam tip: Individual-coefficient inference in multiple regression is still a t-test/CI on one $$\hat\beta_j$$ — the F-test is only for *joint* nulls. CI half-width = $$t_{\alpha/2}\times SE$$, not the bare SE.

**Q7.** A model with $$n = 50$$, $$k = 4$$, and $$R^2 = 0.36$$ is augmented with a fifth regressor whose individual t-statistic turns out to be 0.9 (insignificant at 5%), raising $$R^2$$ to 0.37. Which statement is correct?

- A. Adjusted $$R^2$$ likely fell, because the new regressor's $$|t| < 1$$ — it does not earn its degree of freedom
- B. Adjusted $$R^2$$ must have risen, because $$R^2$$ rose
- C. The new regressor is statistically significant because $$R^2$$ increased
- D. Adjusted $$R^2$$ is unchanged because $$R^2$$ changed by only 0.01

**Answer: A**

We are asked how adjusted $$R^2$$ responds when a weak ($$|t|<1$$) regressor is added.

1. Key rule: adding a regressor raises adjusted $$R^2$$ only if its $$|t| > 1$$. With $$|t| = 0.9 < 1$$, the variable fails this hurdle, so adjusted $$R^2$$ **falls** (or at best stays flat).
2. Confirm numerically. Before ($$k=4$$):
$$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1-0.36)\frac{49}{45} = 1 - 0.64 \times 1.0889 = 1 - 0.6969 = 0.3031$$
After ($$k=5$$, $$R^2=0.37$$):
$$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1-0.37)\frac{49}{44} = 1 - 0.63 \times 1.1136 = 1 - 0.7016 = 0.2984$$
3. Adjusted $$R^2$$ dropped from ≈0.303 to ≈0.298 — confirming the $$|t|<1$$ rule.

Why A is correct: $$|t| = 0.9 < 1$$, so the penalty outweighs the tiny $$R^2$$ gain and adjusted $$R^2$$ declines.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** assumes adjusted $$R^2$$ tracks $$R^2$$ — false; the whole point of the adjustment is that it can fall when $$R^2$$ rises only slightly.
- **C** equates a higher $$R^2$$ with significance — $$R^2$$ never falls when adding a regressor, so it says nothing about significance.
- **D** asserts no change from a small $$R^2$$ move, but the degrees-of-freedom penalty still bites.

📌 Exam tip: The $$|t| > 1$$ rule is the cutoff for adjusted $$R^2$$ to rise — a far lower bar than the $$|t| > 1.96$$ needed for 5% significance. "Adjusted $$R^2$$ went up" does NOT mean the variable is significant.

**Q8.** In a multiple regression of credit spread on a leverage ratio and a set of rating dummy variables (investment grade as the base category), the coefficient on leverage is estimated at $$+0.85$$. What is the correct interpretation of this coefficient?

- A. The expected increase in spread per one-unit increase in leverage, holding the rating dummies constant
- B. The expected increase in spread per one-unit increase in leverage, ignoring all other regressors
- C. The expected spread when leverage and all rating dummies equal zero
- D. The expected increase in spread per one-unit increase in leverage, but only for the investment-grade base category

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the correct interpretation of a slope coefficient in a multiple regression.

1. In multiple regression, each slope is a **partial (ceteris paribus) effect**: $$\beta_j$$ is the expected change in $$Y$$ for a one-unit change in $$X_j$$ **holding all other included regressors fixed**.
2. So $$+0.85$$ means: with the rating dummies held constant, a one-unit rise in leverage is associated with a 0.85-unit rise in spread.

Why A is correct: it states the partial-effect interpretation, which is what distinguishes multiple-regression coefficients from simple-regression slopes.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** "ignoring all other regressors" describes a *simple* (univariate) regression slope; in multiple regression the effect is conditional on the other regressors, not marginal.
- **C** describes the *intercept* (value of $$Y$$ when all regressors are zero), not the leverage slope.
- **D** wrongly restricts the leverage effect to one rating category — the leverage coefficient applies across all observations (the dummies shift the intercept, they do not localize the leverage slope unless an interaction term is present).

📌 Exam tip: A multiple-regression coefficient is always "holding the other regressors constant." If a question adds "ignoring other variables" or "only for one group," it is describing a different (simple-regression or interaction) quantity.
