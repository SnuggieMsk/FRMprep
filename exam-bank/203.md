**Q1.** A portfolio holds 20 independent loans, each with a 5% annual default probability. What is the probability of exactly 2 defaults during the year?

- A. 18.9%
- B. 37.7%
- C. 26.4%
- D. 9.2%

**Answer: A**

This is a binomial calculation with $$n=20$$, $$p=0.05$$, $$k=2$$.

1. Formula: $$P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$$.
2. Combinations: $$\binom{20}{2} = \frac{20 \times 19}{2} = 190$$.
3. $$p^k = (0.05)^2 = 0.0025$$.
4. $$(1-p)^{n-k} = (0.95)^{18}$$. Compute: $$\ln(0.95) = -0.051293$$; $$\times 18 = -0.92327$$; $$e^{-0.92327} = 0.3972$$.
5. Multiply: $$190 \times 0.0025 \times 0.3972 = 0.475 \times 0.3972 = 0.1887 \approx 18.9\%$$.

A is correct (18.9%). B (37.7%) is the probability of exactly *one* default, $$\binom{20}{1}(0.05)(0.95)^{19} = 20\times0.05\times0.3774 = 0.377$$ — the wrong $$k$$. C (26.4%) is the probability of *at least* 2 defaults, $$1 - P(0) - P(1)$$ — the wrong event. D (9.2%) uses $$(0.95)^{20}$$ in place of $$(0.95)^{18}$$ and mishandles the exponent.

📌 Exam tip: Binomial needs all three pieces — the combination $$\binom{n}{k}$$, $$p^k$$, and $$(1-p)^{n-k}$$, with the exponent on the failure term equal to $$n-k$$ (here 18, not 20). Forgetting the combination or miscounting the exponent are the classic errors.

**Q2.** Operational-loss events arrive as a Poisson process with rate $$\lambda = 3$$ per quarter. What is the probability of observing 5 or more events in a single quarter?

- A. 18.5%
- B. 81.5%
- C. 10.1%
- D. 91.6%

**Answer: A**

We need $$P(X \ge 5) = 1 - P(X \le 4)$$ for Poisson($$\lambda=3$$). Use $$P(X=k)=\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$ with $$e^{-3}=0.049787$$.

1. $$P(0) = e^{-3} = 0.049787$$.
2. $$P(1) = 3 e^{-3} = 0.149361$$.
3. $$P(2) = \frac{9}{2}e^{-3} = 4.5 \times 0.049787 = 0.224042$$.
4. $$P(3) = \frac{27}{6}e^{-3} = 4.5 \times 0.049787 = 0.224042$$.
5. $$P(4) = \frac{81}{24}e^{-3} = 3.375 \times 0.049787 = 0.168031$$.
6. Sum $$P(X\le 4) = 0.049787 + 0.149361 + 0.224042 + 0.224042 + 0.168031 = 0.815263$$.
7. $$P(X\ge 5) = 1 - 0.815263 = 0.184737 \approx 18.5\%$$.

A is correct. B (81.5%) is $$P(X\le 4)$$, the complement — answering "4 or fewer" instead of "5 or more." C (10.1%) computes $$P(X\ge 5)$$ but stops the cumulative sum at $$P(X\le 3)$$, omitting $$P(4)$$. D (91.6%) is $$1-P(0)-P(1)$$ = $$P(X\ge 2)$$, the wrong threshold.

📌 Exam tip: "$$\ge 5$$" means subtract the cumulative through 4 ($$0,1,2,3,4$$) from 1. Count the terms carefully — an off-by-one in the cumulative sum is the classic Poisson trap.

**Q3.** Daily log returns of a stock are normally distributed with mean 0.05% and standard deviation 1.8%. Assuming the price today is $100, which statement is correct?

- A. Tomorrow's price is normally distributed, so it could be negative
- B. Tomorrow's price is lognormally distributed and is bounded below by zero
- C. Tomorrow's log return is lognormal with a fat right tail
- D. The price has the same standard deviation, 1.8%, as the log return

**Answer: B**

Apply the returns-vs-prices relationship: if log returns are normal, the *price* is lognormal.

1. By definition, $$X$$ is lognormal when $$\ln X$$ is normal. Here $$\ln(\text{price ratio})$$ = log return is normal, so the price ratio (and hence price) is lognormal.
2. A lognormal variable has support $$(0, \infty)$$ — it can never be negative, which matches the economic reality that prices cannot go below zero.

B is correct. A confuses the *log return* (normal) with the *price* (lognormal); a normal price could go negative, which is exactly why prices are modeled as lognormal, not normal. C reverses the relationship — the log return is normal (symmetric), not lognormal. D wrongly equates the price's standard deviation with the return's — different units and scales entirely.

📌 Exam tip: "Log returns normal" ⇒ "prices lognormal." Normal log return is symmetric; the lognormal price is right-skewed and strictly positive. Never let the price be normally distributed in these questions.

**Q4.** A trading book has 200 independent positions, each with a 1.5% probability of a limit breach during the day. Using the Poisson approximation to the binomial, the probability of observing exactly 3 breaches is closest to:

- A. 22.4%
- B. 14.9%
- C. 5.0%
- D. 16.8%

**Answer: A**

When $$n$$ is large and $$p$$ small, binomial $$\approx$$ Poisson with $$\lambda = np$$.

1. $$\lambda = np = 200 \times 0.015 = 3.0$$.
2. $$P(X=3) = \frac{\lambda^3 e^{-\lambda}}{3!} = \frac{3^3 e^{-3}}{6} = \frac{27 \times 0.049787}{6}$$.
3. Numerator: $$27 \times 0.049787 = 1.344249$$.
4. Divide: $$1.344249 / 6 = 0.224042 \approx 22.4\%$$.

A is correct. B (14.9%) is $$P(X=1)=\lambda e^{-\lambda}=3(0.049787)=0.1494$$ — wrong $$k$$. C (5.0%) is roughly $$e^{-3}=P(X=0)$$. D (16.8%) is $$P(X=4)=\frac{81}{24}e^{-3}=0.168$$ — off by one in $$k$$.

📌 Exam tip: For large $$n$$, small $$p$$, set $$\lambda=np$$ and use Poisson. Then double-check you used the $$k$$ the question asks for — adjacent $$k$$ values are planted as distractors.

**Q5.** A risk model treats monthly returns as a mixture: 90% of months are "calm" with distribution $$N(1\%, (2\%)^2)$$ and 10% are "stressed" with distribution $$N(-3\%, (8\%)^2)$$. What is the mean of the mixture's monthly return?

- A. 1.00%
- B. 0.60%
- C. $$-1.00\%$$
- D. 0.40%

**Answer: B**

The mean of a mixture is the weighted average of the component means.

1. Weights: calm 0.90, stressed 0.10.
2. Component means: calm $$+1\%$$, stressed $$-3\%$$.
3. Mixture mean: $$0.90 \times 1\% + 0.10 \times (-3\%) = 0.90\% - 0.30\% = 0.60\%$$.

B is correct. A (1.00%) ignores the stressed component entirely, using only the calm mean. C ($$-1.00\%$$) averages the two means with equal weights, $$\frac{1 + (-3)}{2}=-1$$, ignoring the 90/10 weighting. D (0.40%) miscomputes the weighted sum (e.g., $$0.90\times1 - 0.50\times... $$ slip).

📌 Exam tip: A mixture's mean is always the weight-weighted average of component means. But its *variance* is NOT the weighted average of variances — it includes a spread-of-means term, which is why mixtures with different means or variances exhibit fat tails (excess kurtosis).

**Q6.** A statistician forms the random variable $$T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/8}}$$, where $$Z \sim N(0,1)$$ and $$W \sim \chi^2_{8}$$ are independent. Which statement about $$T$$ is correct?

- A. $$T$$ follows a chi-squared distribution with 8 degrees of freedom
- B. $$T$$ follows a Student's t distribution with 8 degrees of freedom; its tails are fatter than the standard normal's, so its variance exceeds 1
- C. $$T$$ follows an F distribution with 8 and 1 degrees of freedom
- D. $$T$$ is standard normal because $$W/8 \to 1$$

**Answer: B**

Recognize the construction. A standard normal divided by the square root of an independent chi-squared over its degrees of freedom is the *definition* of a Student's t.

1. By definition, $$t_\nu = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/\nu}}$$ with $$Z\sim N(0,1)$$, $$W\sim\chi^2_\nu$$. Here $$\nu = 8$$, so $$T \sim t_8$$.
2. The t has fatter tails than the normal; its variance is $$\frac{\nu}{\nu-2} = \frac{8}{6} = 1.333 > 1$$, confirming the heavier tails.

B is correct. A confuses the construction with the chi-squared (which is a *sum of squared normals*, not this ratio). C describes the F-statistic form (ratio of two chi-squareds over their d.o.f.), not a single normal over a chi-squared. D wrongly treats $$W/8$$ as exactly 1 — it converges to 1 only as $$\nu\to\infty$$, so for finite $$\nu=8$$ the tails remain fatter than normal.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the three constructions — $$\chi^2$$ = sum of squared standard normals; $$t$$ = normal over $$\sqrt{\chi^2/\nu}$$; $$F$$ = ratio of two $$\chi^2$$ each over its d.o.f. The t's variance $$\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}>1$$ encodes its fat tails.

**Q7.** Annual operational-loss events follow a Poisson distribution. The firm observes an average of 2.5 events per quarter. Treating quarters as independent, what is the mean and variance of the *annual* number of events?

- A. Mean 2.5, variance 2.5
- B. Mean 10, variance 10
- C. Mean 10, variance 100
- D. Mean 10, variance 40

**Answer: B**

Poisson counts over disjoint intervals add, and for Poisson the mean equals the variance.

1. Annual rate: events accumulate over 4 quarters, so $$\lambda_{\text{year}} = 4 \times 2.5 = 10$$.
2. The sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson with the summed rate, so annual count $$\sim$$ Poisson(10).
3. For Poisson, mean = variance = $$\lambda$$, so mean 10 and variance 10.

B is correct. A reports the quarterly figures, failing to scale to a year. C correctly scales the mean to 10 but squares it for the variance (variance $$\ne \lambda^2$$ for Poisson — that confuses it with a multiplicative scaling). D scales the variance by 4 separately ($$4\times... $$ giving 40) as if variance scaled differently from the mean — but for a Poisson sum both scale identically by the rate.

📌 Exam tip: Poisson's signature is mean = variance = $$\lambda$$, and rates add over disjoint periods. Scaling from quarterly to annual multiplies $$\lambda$$ by 4 — applied to BOTH the mean and the variance equally.
