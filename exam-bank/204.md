**Q1.** Two assets have $$\sigma_A = 18\%$$, $$\sigma_B = 25\%$$, and correlation $$\rho = 0.40$$. A portfolio invests 70% in A and 30% in B. What is the portfolio's standard deviation (closest)?

- A. 17.05%
- B. 14.66%
- C. 20.10%
- D. 22.60%

**Answer: A**

Use the two-asset portfolio variance formula, then take the square root.

1. Covariance: $$\mathrm{Cov}(A,B) = \rho\sigma_A\sigma_B = 0.40 \times 0.18 \times 0.25 = 0.018$$.
2. Weighted terms ($$w_A=0.70$$, $$w_B=0.30$$):
   - $$w_A^2\sigma_A^2 = 0.49 \times 0.0324 = 0.015876$$
   - $$w_B^2\sigma_B^2 = 0.09 \times 0.0625 = 0.005625$$
   - $$2 w_A w_B \mathrm{Cov} = 2(0.70)(0.30)(0.018) = 0.42 \times 0.018 = 0.007560$$
3. Sum: $$\sigma_P^2 = 0.015876 + 0.005625 + 0.007560 = 0.029061$$.
4. $$\sigma_P = \sqrt{0.029061} = 0.17047 \approx 17.05\%$$.

A is correct. B (14.66%) sets $$\rho=0$$, omitting the covariance term: $$\sqrt{0.015876+0.005625}=\sqrt{0.021501}=14.66\%$$ — understates risk. C (20.10%) is the weighted average of the standard deviations $$0.70(18)+0.30(25)=20.1\%$$, the no-diversification ($$\rho=1$$) ceiling. D (22.60%) double-counts or adds an extra factor in the cross term.

📌 Exam tip: The cross term is $$2 w_A w_B \rho\sigma_A\sigma_B$$ — easy to drop the factor of 2 or the correlation. Portfolio std dev must fall between the ρ=0 value (14.66%) and the weighted-average ρ=1 value (20.10%).

**Q2.** $$X$$ and $$Y$$ have $$\sigma_X = 6$$, $$\sigma_Y = 10$$, and $$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = -18$$. What is $$\mathrm{Var}(2X - 3Y)$$?

- A. 1{,}260
- B. 828
- C. 1{,}044
- D. 1{,}476

**Answer: A**

Use $$\mathrm{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\sigma_X^2 + b^2\sigma_Y^2 + 2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$$ with $$a=2$$, $$b=-3$$.

1. $$a^2\sigma_X^2 = 4 \times 36 = 144$$.
2. $$b^2\sigma_Y^2 = 9 \times 100 = 900$$.
3. Cross term: $$2ab\,\mathrm{Cov} = 2(2)(-3)(-18)$$. Step by step: $$2\times2\times(-3) = -12$$; then $$-12 \times (-18) = +216$$.
4. Sum: $$\mathrm{Var}(2X-3Y) = 144 + 900 + 216 = 1{,}260$$.

A is correct. B (828) flips the cross-term sign to $$-216$$ ($$144+900-216$$), forgetting that the negative coefficient product times a negative covariance gives a *positive* term. C (1,044) drops the factor of 2 in the cross term, using $$+108$$ ($$144+900$$ plus a halved term). D (1,476) uses $$+2 \times 6 \times 10 \times ... $$ with raw std devs instead of $$ab$$, double-counting.

📌 Exam tip: In $$2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}$$, track the signs carefully: $$ab = (2)(-3) = -6$$; times a negative covariance flips the cross term *positive*, raising variance. Two negatives multiply to a positive — and never drop the factor of 2.

**Q3.** The joint PMF of market direction $$X$$ (0 = down, 1 = up) and a desk's outcome $$Y$$ (0 = loss, 1 = gain) is: $$P(0,0)=0.25$$, $$P(0,1)=0.10$$, $$P(1,0)=0.15$$, $$P(1,1)=0.50$$. What is $$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$$?

- A. 0.155
- B. 0.110
- C. 0.500
- D. 0.000

**Answer: B**

Compute marginals, $$E[XY]$$, then $$\mathrm{Cov} = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]$$.

1. Marginal of $$X$$: $$P(X=1) = P(1,0)+P(1,1) = 0.15+0.50 = 0.65$$, so $$E[X]=0.65$$.
2. Marginal of $$Y$$: $$P(Y=1) = P(0,1)+P(1,1) = 0.10+0.50 = 0.60$$, so $$E[Y]=0.60$$.
3. $$E[XY]$$: only the $$(1,1)$$ cell has $$XY=1$$; all others give 0, so $$E[XY] = 1\times1\times0.50 = 0.50$$.
4. $$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = 0.50 - (0.65)(0.60) = 0.50 - 0.39 = 0.11$$.

B is correct (0.110). A (0.155) subtracts the wrong product (e.g., $$0.50 - 0.345$$ using a miscomputed marginal). C (0.500) reports $$E[XY]$$ itself, forgetting to subtract $$E[X]E[Y]$$. D (0.000) wrongly assumes independence — but $$P(1,1)=0.50 \ne P(X=1)P(Y=1)=0.65\times0.60=0.39$$, so they are dependent and the covariance is nonzero.

📌 Exam tip: For binary $$X,Y$$, $$E[XY]$$ equals the single $$(1,1)$$ joint probability. Then subtract $$E[X]E[Y]$$ — never report $$E[XY]$$ alone as the covariance.

**Q4.** If $$U = -2X + 7$$ and $$V = 5Y - 3$$, and the correlation $$\rho(X,Y) = 0.6$$, what is $$\rho(U,V)$$?

- A. 0.60
- B. $$-0.60$$
- C. $$-6.00$$
- D. 0.00

**Answer: B**

Correlation is invariant in magnitude under linear transforms; only the sign of the product of the scale factors matters.

1. Rule: $$\rho(aX+b,\, cY+d) = \mathrm{sign}(ac)\,\rho(X,Y)$$.
2. Scale factors: $$a = -2$$, $$c = +5$$, so $$ac = -10 < 0 \Rightarrow \mathrm{sign}(ac) = -1$$.
3. Therefore $$\rho(U,V) = -1 \times 0.6 = -0.60$$.

B is correct. A keeps the original sign, ignoring the negative scale on $$X$$. C ($$-6.00$$) wrongly scales the correlation by the product of the coefficients $$ac\times\rho = -10\times0.6$$ — but correlation magnitude never changes, and it must stay within $$[-1,1]$$. D assumes the transforms destroy correlation — they don't; only the sign can flip.

📌 Exam tip: Linear transforms leave $$|\rho|$$ untouched; the only effect is a sign flip when the scale factors have opposite signs. Any answer outside $$[-1,1]$$ (like $$-6$$) is automatically wrong.

**Q5.** Daily returns are iid with daily standard deviation 0.9%. Assuming the square-root-of-time rule holds, what is the standard deviation of the cumulative return over 16 trading days, and how would positive autocorrelation change the *true* multi-period risk?

- A. 3.6%; true risk would be lower than 3.6%
- B. 3.6%; true risk would be higher than 3.6%
- C. 14.4%; true risk would be higher than 3.6%
- D. 0.225%; true risk would be unchanged

**Answer: B**

Use $$\sigma_{\text{sum}} = \sigma\sqrt{t}$$ for iid returns, then reason about autocorrelation.

1. $$\sigma_{16} = 0.9\% \times \sqrt{16} = 0.9\% \times 4 = 3.6\%$$.
2. The $$\sqrt{t}$$ rule assumes zero autocorrelation. With *positive* autocorrelation, shocks persist and reinforce across days, so true multi-period variance *exceeds* $$t\sigma^2$$ and true risk is *higher* than $$\sigma\sqrt{t}=3.6\%$$.

B is correct. A correctly computes 3.6% but reverses the autocorrelation effect (positive autocorrelation raises, not lowers, multi-period risk — mean reversion would lower it). C scales variance instead of std dev ($$0.9\%\times16 = 14.4\%$$), confusing the two scaling laws. D scales by $$1/t$$ or divides, producing 0.225%, and wrongly claims autocorrelation is irrelevant.

📌 Exam tip: Variance scales with $$t$$, std dev with $$\sqrt{t}$$. The $$\sqrt{t}$$ rule needs iid returns; positive autocorrelation ⇒ true risk *above* $$\sigma\sqrt{t}$$, mean reversion ⇒ *below*.

**Q6.** Which statement about the relationship between zero covariance and independence is correct?

- A. Zero covariance always implies independence
- B. Independence always implies zero covariance, but zero covariance implies independence only when the variables are jointly normal
- C. Zero covariance never implies independence, even for jointly normal variables
- D. Independence implies zero covariance only if both variables have zero mean

**Answer: B**

Recall the precise logical relationship.

1. Independence $$\Rightarrow$$ $$\mathrm{Cov} = 0$$ always (independent variables have $$E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$$).
2. The converse fails in general: covariance captures only *linear* association. Example: $$Y = X^2$$ with symmetric $$X$$ has $$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$$ yet $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are perfectly dependent.
3. The one exception where zero covariance *does* imply independence is joint normality.

B is correct. A overstates the converse — zero covariance does not imply independence in general (the $$Y=X^2$$ counterexample). C wrongly denies the joint-normal exception, where uncorrelated does imply independent. D adds a spurious zero-mean condition — independence implies zero covariance regardless of the means.

📌 Exam tip: "Uncorrelated" = no *linear* relationship. Independence ⇒ zero covariance always; the reverse holds only under joint normality. This exact distinction is a perennial exam favorite.

**Q7.** A market-neutral book is long position $$X$$ and short position $$Y$$, with payoff $$X - Y$$. Given $$\sigma_X = 12\%$$, $$\sigma_Y = 9\%$$, and $$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = +0.0081$$, what is the standard deviation of the long-short payoff?

- A. 15.00%
- B. 7.94%
- C. 21.00%
- D. 5.61%

**Answer: B**

For a difference, $$\mathrm{Var}(X - Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$$.

1. $$\sigma_X^2 = 0.12^2 = 0.0144$$.
2. $$\sigma_Y^2 = 0.09^2 = 0.0081$$.
3. Cross term (subtracted for a difference): $$2\mathrm{Cov} = 2 \times 0.0081 = 0.0162$$.
4. $$\mathrm{Var}(X-Y) = 0.0144 + 0.0081 - 0.0162 = 0.0063$$.
5. $$\sigma = \sqrt{0.0063} = 0.07937 \approx 7.94\%$$.

B is correct. A (15.00%) uses $$\sqrt{0.0144+0.0081} = \sqrt{0.0225} = 15\%$$, dropping the covariance term (treating positive covariance as if it didn't reduce the spread of a difference). C (21.00%) *adds* the cross term, $$\sqrt{0.0144+0.0081+0.0162}$$, applying the sum formula instead of the difference. D (5.61%) subtracts the covariance once without the factor of 2, or makes a square-root slip.

📌 Exam tip: For a *difference*, the covariance term is *subtracted*: positive covariance between a long and a short reduces net volatility (a hedge). For a *sum*, it is added. The factor of 2 is mandatory.
