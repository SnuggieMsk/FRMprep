**Q1.** An investor goes long 3 crude oil futures contracts (1,000 barrels each) at $78.00/bbl. Initial margin is $6,600 per contract and maintenance margin is $4,800 per contract. Settlement prices over four days are: Day 1 = $77.10, Day 2 = $75.40, Day 3 = $76.20, Day 4 = $74.10. On which day(s) does a margin call occur, and what is the total of all margin calls?
- A. Day 2 only; total call = $7,800
- B. Day 2 and Day 4; total call = $13,800
- C. Day 4 only; total call = $5,400
- D. Day 2 only; total call = $5,100

**Answer: A**

The ask: track the margin balance across four days for 3 contracts (3,000 bbl total), flag each day the balance falls *below* maintenance, and restore to *initial*.

1. Totals: initial margin = 3 × $6,600 = $19,800; maintenance margin = 3 × $4,800 = $14,400. Position size = 3,000 bbl, so each $1 price move = $3,000.

2. Day 1: 78.00 → 77.10, change −0.90. Loss = 0.90 × 3,000 = $2,700. Balance = 19,800 − 2,700 = $17,100. Above $14,400 → no call.

3. Day 2: 77.10 → 75.40, change −1.70. Loss = 1.70 × 3,000 = $5,100. Balance = 17,100 − 5,100 = $12,000. Below $14,400 → **margin call** = 19,800 − 12,000 = **$7,800**. Balance restored to $19,800.

4. Day 3: 75.40 → 76.20, change +0.80. Gain = 0.80 × 3,000 = $2,400. Balance = 19,800 + 2,400 = $22,200. No call.

5. Day 4: 76.20 → 74.10, change −2.10. Loss = 2.10 × 3,000 = $6,300. Balance = 22,200 − 6,300 = $15,900. This is *above* $14,400 → no call.

So a single margin call occurs on **Day 2** for **$7,800**, total = $7,800. This is option **A**.

- **B** and the Day-4 traps assume any large daily loss triggers a call; on Day 4 the balance ($15,900) is still above maintenance ($14,400) because the Day-3 gain rebuilt the account, so no call fires.
- **C** ignores the genuine Day-2 call and invents a Day-4 call.
- **D** restores only to maintenance (19,800 − 14,400 logic / or just the daily loss $5,100) rather than to *initial* — the call must restore to initial margin, giving $7,800, not $5,100.

📌 Exam tip: A call fires only when the *balance* drops **below maintenance**, and you then restore to **initial** (not to maintenance). A big daily loss alone does not trigger a call if the running balance stays at/above maintenance.

**Q2.** A speculator shorts 4 silver futures (5,000 oz each) at $24.00/oz. Initial margin is $11,000 per contract; maintenance margin is $8,000 per contract. To what price must silver *rise* before the short receives the first margin call?
- A. $24.40
- B. $24.60
- C. $25.50
- D. $26.00

**Answer: B**

The ask: find the price increase that drives a *short's* balance below maintenance.

1. Totals: initial margin = 4 × $11,000 = $44,000; maintenance = 4 × $8,000 = $32,000. Position = 4 × 5,000 = 20,000 oz, so each $1 move = $20,000.

2. The short *loses* when price rises. A call occurs when balance falls below maintenance, i.e., when cumulative loss exceeds initial − maintenance = 44,000 − 32,000 = $12,000.

3. Price move needed: loss of $12,000 / $20,000 per $1 = $0.60 increase. Trigger price = 24.00 + 0.60 = **$24.60**.

- **A** ($24.40) uses a $0.40 move — would correspond to an $8,000 loss, wrongly equating the trigger to maintenance margin itself rather than (IM − MM).
- **C** ($25.50) uses the full initial margin ($1.50 move = $30,000 ≈ wrong basis).
- **D** ($26.00) uses a $2.00 move ($40,000), near full IM, also wrong.

📌 Exam tip: For a short, a call triggers after a *price rise* equal to (IM − MM) ÷ (contracts × contract size). Here ($44,000 − $32,000)/20,000 = $0.60.

**Q3.** Day-start open interest is 2,500 contracts. During the day: (i) Trader A opens a new long buying 60 from Trader B who opens a new short; (ii) Trader C closes an existing long by selling 40 to Trader D who opens a new long; (iii) Trader E closes a long by selling 25 to Trader F who buys to close an existing short; (iv) Trader G opens a new short selling 30 to Trader H who buys to close a short. What are end-of-day open interest and total volume?
- A. OI = 2,535; volume = 155
- B. OI = 2,535; volume = 130
- C. OI = 2,505; volume = 155
- D. OI = 2,560; volume = 155

**Answer: A**

The ask: apply the open-interest rules per trade and sum volume (every trade adds to volume regardless of OI effect).

1. Trade (i): new long + new short → both open → OI **+60**. Volume +60.
2. Trade (ii): closing long + new long → one opens, one closes → OI **unchanged**. Volume +40.
3. Trade (iii): closing long + closing short → both close → OI **−25**. Volume +25.
4. Trade (iv): new short + closing short → one opens (new short), one closes (buy to close a short) → OI **unchanged**. Volume +30.

5. End-of-day OI = 2,500 + 60 + 0 − 25 + 0 = 2,560 − 25 = **2,535**.
6. Total volume = 60 + 40 + 25 + 30 = **155** (every trade counts).

So OI = 2,535 and volume = 155, which is option **A**.

- **B** has the right OI but undercounts volume (it drops one trade — volume counts *all* 155 contracts traded).
- **C** miscomputes OI by treating trade (iv) as closing-closing (−30), giving 2,505.
- **D** treats trade (ii) as opening-opening (+40), giving 2,560.

📌 Exam tip: Volume counts *every* contract traded; OI changes only with the open/close mix — both opening = +; both closing = −; one of each = unchanged.

**Q4.** A portfolio manager is long an equity futures position at 4,200. She wants to guarantee an automatic exit if the market rallies to a target of 4,260 so she can take profits, and she is willing to accept whatever fill the market gives once that level prints. Which order type is appropriate?
- A. Limit sell at 4,260
- B. Stop (stop-loss) sell at 4,260
- C. Market-if-touched (MIT) sell at 4,260
- D. Stop-limit sell at 4,260

**Answer: C**

The ask: choose the order that fires a *market* order to *take profit* at a *favorable* (higher) price for a long.

1. The manager is long and wants to *sell into a rally* — entering/exiting at a favorable price as the market moves up to 4,260.
2. A **market-if-touched (MIT)** becomes a market order when the price is touched, used to enter/exit at a *favorable* price (the opposite trigger side from a stop). For a long taking profit on a rise, MIT-sell at 4,260 fires a market order when 4,260 prints, accepting the fill — exactly the requirement.

- **A** (limit sell at 4,260) would fill at 4,260 or better but might be skipped in a fast rally or not guarantee execution; the manager wants a guaranteed fire (market) once touched.
- **B** (stop sell) is used to *cap losses* on a long and triggers when price falls *to/through* the stop, i.e., on the *downside* — wrong trigger side for taking profit on a rally.
- **D** (stop-limit) becomes a *limit* order (may not fill in a fast market) and is again a downside loss-cap mechanism — wrong direction and not guaranteed to execute.

📌 Exam tip: Stop orders trigger on adverse moves to cap losses; MIT triggers on *favorable* moves to enter/take profit. MIT becomes a market order (guaranteed fire, uncertain price); stop-limit becomes a limit order (may not fill).

**Q5.** Which statement comparing margin requirements and the role of the clearing house is correct?
- A. Exchanges set higher margins for bona fide hedgers than speculators because hedgers hold positions longer
- B. Bona fide hedgers and spread positions typically receive *lower* margin requirements than outright speculators because their offsetting exposures reduce total risk
- C. The clearing house collects variation margin but never initial margin from clearing members
- D. Maintenance margin is typically set above initial margin to provide a buffer

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct statement about margining and the clearing house.

1. Exchanges set *lower* margins for bona fide hedgers (and for spreads and day trades) because the futures position offsets an underlying exposure (or another futures leg), so net risk is lower. B is correct.

- **A** reverses the relationship: hedgers get *lower*, not higher, margins.
- **C** is false: the clearing house collects *both* initial and variation margin from clearing members (who collect from clients) and guarantees performance via novation.
- **D** is false: maintenance margin is *below* initial margin (typically ~75% of IM); the gap is the buffer.

📌 Exam tip: Hedgers/spreads/day trades = lower margins (lower net risk). Maintenance < Initial. The clearing house novates and collects IM + VM, virtually eliminating counterparty risk.

**Q6.** Which of the following correctly distinguishes a forward contract from an exchange-traded futures contract?
- A. Forwards realize gains and losses daily through marking to market, while futures settle only at maturity
- B. Futures are customized OTC agreements, while forwards are standardized and exchange-traded
- C. Forwards expose holders to counterparty (dealer) credit risk and settle once at maturity, while futures are marked to market daily and carry minimal counterparty risk via the clearing house
- D. Forwards are easier to close out than futures because they can be offset by an identical exchange trade

**Answer: C**

The ask: pick the accurate forward-vs-futures contrast.

1. Forwards: OTC, customized, settle once at maturity, bear dealer counterparty/credit risk, harder to unwind (negotiate with the counterparty).
2. Futures: exchange-traded, standardized, marked to market daily (gains/losses realized daily), minimal counterparty risk via the clearing house/margining, easily offset. C captures this exactly.

- **A** swaps the settlement features (it is *futures* that mark to market daily).
- **B** swaps the venues (futures are exchange-traded/standardized; forwards are OTC/customized).
- **D** is backwards: *futures* are easier to close out (offsetting exchange trade); forwards require negotiating with the original counterparty.

📌 Exam tip: Forward = OTC, custom, single settlement, credit risk, hard to unwind. Futures = exchange, standardized, daily MTM, clearing-house guaranteed, easy offset.

**Q7.** As a deliverable commodity futures contract approaches expiration, the futures price and spot price converge. Which statement best explains why, and what defines an *inverted* (downward-sloping) futures market?
- A. Convergence occurs because of daily settlement; an inverted market has futures prices rising with maturity
- B. Convergence is enforced by arbitrage (buy spot/sell futures and deliver, or the reverse); an inverted market has futures prices *decreasing* with maturity, often from a high convenience yield
- C. Convergence is a regulatory requirement; an inverted market shows the futures price equal to spot at every maturity
- D. Convergence happens only for cash-settled contracts; an inverted market has the highest price at the longest maturity

**Answer: B**

The ask: explain convergence and define an inverted market.

1. Near expiry, if futures ≠ spot, an arbitrageur could buy the cheaper and sell the dearer and deliver, locking in a riskless profit. This arbitrage forces the futures price to the spot price at delivery — convergence is *arbitrage-enforced*.
2. An **inverted market** (downward-sloping term structure) has futures prices *decreasing* with maturity, typically when the convenience yield is high (tight supply) or prices are expected to fall.

- **A** misattributes convergence to daily settlement and describes a *normal* (not inverted) market.
- **C** is false: convergence is arbitrage-driven, not a regulation; inverted ≠ flat.
- **D** is false: convergence applies to deliverable contracts via arbitrage; an inverted market has *lower* prices at longer maturities.

📌 Exam tip: Convergence = arbitrage. Normal market = futures rise with maturity (carry costs dominate); inverted market = futures fall with maturity (high convenience yield / expected declines).
