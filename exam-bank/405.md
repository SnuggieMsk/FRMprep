**Q1.** A sovereign's 5-year USD bond CDS trades at 360 basis points. Assuming a loss given default of 80%, the implied (risk-neutral) annual default probability is closest to:

- A. 2.88%
- B. 4.50%
- C. 3.60%
- D. 0.45%

**Answer: B**

The ask: convert a CDS spread to an implied risk-neutral annual PD using the small-spread approximation.

1. Approximation: $$s \approx PD \times LGD$$, so $$PD \approx \frac{s}{LGD}$$.
2. Spread in decimal: $$360\text{ bp} = 0.0360$$.
3. PD: $$\frac{0.0360}{0.80} = 0.045 = 4.50\%$$.

- A. 2.88% *multiplies* spread by LGD ($$0.036 \times 0.80$$) instead of dividing — the classic inversion error.
- C. 3.60% sets PD equal to the spread (ignores LGD entirely, i.e., assumes LGD = 100%).
- D. 0.45% mis-scales by a factor of 10 (treats 360 bp as 0.0036).
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: $$PD \approx s/LGD$$ — *divide* the spread by LGD. Multiplying (or forgetting to divide) is the trap. And the result is a *risk-neutral* PD, which exceeds the real-world default frequency because it embeds risk premia and liquidity.

**Q2.** A sovereign borrows in its own currency, issued under domestic law, with an independent central bank that can print. Which statement is correct?

- A. The sovereign cannot default on local-currency debt because it can always print money.
- B. The sovereign may still default on local-currency debt if monetizing would cause inflation or currency collapse judged more costly than restructuring.
- C. Local-currency default is impossible only if the country uses the euro.
- D. Local-currency ratings must be lower than foreign-currency ratings.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct view on local-currency sovereign default.

1. Printing to repay is *possible*, but it can trigger high inflation and currency collapse — costs that may exceed those of a restructuring.
2. Default is partly a *choice*: a sovereign weighs monetization costs against restructuring costs. Historical local-currency defaults (e.g., Russia 1998) confirm it happens.
3. Some sovereigns also *cannot* print (euro members like Greece, currency boards), removing the option entirely.

- A is the false statement GARP loves: printing ability does not make default impossible.
- C is backwards — euro membership *removes* the printing option, making default *more* likely, not the only case of possibility.
- D is false: local-currency ratings are usually *equal to or higher* than foreign-currency ratings.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: "A sovereign that prints its own currency cannot default on local-currency debt" is a classic false statement. Default is a cost-benefit choice; monetization's inflation cost can exceed restructuring's cost, and some sovereigns cannot print at all.

**Q3.** Comparing sovereign credit ratings, bond default spreads, and CDS spreads as measures of sovereign risk, which statement is most accurate?

- A. Ratings react faster than CDS spreads and contain less noise.
- B. CDS and bond spreads are continuous and tend to lead ratings, but embed liquidity and risk-premium noise; ratings are smoother but lag.
- C. Bond default spreads are risk-free measures with no liquidity contamination.
- D. CDS-implied PDs equal historical (real-world) default frequencies.

**Answer: B**

The ask: contrast the three sovereign-risk measures on timeliness and noise.

1. Ratings are episodic and smooth; they *lag* markets (downgrades follow spread widening). Low noise but stale.
2. Bond default spreads and CDS spreads are continuous, forward-looking, and tend to *lead* ratings — but carry liquidity premia, currency mismatch, and risk-appetite noise.
3. CDS-implied PDs are *risk-neutral* and systematically exceed real-world default frequencies (they price risk aversion + liquidity).

- A reverses timeliness: ratings *lag*, markets lead.
- C is false: bond spreads carry liquidity and currency-mismatch noise.
- D is false: risk-neutral ≠ real-world; CDS PDs are higher.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Ratings = smooth but lagging; market spreads = timely but noisy and risk-neutral. Best practice uses both — ratings for the level, spreads for direction/early warning. "Risk-neutral PD = historical PD" is a guaranteed trap.

**Q4.** Most sovereign defaults historically have occurred on foreign-currency debt. The single best explanation is:

- A. Foreign-currency debt always carries a higher coupon.
- B. The sovereign cannot print the foreign currency, so once FX reserves are exhausted it cannot pay.
- C. Foreign-currency debt is always issued at shorter maturities.
- D. Rating agencies prohibit local-currency default.

**Answer: B**

The ask: explain why foreign-currency defaults dominate.

1. A sovereign controls only its *own* currency's printing press. It cannot print USD or EUR.
2. Foreign-currency obligations must be paid from FX reserves or earned/borrowed foreign exchange; when reserves run out and access is cut off, payment becomes impossible.
3. By contrast, local-currency debt can (institutional constraints aside) be monetized, so default there is more often a choice than a hard constraint.

- A: coupon level is not the structural reason for default frequency.
- C: maturity structure matters for rollover risk but is not the defining reason foreign-currency defaults dominate.
- D is fabricated; agencies do not prohibit default.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Foreign-currency default ≈ a *capacity* problem (can't print FX, reserves run dry). Local-currency default ≈ a *willingness/cost* problem (printing is possible but inflationary). This capacity-vs-willingness distinction is the heart of the chapter.

**Q5.** A sovereign's foreign-currency rating is BB and its local-currency rating is BBB. Which statement best explains the gap?

- A. The agency made an error; the two should always match.
- B. Local-currency capacity is stronger because the sovereign can print its own currency and tax domestic holders, so the local-currency rating is typically equal or 1–2 notches higher.
- C. Foreign-currency debt is safer, so its rating should be higher than shown.
- D. The local-currency rating reflects the largest domestic bank's rating.

**Answer: B**

The ask: explain why a local-currency rating exceeds the foreign-currency rating.

1. Agencies assign separate foreign- and local-currency ratings.
2. Local-currency obligations benefit from the printing press and the ability to tax domestic holders — greater capacity to pay — so the local-currency rating is typically equal to or 1–2 notches *higher*.
3. A two-notch gap (BBB local vs BB foreign) is entirely normal, not an error.

- A is false: the ratings differ by design.
- C reverses the relationship — foreign currency is the *riskier* obligation.
- D is fabricated; sovereign local-currency ratings are not set to a bank's rating.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Local-currency rating ≥ foreign-currency rating (usually 1–2 notches higher) because of the printing press and domestic taxation. A higher local-currency rating is expected, not anomalous.

**Q6.** Sovereign ratings have been criticized for procyclicality. Which behavior best illustrates this?

- A. Slow, incremental single-notch upgrades during expansions.
- B. Clustered multi-notch downgrades during crises (e.g., Asia 1997, eurozone 2010–12), which can deepen the crisis.
- C. Assigning identical ratings to all sovereigns in a region permanently.
- D. Refusing to assign local-currency ratings.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the procyclical sovereign-rating behavior.

1. Procyclicality means rating actions amplify the cycle. Upgrades are slow and incremental; *downgrades cluster and cascade* in crises.
2. Sharp multi-notch downgrades during stress (Asia 1997, eurozone 2010–12) raise borrowing costs and trigger forced selling exactly when the sovereign is weakest, deepening the crisis. Sovereign ceilings also drag down banks/corporates.

- A describes upgrade behavior, not the procyclical (downgrade-cascade) problem.
- C is not observed and not the criticism.
- D is unrelated and false.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Procyclicality = clustered multi-notch downgrades in crises that worsen the downturn. Upgrades slow and small; downgrades fast and cascading (plus sovereign-ceiling spillover to banks/corporates).

**Q7.** Which factor is correctly classified as a source of country risk under the standard framework?

- A. Commodity/sector concentration of the economy — an element of economic-structure risk.
- B. The risk-free rate of the United States — a legal-system risk.
- C. The volatility of a single firm's earnings — a political risk.
- D. The choice of VaR confidence level — an economic-maturity risk.

**Answer: A**

The ask: match a factor to the correct country-risk source category.

1. Country-risk sources: economic structure, political risk, legal system, economic maturity.
2. Commodity/sector concentration (e.g., an oil exporter dependent on crude prices) falls squarely under **economic structure** — concentration and lack of diversification amplify shocks.

- B: the US risk-free rate is not a legal-system risk; it is a global benchmark rate, unrelated to a country's legal framework.
- C: a single firm's earnings volatility is idiosyncratic firm risk, not political (country) risk.
- D: VaR confidence level is a measurement choice, not a country-risk source.
- A is correct.

📌 Exam tip: The four country-risk buckets are economic structure (diversification/concentration, growth stage), political risk (continuity, corruption, expropriation, war), legal system (property rights, contract enforcement), and economic maturity (volatility of early-stage economies). **Q8.** An analyst values an emerging-market project and adds a country risk premium to the discount rate. The CDS spread of the sovereign is 250 bp and the analyst scales it to estimate the premium. Which statement is most accurate for FRM Part I purposes?

- A. The country risk premium should be set to zero because CDS spreads are pure noise.
- B. A scaled sovereign default spread is commonly added to the discount rate as a country risk premium; the concept is examinable, the precise scaling mechanics are not.
- C. The country risk premium must equal exactly the CDS spread divided by the equity beta.
- D. Adding a country risk premium double-counts risk and is never appropriate.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct Part I framing of the country-risk-premium concept.

1. Analysts commonly add a country risk premium — often a scaled sovereign default/CDS spread — to the discount rate for emerging-market investments.
2. For FRM Part I, the *concept* (linking sovereign spreads to a discount-rate add-on) is what is tested, not a precise scaling formula.

- A is false: spreads carry information (forward-looking risk), not pure noise.
- C invents a precise formula that Part I does not require and is not the standard approach.
- D is false: a country risk premium is a standard, appropriate adjustment, not double-counting.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: For Part I, know that a scaled sovereign default spread can serve as a country risk premium added to discount rates — understand the *idea*, not exact mechanics. Distractors that demand precise formulas or dismiss the premium entirely are wrong.
