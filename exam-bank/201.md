**Q1.** A credit analyst classifies obligors as high-risk (15% of the book) or low-risk (85%). A high-risk obligor defaults within a year with probability 0.30; a low-risk obligor with probability 0.04. An obligor selected at random defaulted this year. What is the probability the obligor was high-risk?

- A. 30.0%
- B. 50.0%
- C. 56.9%
- D. 76.0%

**Answer: C**

We are asked for the posterior $$P(\text{High} \mid \text{Default})$$ — invert the conditional using Bayes' theorem.

1. Joint probability of high-risk AND default: $$0.15 \times 0.30 = 0.045$$.
2. Joint probability of low-risk AND default: $$0.85 \times 0.04 = 0.034$$.
3. Total (unconditional) default probability: $$0.045 + 0.034 = 0.079$$.
4. Bayes: $$P(\text{High} \mid \text{Default}) = \dfrac{0.045}{0.079} = 0.5696 \approx 56.9\%$$.

C is correct. A (30%) is the *conditional default rate of a high-risk obligor*, $$P(\text{Default}\mid\text{High})$$ — the question asks for the reverse direction, the classic Bayes inversion error. D (76%) uses only the high-risk joint over the wrong base, $$0.045/0.059$$-type miscount, or confuses with the proportion of defaults that are low risk inverted. B (50%) is a "split the difference" guess with no support.

📌 Exam tip: When a stem gives you $$P(\text{evidence}\mid\text{state})$$ and asks for $$P(\text{state}\mid\text{evidence})$$, you MUST run Bayes — never report the given conditional back. Build a two-cell numerator/denominator: target joint over total joint.

**Q2.** Events $$A$$ and $$B$$ satisfy $$P(A) = 0.50$$, $$P(B) = 0.40$$, and $$P(A \cup B) = 0.70$$. Which statement is correct?

- A. $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive
- B. $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent
- C. $$P(A \mid B) = 0.40$$
- D. $$P(A \cup B)$$ should equal $$0.90$$

**Answer: B**

We must find $$P(A \cap B)$$, then test the relationships.

1. Addition rule: $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.50 + 0.40 - 0.70 = 0.20$$.
2. Independence check: $$P(A)P(B) = 0.50 \times 0.40 = 0.20 = P(A\cap B)$$. They match, so $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent.
3. Verify via conditional: $$P(A\mid B) = 0.20/0.40 = 0.50 = P(A)$$ — knowing $$B$$ does not change $$A$$, confirming independence.

B is correct. A is false: mutual exclusivity requires $$P(A\cap B)=0$$, but the intersection is 0.20. C reports $$P(A\mid B)=0.40$$, but the actual value is $$0.50$$ (it mistakes $$P(A\mid B)$$ for $$P(B)$$). D is the double-counting error: $$0.50+0.40=0.90$$ omits the subtraction of the intersection, which the correct addition rule includes.

📌 Exam tip: Independence test is always $$P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$. Mutual exclusivity is $$P(A\cap B)=0$$. For positive-probability events the two are opposites — never both at once.

**Q3.** A fraud filter flags 92% of fraudulent transactions and 3% of legitimate ones. Historically 0.5% of transactions are fraudulent. Given that a transaction is flagged, the probability it is actually fraudulent is closest to:

- A. 92.0%
- B. 13.4%
- C. 86.6%
- D. 0.5%

**Answer: B**

We need $$P(\text{Fraud} \mid \text{Flag})$$ via Bayes.

1. Fraud and flagged: $$0.005 \times 0.92 = 0.0046$$.
2. Legit and flagged: $$0.995 \times 0.03 = 0.02985$$.
3. Total flagged: $$0.0046 + 0.02985 = 0.03445$$.
4. $$P(\text{Fraud}\mid\text{Flag}) = \dfrac{0.0046}{0.03445} = 0.1335 \approx 13.4\%$$.

B is correct. A (92%) is the detection sensitivity $$P(\text{Flag}\mid\text{Fraud})$$, reported backwards. D (0.5%) is the prior, ignoring the evidence entirely. C (86.6%) is $$1 - 0.134$$ — the probability it is legitimate given a flag, the complement reported as the answer. This is the "base-rate fallacy": a rare event plus a small false-positive rate still yields mostly false alarms.

📌 Exam tip: When the prior probability is tiny, even a highly accurate test produces a posterior far below the sensitivity — the legitimate population swamps the flags. Always compute both joint terms.

**Q4.** A bond portfolio defaults with probability 12% in a recession and 2% in an expansion. The economy will be in recession next year with probability 30%, otherwise expansion. Two independent, identical bonds are held. Conditional on the economic state, defaults are independent. What is the probability that BOTH bonds default next year?

- A. 0.25%
- B. 0.46%
- C. 5.00%
- D. 1.44%

**Answer: B**

We need the unconditional probability that both default, accounting for state-dependent default rates. Defaults are independent *given* the state, not unconditionally.

1. In recession, both default: $$(0.12)^2 = 0.0144$$.
2. In expansion, both default: $$(0.02)^2 = 0.0004$$.
3. Weight by state probabilities (total probability rule):
$$P(\text{both}) = 0.30 \times 0.0144 + 0.70 \times 0.0004 = 0.00432 + 0.00028 = 0.0046 = 0.46\%.$$

B is correct. A (0.25%) squares the *blended* unconditional default rate $$p = 0.30(0.12)+0.70(0.02)=0.050$$, giving $$0.050^2 = 0.0025$$ — this wrongly assumes the bonds are unconditionally independent and ignores the common economic factor. C (5.00%) is that blended single-bond default rate itself, not a joint probability. D (1.44%) uses only the recession term $$(0.12)^2=0.0144$$ and forgets to weight by the 30% recession probability (and to add the expansion term).

📌 Exam tip: "Independent *conditional on* the state" does NOT mean unconditionally independent. You must square the default probability *inside each state* and then weight — squaring the blended rate understates joint defaults because it ignores the common factor (the economy).

**Q5.** A risk committee assigns prior probabilities to three market regimes for the coming month: calm (60%), volatile (30%), crisis (10%). The probability that the index falls more than 5% in a month is 2% in calm, 15% in volatile, and 50% in crisis. The index fell more than 5% last month. The probability the regime was "crisis" is closest to:

- A. 10.0%
- B. 46.7%
- C. 50.0%
- D. 42.1%

**Answer: B**

We need $$P(\text{Crisis} \mid \text{Drop})$$ with three states (Bayes with total-probability denominator).

1. Joint terms:
   - Calm & drop: $$0.60 \times 0.02 = 0.012$$
   - Volatile & drop: $$0.30 \times 0.15 = 0.045$$
   - Crisis & drop: $$0.10 \times 0.50 = 0.050$$
2. Total probability of a drop: $$0.012 + 0.045 + 0.050 = 0.107$$.
3. $$P(\text{Crisis}\mid\text{Drop}) = \dfrac{0.050}{0.107} = 0.4673 \approx 46.7\%$$.

B is correct. A (10%) is the prior, ignoring the evidence. C (50%) is $$P(\text{Drop}\mid\text{Crisis})$$ reported backwards. D (42.1%) is the *volatile* posterior $$0.045/0.107 = 0.4206$$ — the right method but the wrong state selected.

📌 Exam tip: With more than two states, the Bayes denominator is the full sum of all joint terms. Forgetting one state (often the "calm/normal" one with low conditional probability) is the most common error.

**Q6.** $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are two events with $$P(X)=0.5$$, $$P(Y)=0.3$$, and $$P(X \cap Y)=0.15$$. Which of the following is TRUE?

- A. $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are mutually exclusive
- B. $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are independent, and $$P(X \cup Y)=0.65$$
- C. $$P(Y \mid X)=0.30$$ and the events are dependent
- D. $$P(X \cup Y)=0.80$$

**Answer: B**

Check independence and compute the union.

1. Independence test: $$P(X)P(Y) = 0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.15 = P(X\cap Y)$$. Equal, so $$X$$ and $$Y$$ ARE independent.
2. Union: $$P(X\cup Y) = 0.5 + 0.3 - 0.15 = 0.65$$.
3. Conditional: $$P(Y\mid X) = 0.15/0.5 = 0.30 = P(Y)$$ — confirms independence (knowing $$X$$ doesn't change $$Y$$).

B is correct. A is false: $$P(X\cap Y)=0.15\ne 0$$. C correctly computes $$P(Y\mid X)=0.30$$ but wrongly concludes "dependent" — equality with $$P(Y)$$ proves independence, so C is the subtle trap. D adds the marginals without subtracting the intersection: $$0.5+0.3=0.80$$ — the double-counting error.

📌 Exam tip: $$P(Y\mid X)=P(Y)$$ is itself the independence definition. If a stem hands you that equality, the events are independent — and the union always subtracts the intersection once.

**Q7.** During a recession (probability 0.20), a particular hedge fund posts a loss with probability 0.65. In normal times (probability 0.80), it posts a loss with probability 0.10. Over a year, what is the unconditional probability the fund posts a loss, and given a loss, what is the probability the economy was normal?

- A. Loss = 21%; P(normal | loss) = 38.1%
- B. Loss = 75%; P(normal | loss) = 80.0%
- C. Loss = 21%; P(normal | loss) = 80.0%
- D. Loss = 13%; P(normal | loss) = 61.9%

**Answer: A**

Two-part: total probability, then Bayes.

1. Joint terms:
   - Recession & loss: $$0.20 \times 0.65 = 0.13$$
   - Normal & loss: $$0.80 \times 0.10 = 0.08$$
2. Unconditional loss probability: $$0.13 + 0.08 = 0.21 = 21\%$$.
3. Bayes: $$P(\text{Normal}\mid\text{Loss}) = \dfrac{0.08}{0.21} = 0.3810 \approx 38.1\%$$.

A is correct. C uses the right unconditional loss (21%) but reports the prior $$P(\text{Normal})=0.80$$ instead of the posterior — failing to update on the loss evidence. B adds the conditional loss rates $$0.65+0.10=0.75$$ (nonsense — conditionals don't add) and reports the prior. D miscomputes the joint (uses $$0.20\times0.65=0.13$$ alone as the total, dropping the normal-state contribution) and then inverts using $$0.08/0.13$$.

📌 Exam tip: Unconditional probability = SUM of joint terms across all states. The posterior $$P(\text{state}\mid\text{event})$$ is a single joint term divided by that sum — never the prior, and conditional rates never add.
