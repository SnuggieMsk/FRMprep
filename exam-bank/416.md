**Q1.** A dealer writes 2,000 call options (each on one share) with a delta of 0.58. To establish a delta-neutral hedge today, the dealer should:

- A. Buy 1,160 shares.
- B. Sell 1,160 shares.
- C. Buy 2,000 shares.
- D. Sell 840 shares.

**Answer: A**

The ask: hedge a short call position to delta-neutral by trading shares.

1. The dealer is SHORT 2,000 calls. A long call has delta +0.58, so a SHORT call has delta −0.58 per option to the writer.
2. Position delta:
   $$-2{,}000 \times 0.58 = -1{,}160$$
3. To neutralize a delta of −1,160, add +1,160 of delta. Each share has delta +1, so BUY 1,160 shares.

- B (sell) moves the wrong way — selling shares makes the delta more negative (−2,320), worsening the exposure.
- C (buy 2,000) ignores the 0.58 delta and over-hedges.
- D (sell 840) is both the wrong direction and an unmotivated size.

📌 Exam tip: Position delta = (number of options, signed) × (delta per option). A short call has NEGATIVE delta to the writer; offset it by BUYING shares (delta +1 each). Get the position sign right first.

**Q2.** A dealer is short 1,000 calls with delta 0.60 and has already bought 600 shares to be delta-neutral. The stock rallies and the calls' delta rises to 0.72. To restore delta-neutrality the dealer must:

- A. Buy 120 more shares.
- B. Sell 120 shares.
- C. Buy 720 shares.
- D. Sell 600 shares.

**Answer: A**

The ask: rebalance a delta hedge after the underlying moves (dynamic hedging).

1. New position delta from the options: short 1,000 calls at delta 0.72 →
   $$-1{,}000 \times 0.72 = -720$$
2. The required total share holding to offset this is 720 (delta +720).
3. The dealer already holds 600 shares, so the additional shares needed are:
   $$720 - 600 = 120 \text{ shares (buy)}$$

The dealer BUYS 120 more shares, bringing the total to 720.

- B (sell 120) is the wrong direction — after a rally a short-gamma hedger must BUY more shares, not sell.
- C (buy 720) ignores the 600 shares already held, double-counting the hedge.
- D (sell 600) liquidates the hedge entirely — nonsensical.

📌 Exam tip: As delta rises after a rally, a short-call (short-gamma) hedger must BUY MORE shares — "buy high." This buy-high/sell-low rebalancing cost is exactly what the option premium compensates the writer for.

**Q3.** A book has a portfolio delta of −500 and a portfolio gamma of −9,000. A traded call option has delta 0.50 and gamma 2.0. To make the book both delta- AND gamma-neutral, the dealer should:

- A. Buy 4,500 calls, then sell 1,750 shares.
- B. Buy 4,500 calls, then buy 1,750 shares.
- C. Sell 4,500 calls, then buy 1,750 shares.
- D. Buy 1,000 shares, then buy 4,500 calls.

**Answer: A**

The ask: construct a delta-gamma-neutral hedge in the correct order (gamma first with options, then delta with shares).

1. STEP 1 — neutralize gamma with the traded option (shares have zero gamma, so gamma can only be fixed with options):
   $$w = -\frac{\Gamma_{port}}{\Gamma_{option}} = -\frac{-9{,}000}{2.0} = +4{,}500 \text{ calls (buy)}$$
   New gamma: $$-9{,}000 + 4{,}500 \times 2.0 = 0$$. ✓
2. STEP 2 — the added 4,500 calls change the delta. New delta:
   $$-500 + 4{,}500 \times 0.50 = -500 + 2{,}250 = +1{,}750$$
3. Neutralize this with shares (delta +1, gamma 0, so this does NOT disturb the gamma hedge): SELL 1,750 shares.

- B (buy 1,750 shares) has the right gamma step but wrong delta direction — a +1,750 delta needs SELLING shares.
- C (sell 4,500 calls) gets the gamma direction backward — selling calls makes gamma more negative (−18,000).
- D hedges delta with shares FIRST; then buying the calls re-breaks the delta, so the order is wrong.

📌 Exam tip: Hedge GAMMA first (with options), then DELTA (with shares). Shares have zero gamma, so the delta step never undoes the gamma step — but doing it in the reverse order does undo the delta hedge.

**Q4.** A delta-neutral portfolio has a gamma of +5,000. The underlying stock price suddenly jumps by $4 (in either direction). Using the delta-gamma approximation, the approximate change in portfolio value is closest to:

- A. +$40,000
- B. +$20,000
- C. −$40,000
- D. $0

**Answer: A**

The ask: estimate the P&L of a delta-neutral, positive-gamma book for a large move using the delta-gamma approximation.

1. Delta-gamma approximation:
   $$\Delta P \approx \delta \cdot \Delta S + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma (\Delta S)^2$$
2. The portfolio is delta-neutral, so δ = 0 and the first term vanishes.
3. Gamma term:
   $$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5{,}000 \times (4)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 5{,}000 \times 16 = 40{,}000$$
4. Result: +$40,000, and because $$(\Delta S)^2$$ is positive regardless of the sign of ΔS, the gain is the same for a +$4 or −$4 move — the payoff of long gamma.

- B (+$20,000) forgets the squaring or the factor of 2, e.g., $$\frac{1}{2} \times 5{,}000 \times 4 \times 2$$ type error.
- C (−$40,000) gets the sign wrong — long (positive) gamma GAINS on large moves.
- D ($0) wrongly assumes delta-neutral means no P&L, ignoring the gamma (second-order) term.

📌 Exam tip: For a delta-neutral book, $$\Delta P \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(\Delta S)^2$$. Positive gamma gains on any large move (the square is always positive); the cost of that convexity is negative theta (time decay).

**Q5.** Which pair of Greeks is identical for a European call and a European put that share the same strike, maturity, and underlying?

- A. Gamma and vega
- B. Delta and theta
- C. Delta and rho
- D. Theta and vega

**Answer: A**

The ask: identify which Greeks coincide for a call and put with matched strike/maturity (a put-call parity consequence).

1. Differentiate put-call parity $$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$ with respect to S: the call and put deltas differ by exactly 1 (the constant terms differentiate to the share's delta of 1), so DELTA differs.
2. Differentiate twice with respect to S: the linear terms ($$Ke^{-rT}$$ and $$S_0$$) vanish, so the call and put have IDENTICAL gamma.
3. Differentiate parity with respect to σ: the $$Ke^{-rT}$$ and $$S_0$$ terms have no σ-dependence, so call and put VEGA are identical.
4. Theta and rho differ (the $$Ke^{-rT}$$ term depends on t and r, breaking the symmetry).

- B and C include delta, which differs by 1 between call and put.
- D includes theta, which differs because of the $$Ke^{-rT}$$ time-dependence.

📌 Exam tip: Gamma and vega are identical for a matched call/put — differentiate put-call parity (twice for gamma, once in σ for vega) and the $$S_0$$ and $$Ke^{-rT}$$ terms drop out. Delta, theta, and rho all differ.

**Q6.** A delta-neutral portfolio is observed to have a large POSITIVE theta. Using the relationship $$\Theta + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi$$, its gamma is most likely:

- A. Large and negative.
- B. Large and positive.
- C. Near zero.
- D. Unrelated to theta.

**Answer: A**

The ask: infer the sign of gamma from theta for a delta-neutral portfolio using the BSM PDE relationship.

1. For a delta-neutral portfolio (δ = 0) the term $$rS\delta$$ drops out, leaving:
   $$\Theta + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi$$
2. Rearrange: $$\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi - \Theta$$. A LARGE POSITIVE Θ makes the right side small or negative, forcing Γ to be NEGATIVE.
3. Economically: large positive theta means the book EARNS time decay (a short-options profile), which always carries large NEGATIVE gamma (it loses on large moves).

- B contradicts the relationship — positive theta pairs with negative gamma, not positive.
- C ignores the strong inverse coupling the equation imposes.
- D is wrong — for a delta-neutral book theta and gamma are tightly linked (opposite signs).

📌 Exam tip: For a delta-neutral book, theta and gamma have OPPOSITE signs: you cannot own convexity (positive gamma) without paying time decay (negative theta), and vice versa. Theta is the "rent" on gamma.

**Q7.** A portfolio manager implements portfolio insurance via SYNTHETIC put replication on an equity index, rather than buying listed puts. Which statement best describes how the strategy is executed and its key limitation?

- A. Sell index exposure (e.g., futures) as the market falls and buy as it rises; it fails when markets gap and liquidity dries up, as in October 1987.
- B. Buy index exposure as the market falls and sell as it rises; it fails only when volatility is low.
- C. Hold a constant index position and rebalance only at expiry; it fails because of transaction costs.
- D. Short the index outright and never rebalance; it fails because the hedge has the wrong sign.

**Answer: A**

The ask: describe synthetic-put portfolio insurance and its principal failure mode.

1. A protective put has a delta that becomes more negative as the market falls (the put goes deeper ITM). To replicate it synthetically, the manager reduces equity exposure as prices FALL (sell index/futures) and increases it as prices RISE (buy) — matching the put-adjusted delta dynamically.
2. The strategy relies on continuous trading at fair prices and stable volatility.
3. Key limitation: in a crash the market GAPS and liquidity evaporates exactly when the strategy must sell — programmatic insurance selling amplified the October 1987 crash.

- B reverses the trading rule (it describes buying into declines, the opposite of replicating a put).
- C describes a static, non-dynamic strategy — synthetic insurance requires continuous rebalancing.
- D describes an outright short, not a dynamic replication of a protective put.

📌 Exam tip: Synthetic protective put = sell as the market falls, buy as it rises (track the put's negative, rising-in-magnitude delta). Its fatal flaw is the assumption of continuous, liquid trading — precisely what fails in a gapping crash (1987).
