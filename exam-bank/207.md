**Q1.** An analyst estimates the CAPM beta of a stock by OLS on excess returns. From the data: $$\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(R_s, R_m) = 0.0042$$, $$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(R_m) = 0.0028$$, $$\bar{R}_s = 1.2\%$$, $$\bar{R}_m = 0.7\%$$. What are the slope and intercept of the fitted line $$\hat{R}_s = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta R_m$$?

- A. $$\hat\beta = 1.50$$, $$\hat\alpha = 0.15\%$$
- B. $$\hat\beta = 0.67$$, $$\hat\alpha = 0.73\%$$
- C. $$\hat\beta = 1.50$$, $$\hat\alpha = 1.20\%$$
- D. $$\hat\beta = 1.71$$, $$\hat\alpha = 0.00\%$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the OLS slope and intercept from the moments.

1. Slope is covariance over variance of the regressor:
$$\hat\beta = \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(R_s, R_m)}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(R_m)} = \frac{0.0042}{0.0028} = 1.50$$
2. Intercept forces the line through $$(\bar{R}_m, \bar{R}_s)$$:
$$\hat\alpha = \bar{R}_s - \hat\beta \bar{R}_m = 1.2\% - 1.50 \times 0.7\% = 1.2\% - 1.05\% = 0.15\%$$

Why A is correct: $$\hat\beta = 0.0042/0.0028 = 1.5$$ and $$\hat\alpha = 1.2 - 1.5(0.7) = 0.15\%$$.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** inverts the slope formula ($$0.0028/0.0042 = 0.67$$) — variance over covariance.
- **C** computes $$\hat\beta$$ correctly but sets $$\hat\alpha = \bar{R}_s$$, forgetting to subtract $$\hat\beta\bar{R}_m$$.
- **D** uses $$\bar{R}_s/\bar{R}_m = 1.2/0.7 = 1.71$$ as the slope and forces a zero intercept — confusing the ratio of means with the regression slope.

📌 Exam tip: $$\hat\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)$$ (the *regressor's* variance in the denominator), then $$\hat\alpha = \bar Y - \hat\beta \bar X$$. The fitted line always passes through the point of means.

**Q2.** An OLS regression of $$Y$$ on a single regressor $$X$$ over $$n = 102$$ observations gives $$\hat\beta = 0.72$$ with $$SE(\hat\beta) = 0.20$$. A hedge program requires the slope to equal 1 (a one-for-one hedge). Testing $$H_0: \beta = 1$$ against a two-sided alternative at 5% (critical t ≈ 1.984), what is the result?

- A. t = −1.40; fail to reject — the slope is not significantly different from 1
- B. t = 3.60; reject — the slope is significantly different from 1
- C. t = −1.40; reject — the slope is significantly different from 1
- D. t = 0.72; fail to reject — the slope is not significantly different from 1

**Answer: A**

We are asked to test whether the slope equals 1 (not 0).

1. The t-statistic uses the hypothesized value $$\beta_0 = 1$$, not 0:
$$t = \frac{\hat\beta - \beta_0}{SE(\hat\beta)} = \frac{0.72 - 1}{0.20} = \frac{-0.28}{0.20} = -1.40$$
2. Compare $$|{-1.40}| = 1.40$$ to the critical value 1.984. Since $$1.40 < 1.984$$, **fail to reject** — the slope is statistically indistinguishable from 1.

Why A is correct: subtracting the hypothesized 1 gives t = −1.40, inside the non-rejection band.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** uses $$t = (0.72 - 0)/0.20 = 3.60$$, testing against 0 instead of 1 — answering a different hypothesis.
- **C** computes the right statistic but flips the decision; $$|{-1.40}| < 1.984$$ means fail to reject.
- **D** divides the coefficient by itself-ish (uses $$0.72$$ as t) — ignores the SE entirely.

📌 Exam tip: When the null is $$\beta = \beta_0 \ne 0$$, subtract that specific value in the numerator. Testing a hedge ratio of 1 is the classic case where the null is not zero.

**Q3.** In a single-variable OLS regression, the total sum of squares is $$TSS = 500$$ and the residual sum of squares is $$RSS = 180$$, estimated on $$n = 42$$ observations. What are the $$R^2$$ and the standard error of the regression ($$s_\epsilon$$)?

- A. $$R^2 = 0.64$$, $$s_\epsilon = 2.121$$
- B. $$R^2 = 0.36$$, $$s_\epsilon = 2.121$$
- C. $$R^2 = 0.64$$, $$s_\epsilon = 4.500$$
- D. $$R^2 = 0.36$$, $$s_\epsilon = 4.286$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for $$R^2$$ and the standard error of the regression.

1. Coefficient of determination:
$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{RSS}{TSS} = 1 - \frac{180}{500} = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64$$
2. Standard error of the regression uses $$n - 2$$ (two parameters estimated):
$$s_\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{RSS}{n - 2}} = \sqrt{\frac{180}{40}} = \sqrt{4.5} = 2.121$$

Why A is correct: $$R^2 = 0.64$$ and $$s_\epsilon = \sqrt{180/40} = 2.121$$.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** reports the RSS/TSS ratio (0.36) as $$R^2$$ — that is $$1 - R^2$$, not $$R^2$$.
- **C** computes $$R^2$$ correctly but uses $$s_\epsilon = RSS/n - 2$$ logic or forgets the square root / divides by 2 ($$180/40 = 4.5$$ left un-rooted).
- **D** combines both errors: $$1-R^2$$ for the fit and divides by the wrong degrees of freedom ($$180/42 = 4.286$$, un-rooted, wrong df).

📌 Exam tip: $$R^2 = 1 - RSS/TSS$$ (not $$RSS/TSS$$). The standard error of the regression divides RSS by $$n - 2$$ in single regression because two parameters (slope and intercept) are estimated — then take the square root.

**Q4.** A regression yields $$\hat\beta = 1.10$$ with $$SE(\hat\beta) = 0.25$$ on a large sample. An analyst constructs the 95% confidence interval and then makes three claims. Which interval and conclusion are jointly correct?

- A. CI = $$(0.61, 1.59)$$; reject $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ but fail to reject $$H_0: \beta = 1$$
- B. CI = $$(0.61, 1.59)$$; fail to reject both $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ and $$H_0: \beta = 1$$
- C. CI = $$(0.85, 1.35)$$; reject $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ and reject $$H_0: \beta = 1$$
- D. CI = $$(0.61, 1.59)$$; reject both $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ and $$H_0: \beta = 1$$

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the 95% CI and which nulls it rejects via duality.

1. With a large sample use $$t \approx 1.96$$:
$$\hat\beta \pm 1.96 \times SE = 1.10 \pm 1.96 \times 0.25 = 1.10 \pm 0.49 = (0.61,\; 1.59)$$
2. By duality, reject any null value lying **outside** the interval:
   - $$\beta_0 = 0$$: outside $$(0.61, 1.59)$$ ⇒ **reject** (X is significant).
   - $$\beta_0 = 1$$: inside $$(0.61, 1.59)$$ ⇒ **fail to reject**.

Why A is correct: 0 is outside (reject) while 1 is inside (fail to reject).

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** has the right interval but wrongly fails to reject $$\beta = 0$$ even though 0 is outside.
- **C** uses a margin of $$\pm 0.25$$ (the bare SE, no 1.96 multiplier), producing $$(0.85, 1.35)$$ and then over-rejects.
- **D** has the right interval but wrongly rejects $$\beta = 1$$, which is inside.

📌 Exam tip: A 95% CI is the set of all nulls a 5% two-sided test would *not* reject. Check each candidate: outside ⇒ reject, inside ⇒ fail to reject. The margin must include the $$\approx 1.96$$ (or $$t$$) multiplier — the bare SE is not the half-width.

**Q5.** A researcher fits the model $$Y = \alpha + \beta X^2 + \epsilon$$ and also considers three alternative specifications. Which one of the following CANNOT be estimated directly by ordinary least squares?

- A. $$\ln Y = \alpha + \beta \ln X + \epsilon$$
- B. $$Y = \alpha + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 X^2 + \epsilon$$
- C. $$Y = \alpha + X^\beta + \epsilon$$
- D. $$Y = \alpha + \beta \, e^{X} + \epsilon$$

**Answer: C**

We are asked which model is not linear in the parameters and therefore not OLS-estimable.

1. OLS requires the model to be **linear in the parameters** ($$\alpha, \beta$$), even if nonlinear in the variables. Define a transformed regressor if needed.
2. Check each:
   - **A**: $$\ln Y = \alpha + \beta \ln X + \epsilon$$ — linear in $$\alpha, \beta$$ after taking logs of $$Y$$ and $$X$$. Estimable.
   - **B**: includes $$X$$ and $$X^2$$ as two regressors; linear in $$\beta_1, \beta_2$$. Estimable.
   - **C**: $$X^\beta$$ has the parameter $$\beta$$ in the **exponent** — nonlinear in the parameter, cannot be turned into a linear-in-parameters form by transforming the data. **Not OLS-estimable.**
   - **D**: $$e^X$$ is just a known transformation of $$X$$; the model is linear in $$\beta$$. Estimable (regress $$Y$$ on $$e^X$$).

Why C is correct: the parameter sits in the exponent of $$X$$, breaking linearity in the parameters.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **A, B, D** are all linear in the parameters once you treat $$\ln X$$, $$X^2$$, or $$e^X$$ as the regressor — "nonlinear in variables" is fine.

📌 Exam tip: "Linear" in OLS means linear in the *coefficients*. You may square, log, or exponentiate the *variables* freely. A parameter in an exponent ($$X^\beta$$) is the giveaway that OLS cannot be used directly.

**Q6.** An analyst is told that the OLS errors in a return regression are heteroskedastic (variance depends on the size of the regressor) but that all other classical assumptions hold. What is the consequence for the OLS slope estimate and for inference?

- A. The slope estimate remains unbiased and consistent, but the usual standard errors are invalid, so t-tests can mislead
- B. The slope estimate is biased and inconsistent, and the standard errors are also invalid
- C. The slope estimate is unbiased but inconsistent, and the standard errors are correct
- D. Both the slope estimate and its standard errors are unaffected in large samples

**Answer: A**

We are asked to separate the effect of heteroskedasticity on the coefficient versus on inference.

1. Heteroskedasticity violates the constant-variance assumption (assumption 4). It does **not** violate exogeneity ($$E[\epsilon\mid X]=0$$), so OLS coefficients remain **unbiased and consistent**.
2. However, the textbook standard-error formulas assume homoskedasticity; under heteroskedasticity they are **wrong** (typically understated), making t- and F-tests unreliable. OLS is also no longer efficient (not BLUE).
3. The fix: keep the OLS coefficients, but use heteroskedasticity-robust (White) standard errors.

Why A is correct: coefficients survive; inference breaks. Use robust SEs.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** claims bias — that would require violating exogeneity (the *omitted variable* problem), not heteroskedasticity.
- **C** wrongly calls the estimator inconsistent and the SEs correct — exactly backwards on both counts.
- **D** says nothing breaks, ignoring the invalid standard errors.

📌 Exam tip: Heteroskedasticity (and multicollinearity) damages **precision/standard errors**, not the coefficient. Only an endogeneity problem like omitted variable bias actually biases $$\hat\beta$$. Memorize which assumption breaks what.

**Q7.** A single-variable OLS regression reports $$R^2 = 0.49$$, and the estimated slope $$\hat\beta$$ is negative. What is the correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$?

- A. $$-0.70$$
- B. $$+0.70$$
- C. $$-0.49$$
- D. $$\pm 0.70$$ — sign cannot be determined

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the correlation, given $$R^2$$ and the sign of the slope.

1. In single-variable regression, $$R^2 = \rho_{XY}^2$$, so:
$$|\rho_{XY}| = \sqrt{R^2} = \sqrt{0.49} = 0.70$$
2. The sign of the correlation matches the sign of the slope (both share the sign of the covariance). The slope is negative, so:
$$\rho_{XY} = -0.70$$

Why A is correct: magnitude $$\sqrt{0.49} = 0.70$$, sign negative to match the slope.

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** has the right magnitude but the wrong sign — ignores that $$\hat\beta < 0$$.
- **C** reports $$R^2$$ itself (0.49) as the correlation — forgets to take the square root.
- **D** would be correct only if the slope's sign were unknown; here we are told it is negative, so the sign is pinned down.

📌 Exam tip: $$\rho = \pm\sqrt{R^2}$$ in single regression. Take the square root for the magnitude, and let the slope's (or covariance's) sign fix the sign.

**Q8.** Which of the following is the correct interpretation of the slope $$\hat\beta$$ and intercept $$\hat\alpha$$ in a fitted single-variable OLS regression $$\hat Y = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta X$$, together with a true in-sample property of the residuals?

- A. $$\hat\beta$$ is the expected change in $$Y$$ per one-unit change in $$X$$; $$\hat\alpha$$ is the expected $$Y$$ when $$X = 0$$; in-sample the residuals sum to zero
- B. $$\hat\beta$$ is the expected change in $$X$$ per one-unit change in $$Y$$; $$\hat\alpha$$ is the mean of $$Y$$; the residuals sum to one
- C. $$\hat\beta$$ is the correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$; $$\hat\alpha$$ is the expected $$Y$$ when $$X = 0$$; the residuals are correlated with $$X$$
- D. $$\hat\beta$$ is the expected change in $$Y$$ per one-unit change in $$X$$; $$\hat\alpha$$ is the value of $$Y$$ at the maximum of $$X$$; the residuals have nonzero mean

**Answer: A**

We are asked for the correct coefficient interpretations plus a valid residual property.

1. **Slope**: $$\hat\beta$$ measures the expected change in the dependent variable $$Y$$ for a one-unit increase in the explanatory variable $$X$$.
2. **Intercept**: $$\hat\alpha$$ is the fitted value of $$Y$$ when $$X = 0$$ (the line passes through $$(\bar X, \bar Y)$$, and extends to $$X=0$$ at height $$\hat\alpha$$).
3. **Residual property**: OLS residuals **sum to zero** in-sample and are **uncorrelated with $$X$$** — both are direct consequences of the first-order conditions.

Why A is correct: it states the standard slope and intercept meanings and a genuine residual property (sum to zero).

Why the distractors are traps:
- **B** reverses the regression (change in $$X$$ per unit $$Y$$), calls $$\hat\alpha$$ the mean of $$Y$$, and claims residuals sum to one — all false.
- **C** confuses the slope with the correlation (it equals $$\rho \, s_Y/s_X$$, not $$\rho$$) and wrongly says residuals are correlated with $$X$$ (they are uncorrelated by construction).
- **D** mangles the intercept ("value at max of $$X$$") and claims nonzero-mean residuals.

📌 Exam tip: $$\hat\beta$$ = effect of $$X$$ on $$Y$$ (not the correlation, not the reverse regression). In-sample, OLS guarantees residuals sum to zero and are orthogonal to $$X$$ — handy sanity checks.
