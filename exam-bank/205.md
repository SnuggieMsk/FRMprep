**Q1.** A sample of six monthly returns (%) is: 3, −2, 5, 1, −4, 9. What is the unbiased sample variance?

- A. 18.67
- B. 22.40
- C. 134.00
- D. 26.80

**Answer: B**

Unbiased sample variance uses the $$n-1$$ divisor: $$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i-\bar X)^2$$.

1. Mean: $$\bar X = \frac{3 - 2 + 5 + 1 - 4 + 9}{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2.0$$.
2. Deviations from mean: $$1,\ -4,\ 3,\ -1,\ -6,\ 7$$.
3. Squared deviations: $$1,\ 16,\ 9,\ 1,\ 36,\ 49$$; sum $$= 112$$.
4. Divide by $$n-1 = 5$$: $$s^2 = 112/5 = 22.40$$.

B is correct. A (18.67) uses the biased $$n$$ divisor: $$112/6 = 18.67$$ — the question asks for the *unbiased* version. C (134.00) is the sum of squared raw values minus a slip, or forgets to subtract the mean. D (26.80) divides by $$n-2=4.18$$ or miscounts; it overstates by using too small a divisor inconsistently.

📌 Exam tip: "Unbiased" or "sample" variance ⇒ divide by $$n-1$$. The biased/population version uses $$n$$. With small $$n$$ the gap is large (here 18.67 vs 22.40), and GARP plants both as options — read the stem.

**Q2.** Losses on a portfolio have an unknown, right-skewed distribution with mean $9M and standard deviation $24M. An analyst draws 144 independent losses and computes the sample average. The approximate probability that the sample average exceeds $13M is closest to:

- A. 2.28%
- B. 43.3%
- C. 0.13%
- D. 15.9%

**Answer: A**

By the CLT, the sample mean of $$n=144$$ iid losses is approximately normal regardless of the skewed underlying.

1. Standard error: $$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{24}{\sqrt{144}} = \frac{24}{12} = 2$$ ($M).
2. Sampling distribution: $$\bar X \approx N(9, 2^2)$$.
3. Standardize: $$z = \frac{13 - 9}{2} = \frac{4}{2} = 2.0$$.
4. $$P(\bar X > 13) = P(Z > 2.0) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228 = 2.28\%$$.

A is correct. B (43.3%) uses the raw $$\sigma=24$$ as the standard deviation: $$z=(13-9)/24=0.167$$, $$P(Z>0.167)=43.3\%$$ — forgetting to divide by $$\sqrt{n}$$. C (0.13%) uses $$z=3$$ (e.g., $$SE=4/3$$ from a $$\sqrt{n}$$ slip). D (15.9%) uses $$z=1$$ (sets $$SE=4$$, dividing by $$\sqrt{36}$$ instead of $$\sqrt{144}$$).

📌 Exam tip: The CLT applies to the *sample mean*, whose standard error is $$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$$ — not the raw $$\sigma$$. Forgetting the $$\sqrt{n}$$ shrinkage is the most common CLT error and gives a far-too-fat distribution.

**Q3.** Which statement about estimator properties is correct?

- A. A consistent estimator must be unbiased in every finite sample
- B. The sample mean is BLUE — the minimum-variance estimator among all linear, unbiased estimators of the population mean
- C. The $$n$$-divisor variance estimator is biased and therefore inconsistent
- D. An efficient estimator is one whose bias shrinks fastest as $$n$$ grows

**Answer: B**

Recall the precise definitions.

1. BLUE = Best Linear Unbiased Estimator: among all estimators that are linear in the data and unbiased, the sample mean has the smallest variance (needs only iid with finite variance — no distributional assumption).
2. Consistency is asymptotic ($$\hat\theta \to \theta$$ as $$n\to\infty$$); a consistent estimator *can* be biased in finite samples.
3. The $$n$$-divisor variance is biased but *consistent* — its bias vanishes as $$n\to\infty$$.

B is correct. A confuses consistency with unbiasedness — a consistent estimator may be biased in finite samples (the $$n$$-divisor variance is the standard example). C wrongly concludes "inconsistent" from "biased"; the $$n$$-divisor estimator is biased yet consistent. D misdefines efficiency — efficiency is about *minimum variance* among unbiased estimators, not about how fast bias shrinks.

📌 Exam tip: Keep the four straight — unbiased (right on average, any $$n$$), efficient (smallest variance among unbiased), consistent (converges as $$n\to\infty$$, may be biased for finite $$n$$), BLUE (best linear unbiased = the sample mean for $$\mu$$).

**Q4.** Three paired observations of asset returns (%) are $$X$$: 2, 4, 9 and $$Y$$: 1, 5, 6. What is the sample correlation $$\hat\rho$$?

- A. 0.500
- B. 0.839
- C. 0.945
- D. 1.000

**Answer: B**

Compute deviations, then $$\hat\rho = \frac{\hat\sigma_{XY}}{s_X s_Y}$$ (the $$n-1$$ factors cancel, so use sums of products and squares directly).

1. Means: $$\bar X = (2+4+9)/3 = 15/3 = 5$$; $$\bar Y = (1+5+6)/3 = 12/3 = 4$$.
2. $$X$$-deviations: $$-3,\ -1,\ 4$$; $$Y$$-deviations: $$-3,\ 1,\ 2$$.
3. Cross products: $$(-3)(-3)=9$$; $$(-1)(1)=-1$$; $$(4)(2)=8$$; sum $$=16$$.
4. $$X$$ squared deviations: $$9,\ 1,\ 16$$; sum $$=26$$. $$Y$$ squared deviations: $$9,\ 1,\ 4$$; sum $$=14$$.
5. Correlation: $$\hat\rho = \frac{\sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum(x-\bar x)^2 \sum(y-\bar y)^2}} = \frac{16}{\sqrt{26 \times 14}} = \frac{16}{\sqrt{364}} = \frac{16}{19.079} = 0.839$$.

B is correct (0.839). A (0.500) drops a cross-product term, shrinking the numerator. C (0.945) divides by $$\sqrt{\sum x^2}$$ for one variable but mishandles the other's sum, inflating $$\hat\rho$$. D (1.000) assumes perfect correlation, ignoring the imperfect co-movement (the middle pair moves oppositely, contributing $$-1$$).

📌 Exam tip: For sample correlation, the $$n-1$$ divisors in covariance and both standard deviations cancel — just use raw sums of cross products and squared deviations. Always include every paired term, including ones with a negative product.

**Q5.** Quadrupling a sample from $$n=25$$ to $$n=100$$, with the population standard deviation unchanged, changes the standard error of the sample mean by what factor, and the width of a fixed-confidence interval for the mean by what factor?

- A. SE × 1/4; interval width × 1/4
- B. SE × 1/2; interval width × 1/2
- C. SE × 1/2; interval width × 1/4
- D. SE × 1/4; interval width × 1/2

**Answer: B**

The standard error is $$SE = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$$, and a confidence interval half-width is a fixed multiple (e.g., 1.96) times the SE — so both scale identically with the SE.

1. SE ratio: $$\frac{SE_{100}}{SE_{25}} = \frac{\sigma/\sqrt{100}}{\sigma/\sqrt{25}} = \frac{\sqrt{25}}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2}$$.
2. Quadrupling $$n$$ multiplies $$\sqrt{n}$$ by 2, so SE is halved.
3. The interval width $$= 2 \times z \times SE$$, so it scales with the SE — also halved.

B is correct. A treats SE as $$\propto 1/n$$ (it is $$\propto 1/\sqrt{n}$$), giving 1/4 incorrectly. C halves the SE correctly but then quarters the interval, breaking the proportionality. D mismatches both factors.

📌 Exam tip: $$SE \propto 1/\sqrt{n}$$, so to *halve* the SE you must *quadruple* $$n$$. Confidence-interval width is a constant multiple of the SE, so it scales by the same factor — both halve here.

**Q6.** An analyst computes the sample variance of five returns two ways: with the $$n$$ divisor she gets 8.0; her colleague uses the $$n-1$$ divisor on the same data. What value does the colleague obtain, and which estimator is unbiased?

- A. 6.4; the $$n$$-divisor estimator is unbiased
- B. 10.0; the $$n-1$$ divisor estimator is unbiased
- C. 10.0; the $$n$$-divisor estimator is unbiased
- D. 6.67; the $$n-1$$ divisor estimator is unbiased

**Answer: B**

The two estimators differ only by the divisor; convert between them via the common sum of squared deviations $$SS$$.

1. From the $$n$$ divisor: $$8.0 = SS/n = SS/5 \Rightarrow SS = 40$$.
2. The $$n-1$$ estimator: $$s^2 = SS/(n-1) = 40/4 = 10.0$$.
3. The $$n-1$$ divisor produces the *unbiased* estimator (it corrects for the one degree of freedom spent estimating the mean).

B is correct. A inverts the relationship (the $$n-1$$ value is larger, not smaller, than the $$n$$ value, and the $$n$$ version is biased). C gets the value 10.0 right but labels the wrong estimator unbiased. D applies $$SS/6$$ (dividing by $$n+1$$) or mis-derives $$SS$$, giving 6.67.

📌 Exam tip: $$s^2_{n-1} = s^2_n \times \frac{n}{n-1}$$, so the unbiased ($$n-1$$) estimate is always *larger* than the biased ($$n$$) one. Here $$8.0 \times \frac{5}{4} = 10.0$$. The $$n-1$$ version is the unbiased one.

**Q7.** A sample of seven daily returns (sorted, %) is: −5, −2, 0, 1, 3, 4, 12. Using the median and the mean, which statement best describes the distribution and the robustness of the two measures?

- A. Mean 1.86, median 1; the mean exceeds the median, suggesting positive skew, and the median is more robust to the +12 outlier
- B. Mean 1, median 1.86; the median exceeds the mean, suggesting negative skew
- C. Mean 1.86, median 0; the two are equal so the data are symmetric
- D. Mean 1.86, median 1; the mean is more robust to the +12 outlier than the median

**Answer: A**

Compute the mean and the median (middle value of 7 sorted observations is the 4th).

1. Mean: $$\bar X = \frac{-5 - 2 + 0 + 1 + 3 + 4 + 12}{7} = \frac{13}{7} = 1.857 \approx 1.86$$.
2. Median: with $$n=7$$ (odd), the median is the 4th sorted value $$= 1$$.
3. Mean (1.86) > median (1): the long right tail driven by the $$+12$$ observation pulls the mean above the median — a sign of positive skew.
4. The median ignores the magnitude of the extreme $$+12$$ (only its rank), so it is robust to that outlier; the mean is dragged toward it.

A is correct. B swaps the two statistics and the skew direction. C wrongly reads the median as 0 (that is the 3rd value, not the 4th) and claims symmetry despite mean ≠ median. D correctly computes both but reverses robustness — the mean is *sensitive* to outliers, the median is robust.

📌 Exam tip: For odd $$n$$, the median is the middle (here 4th) sorted value. Mean > median signals positive (right) skew. The median is robust to outliers; the mean is not — the $$+12$$ pulls the mean up but leaves the median unchanged.
