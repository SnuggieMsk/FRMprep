**Q1.** A risk analyst observes that a commodity spot price follows a driftless random walk with daily shock standard deviation $\sigma = \$3$. Today's price is $\$200$. The analyst wants the width (upper bound minus lower bound) of the 95% forecast interval for the price 64 trading days from now. The width is closest to:

- A. $\$5.88$
- B. $\$11.76$
- C. $\$94.08$
- D. $\$47.04$

**Answer: C**

We are asked for the full width of the 95% forecast interval for a driftless random walk 64 days ahead.

1. Random-walk variance grows linearly with the horizon: $$\mathrm{Var}(Y_{t+h}) = h\,\sigma^2 = 64 \times 3^2 = 64 \times 9 = 576.$$
2. Horizon standard deviation: $$\sigma_{64} = \sqrt{576} = 24.$$
3. The 95% interval is $$\hat Y_{t+h} \pm 1.96\,\sigma_{64} = 200 \pm 1.96 \times 24 = 200 \pm 47.04.$$
4. The **width** is the distance between the two bounds: $$2 \times 47.04 = 94.08.$$

C is correct. Distractor D ($47.04$) is the half-width ($1.96\sigma_{64}$) — the trap of confusing the half-width with the full width. Distractor B ($11.76$) uses $\sigma$ alone scaled by $\sqrt{... }$ incorrectly: it is $2 \times 1.96 \times 3 = 11.76$, forgetting to scale variance by the horizon (treating one-day risk as 64-day risk). Distractor A ($5.88$) is the one-day half-width $\times 2 / 2$ confusion — it ignores the $\sqrt{64}=8$ time-scaling entirely.

📌 Exam tip: For a random walk, variance scales with $h$ (not $\sqrt{h}$), so volatility scales with $\sqrt{h}$. Read carefully whether the question wants the half-width ($\pm$ term) or the full interval width.

**Q2.** An analyst runs an augmented Dickey–Fuller regression on the *level* of a 10-year swap rate series ($T = 300$): $$\Delta Y_t = \delta + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t,$$ obtaining $\hat\gamma = -0.034$ with $SE(\hat\gamma) = 0.013$. The 5% Dickey–Fuller critical value (with constant) is approximately $-2.86$, and the 5% one-sided standard-normal critical value is $-1.645$. Which conclusion is correct?

- A. Reject the unit root using the DF critical value; the series is stationary.
- B. Fail to reject the unit root using the DF critical value; model the series in differences.
- C. The test is two-sided, so compare against $\pm 2.86$ and fail to reject.
- D. The DF distribution does not apply because $T$ exceeds 250; use $-1.645$ and reject.

**Answer: B**

We must compute the DF statistic and compare it against the correct (Dickey–Fuller) critical value.

1. Test statistic: $$DF = \frac{\hat\gamma}{SE(\hat\gamma)} = \frac{-0.034}{0.013} = -2.615.$$
2. The DF test is **one-sided, lower-tail**: $H_0: \gamma = 0$ (unit root) vs. $H_1: \gamma < 0$ (stationary). We reject only if $DF$ is *more negative* than the critical value.
3. Compare: $-2.615$ vs. $-2.86$. Since $-2.615 > -2.86$ (not negative enough), we **fail to reject** the unit root.
4. Treat the series as having a unit root: first-difference it before modeling.

B is correct. A is the classic trap of using the standard-normal critical value ($-1.645$): against $-1.645$ you would wrongly reject because $-2.615 < -1.645$. The DF statistic does NOT follow a t/normal distribution under $H_0$, so its critical values are more negative — exactly why DF tables exist. C is wrong: the DF test is one-sided, not two-sided. D invents a sample-size rule; the DF distribution applies regardless of $T$.

📌 Exam tip: The whole point of Dickey–Fuller is that the usual t-distribution is invalid under a unit root. Always use the (more negative) DF critical values, and remember it is a one-sided lower-tail test.

**Q3.** A trend-stationary process is given by $Y_t = 50 + 0.4t + \epsilon_t$, where $\epsilon_t$ is white noise with variance 9. A second process is a random walk with drift, $Z_t = 0.4 + Z_{t-1} + u_t$, with $u_t$ white noise variance 9 and $Z_0 = 50$. Which statement about these two series is correct?

- A. Both series have the same unconditional variance that grows linearly with time.
- B. Both have expected value $50 + 0.4t$, but only the random walk has variance that grows without bound.
- C. The trend-stationary series should be first-differenced and the random walk should be detrended.
- D. A shock $\epsilon_t$ permanently shifts the trend-stationary series, while a shock $u_t$ dies out for the random walk.

**Answer: B**

The question contrasts a trend-stationary series with a random walk with drift.

1. **Expected values.** Trend-stationary: $$E[Y_t] = 50 + 0.4t$$ (the deterministic trend). Random walk with drift: $$E[Z_t] = Z_0 + \delta t = 50 + 0.4t.$$ Both have the *same* mean path.
2. **Variances.** Trend-stationary: $$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = \mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_t) = 9$$ — constant, because shocks do not accumulate. Random walk: $$\mathrm{Var}(Z_t) = t\sigma^2 = 9t$$ — grows without bound as shocks accumulate.
3. So they share a mean path but differ fundamentally in variance behavior. This is the trend-stationary vs. difference-stationary distinction.

B is correct. A is wrong: only the random walk has time-growing variance; the trend-stationary variance is constant at 9. C reverses the correct treatments — detrend the trend-stationary series, difference the random walk. D reverses shock persistence: shocks are *transitory* (die out) for trend-stationary series and *permanent* (accumulate) for the random walk.

📌 Exam tip: Same-looking mean paths can hide totally different risk. Trend-stationary = constant variance, mean-reverting to trend (detrend it). Unit root = exploding variance, permanent shocks (difference it).

**Q4.** A researcher regresses the price level of Stock A on the price level of Stock B over 5 years of daily data. Both are independent driftless random walks with no economic link. The output shows $R^2 = 0.88$, a slope t-statistic of 14.2, and a Durbin–Watson statistic of 0.08. The most accurate interpretation is:

- A. Stock B is a strong predictor of Stock A; the high $R^2$ and t-stat confirm a robust relationship.
- B. This is a spurious regression; the high $R^2$ with very low Durbin–Watson is the telltale signature, and the relationship is illusory.
- C. The low Durbin–Watson means the model is heteroskedastic; correcting it will validate the relationship.
- D. The regression is valid because the t-statistic far exceeds the Dickey–Fuller critical value.

**Answer: B**

The question tests recognition of the spurious-regression signature.

1. Regressing one independent unit-root series (random walk) on another routinely produces a **large $R^2$** and a **large, "significant" t-statistic** even though the true relationship is zero.
2. The diagnostic giveaway is **strongly autocorrelated residuals**, shown here by a Durbin–Watson statistic near zero (0.08, far below the benchmark of 2 that indicates no autocorrelation).
3. The standard t-statistic is invalid here because the regression assumptions (stationary, non-autocorrelated errors) are violated — the apparent significance is an artifact.
4. Correct practice: work with *returns/differences*, or test for cointegration, before believing any relationship between price levels.

B is correct. A falls for exactly the trap GARP loves — trusting $R^2$ and t-stats on trending levels. C misdiagnoses: low DW signals serial correlation, not heteroskedasticity, and "correcting" it will not create a real relationship that does not exist. D confuses the spurious-regression t-stat with the Dickey–Fuller test (a different test, for unit roots, not for slope significance).

📌 Exam tip: High $R^2$ + huge t-stat + Durbin–Watson near zero on trending *levels* = spurious regression. The cure is differencing or establishing cointegration, never trusting the levels regression.

**Q5.** Quarterly revenue for a retailer shows a strong, stable seasonal pattern (Q4 is always high). An analyst wants to model the series using a seasonal-dummy regression with an intercept. The correct specification for quarterly data is:

- A. Include four quarterly dummies plus an intercept.
- B. Include three quarterly dummies plus an intercept.
- C. Include four quarterly dummies and no intercept.
- D. Either three dummies plus an intercept, or four dummies and no intercept — but never four dummies plus an intercept.

**Answer: D**

The question tests the dummy-variable trap for seasonal modeling.

1. With quarterly data there are 4 seasons. To avoid perfect multicollinearity (the **dummy trap**), you cannot include a full set of season dummies *and* an intercept, because the dummies sum to a column of ones identical to the intercept.
2. Two valid options exist:
   - **Three dummies + an intercept** — the omitted quarter becomes the baseline captured by the intercept; the three coefficients are differences from that baseline.
   - **Four dummies + no intercept** — each coefficient is that quarter's mean directly.
3. What is *never* allowed: four dummies *plus* an intercept (perfect collinearity, the regression cannot be estimated).

D is correct because it states both valid forms and the prohibition. A is the dummy trap itself (four dummies + intercept). B and C are each *individually* valid but incomplete — the question's specification asked "with an intercept," yet the best/most-complete answer recognizes both equivalent forms and the actual rule being tested. D is the precise statement of the rule.

📌 Exam tip: For $s$ seasons, use either $s$ dummies and no intercept, or $s-1$ dummies plus an intercept. Including all $s$ dummies *and* an intercept triggers the dummy trap (perfect multicollinearity).

**Q6.** A log-linear trend model is estimated as $\ln Y_t = 2.0 + 0.015t + \epsilon_t$ (t in months). An analyst draws three conclusions. Which is correct?

- A. The series grows by a constant $0.015 in dollar terms each month.
- B. The series grows by approximately 1.5% per month, i.e., constant percentage growth.
- C. Because the model is in logs, the series is automatically stationary.
- D. The fitted value of $Y$ at $t = 0$ is $\$2.00$.

**Answer: B**

The question tests interpretation of a log-linear (constant-growth) trend.

1. A log-linear trend $$\ln Y_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1 t + \epsilon_t$$ models **constant percentage growth** of $\delta_1$ per period. Here $\delta_1 = 0.015$, so growth is approximately **1.5% per month**.
2. The coefficient on $t$ in a log model is a *growth rate* (proportional change), not a dollar amount — that is the defining feature of log-linear trends.
3. At $t = 0$: $\ln Y_0 = 2.0$, so $Y_0 = e^{2.0} = 7.389$, not $\$2.00$.

B is correct. A misreads the log-coefficient as a dollar increment (the trap of treating a log-linear model like a linear one). C is wrong: a deterministic trend in logs is still non-stationary (the mean grows with $t$); taking logs does not remove a trend — detrending or differencing does. D forgets to exponentiate: the intercept is in log units, so $Y_0 = e^{2.0} \approx 7.39$, not 2.00.

📌 Exam tip: In a log-linear trend, the slope is a *growth rate* (≈ percentage per period) and the intercept is in log units (exponentiate to get the level). Logs change the interpretation of coefficients but do not by themselves make a trending series stationary.

**Q7.** An analyst fails to reject the unit-root null in an ADF test on a series she strongly believes is stationary but highly persistent (true $\phi \approx 0.98$). Which statement best explains and qualifies this result?

- A. Failing to reject proves the series has a unit root, so it must be differenced.
- B. The ADF test has low power against near-unit-root alternatives, so failing to reject does not prove a unit root exists.
- C. The result means the standard t-distribution should now be used for the test statistic.
- D. A near-unit-root series ($\phi = 0.98$) is mathematically identical to a random walk.

**Answer: B**

The question tests the power limitations of the Dickey–Fuller / ADF test.

1. "Failing to reject" $H_0$ is **not** the same as proving $H_0$ true. The DF/ADF test has **low power** against alternatives where the true root is close to (but below) one, such as $\phi = 0.98$.
2. With a near-unit-root but genuinely stationary series, the test frequently fails to reject the false null — a Type II error driven by low power.
3. So the analyst's prior (stationary but very persistent) is consistent with this outcome; she should not conclude a unit root is definitely present.

B is correct. A overstates the conclusion — failing to reject never *proves* the null; that is a fundamental hypothesis-testing principle. C is wrong: the statistic still follows the nonstandard DF distribution, not the t-distribution, regardless of the decision. D is false: $\phi = 0.98 < 1$ means the series is technically stationary (mean-reverting, finite variance), unlike a true unit root ($\phi = 1$) whose variance grows without bound — they are not identical.

📌 Exam tip: "Fail to reject" ≠ "accept the null." The ADF test is notoriously weak at distinguishing a near-unit root ($\phi = 0.98$) from a true unit root ($\phi = 1$). Combine the test with economic judgment and plots.
