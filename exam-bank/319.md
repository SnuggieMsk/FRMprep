**Q1.** A US Treasury bond pays a 6% coupon semiannually on April 15 and October 15. It is quoted at 101-08 for settlement on August 1. Using the correct day-count convention, the **cash (dirty) price** per $100 face is closest to:
- A. $101.25
- B. $103.02
- C. $102.73
- D. $99.48

**Answer: B**

Restate: convert the quote to decimal, compute accrued interest under Actual/Actual (Treasury convention), and add it to the clean price.

1. **Quote to decimal:** 101-08 means $$101\tfrac{8}{32} = 101.25.$$
2. **Day count (Treasury = Actual/Actual in period).** April 15 → August 1: 15 days left in April (16→30), May 31, June 30, July 31, plus 1 day to Aug 1 = $$15 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 1 = 108$$ days. Coupon period April 15 → October 15 = $$183$$ actual days.
3. **Accrued interest:** semiannual coupon $$= 6\%/2 = 3$$ per 100 face:
$$\text{AI} = 3 \times \frac{108}{183} = 3 \times 0.590164 = 1.7705.$$
4. **Dirty price:** $$101.25 + 1.7705 = \$103.02.$$

Why B is correct: dirty = clean quote + accrued interest = 101.25 + 1.77 = $103.02.

- A ($101.25) is the **clean** quote with **no** accrued interest added — the most common omission.
- C ($102.73) uses the **wrong direction** or a 30/360-style day count (122/180 → AI 1.48 plus an arithmetic slip).
- D ($99.48) *subtracts* accrued interest instead of adding it (101.25 − 1.77).

📌 Exam tip: You are *quoted clean* but *pay dirty*: dirty = clean + accrued. Treasuries use **Actual/Actual** — real days elapsed over real days in the coupon period.

**Q2.** The T-bond futures settlement price is 96.50. Three deliverable bonds and their conversion factors:

| Bond | Quoted price | CF |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 102.40 | 1.0450 |
| 2 | 137.20 | 1.4100 |
| 3 | 115.60 | 1.1850 |

The cheapest-to-deliver bond is:
- A. Bond 1
- B. Bond 2
- C. Bond 3
- D. Cannot be determined without accrued interest

**Answer: B**

Restate: minimize the delivery cost $$= \text{Quoted price} - (\text{Futures} \times \text{CF})$$ across the three bonds.

1. **Bond 1:** $$102.40 - 96.50 \times 1.0450 = 102.40 - 100.8425 = 1.5575.$$
2. **Bond 2:** $$137.20 - 96.50 \times 1.4100 = 137.20 - 136.065 = 1.1350.$$
3. **Bond 3:** $$115.60 - 96.50 \times 1.1850 = 115.60 - 114.3525 = 1.2475.$$
4. **Lowest cost:** Bond 2 at 1.1350 → cheapest to deliver.

Why B is correct: Bond 2 minimizes (quoted price − futures × CF), the delivery cost the short bears.

- A (Bond 1) has the **highest** cost (1.5575) — the trap of picking the lowest *quoted price* or highest CF without computing net cost.
- C (Bond 3) has an intermediate cost (1.2475).
- D is wrong: accrued interest is added to **both** the invoice and the purchase, so it cancels — CTD can be determined from quoted prices and CFs alone.

📌 Exam tip: CTD minimizes (quoted price − futures × CF). Accrued interest cancels from both legs, so "need accrued interest" is always a distractor.

**Q3.** A trader is **long** one three-month SOFR (Eurodollar-style) futures contract. The quote rises from 97.60 to 97.85. The trader's gain is closest to:
- A. $625 gain
- B. $625 loss
- C. $250 gain
- D. $2,500 gain

**Answer: A**

Restate: a long gains when the quote rises (rates fall); compute the move in basis points times $25.

1. **Basis-point value:** $$\$1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 \times \tfrac{90}{360} = \$25$$ per basis point.
2. **Quote change:** $$97.85 - 97.60 = 0.25 = 25$$ basis points.
3. **Long position:** a rising quote means falling rates — a **gain** for the long.
4. **Gain:** $$25 \text{ bp} \times \$25 = \$625.$$

Why A is correct: +25 bp on the quote × $25/bp = $625, and the long gains as the quote rises.

- B ($625 loss) gets the magnitude right but the **sign** wrong — a long gains, not loses, when the quote rises.
- C ($250 gain) uses $10/bp or miscounts the move (e.g., 25 bp × $10).
- D ($2,500 gain) treats the 0.25 quote move as 250 bp, off by a factor of 10.

📌 Exam tip: $25 per basis point per contract. Long gains when the **quote rises** (rates fall). Convert quote change to basis points first (0.25 quote = 25 bp).

**Q4.** A Eurodollar futures contract has a futures rate of 7.20% (continuously compounded). With $$\sigma = 1.1\%$$ per annum, $$t_1 = 6$$ years and $$t_2 = 6.25$$ years, the corresponding **forward rate** after the convexity adjustment is closest to:
- A. 7.20%
- B. 6.97%
- C. 7.43%
- D. 6.43%

**Answer: B**

Restate: subtract the convexity adjustment to convert the futures rate to a forward rate.

1. **Formula:** $$\text{forward} = \text{futures} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t_1 t_2.$$
2. **Adjustment:** $$\tfrac{1}{2}(0.011)^2 (6)(6.25) = 0.5 \times 0.000121 \times 37.5 = 0.5 \times 0.0045375 = 0.00226875.$$
3. In percent: $$0.00226875 = 0.2269\%.$$
4. **Forward rate:** $$7.20\% - 0.2269\% = 6.973\% \approx 6.97\%.$$

Why B is correct: the futures rate is **biased upward** by daily settlement, so the forward is the futures rate **minus** the adjustment.

- A (7.20%) ignores the adjustment entirely — assumes futures = forward.
- C (7.43%) **adds** the adjustment instead of subtracting (wrong sign).
- D (6.43%) inflates the adjustment (e.g., forgets the ½ or squares incorrectly).

📌 Exam tip: forward = futures − ½σ²t₁t₂. The futures rate is always the *higher* one (daily margining favors the rate position), so you **subtract** to get the forward. Watch the ½ and the **squared** volatility.

**Q5.** A 60-day US Treasury bill is quoted at a discount rate of 5.40%. Its cash price per $100 of face value, and its true (money-market) yield, are best described as:
- A. Cash price $99.10; the discount rate equals the true yield.
- B. Cash price $99.10; the discount rate understates the true yield.
- C. Cash price $94.60; the discount rate overstates the true yield.
- D. Cash price $99.10; the true yield is exactly 5.40% on a bond-equivalent basis.

**Answer: B**

Restate: price the T-bill from its discount quote and judge how the discount rate compares to the true yield.

1. **Cash price** from discount rate $$Q$$ over $$n$$ days:
$$P = 100 - \frac{n}{360}\,Q = 100 - \frac{60}{360} \times 5.40 = 100 - 0.16667 \times 5.40 = 100 - 0.90 = \$99.10.$$
2. **Why the discount rate understates the true yield:** the discount rate divides the $0.90 gain by **face (100)** and uses a 360-day year. The true money-market yield divides by **price (99.10)** and is therefore higher:
$$\text{true (approx)} = \frac{0.90}{99.10} \times \frac{360}{60} = 0.009082 \times 6 = 5.449\%.$$

Why B is correct: cash price is $99.10, and the discount rate (5.40%) is **below** the true yield (~5.45%) because it divides by face rather than price.

- A is wrong: the discount rate does **not** equal the true yield.
- C miscomputes the price ($94.60 would use the wrong day fraction) and reverses the bias.
- D falsely claims 5.40% is the bond-equivalent yield.

📌 Exam tip: T-bill discount rate divides the gain by **face** and uses 360 days, so it **understates** the true yield. Price = 100 − (n/360)·Q.

**Q6.** A manager holds a $20 million bond portfolio with duration 5.5 years and wants to hedge against rising rates over three months. T-bond futures trade at 124-16 (contract value $124,500.00) and the cheapest-to-deliver bond will have duration 8.0 years at futures expiry. The hedge requires the manager to:
- A. Short 76 contracts
- B. Short 110 contracts
- C. Short 138 contracts
- D. Long 110 contracts

**Answer: B**

Restate: compute the duration-based hedge ratio using the CTD duration, and determine direction.

1. **Contract value:** 124-16 $$= 124\tfrac{16}{32} = 124.50$$ → $$\$124{,}500.00.$$
2. **Hedge formula:**
$$N^* = -\frac{P\,D_P}{F_C\,D_F}.$$
3. **Plug in** ($$D_F$$ = CTD duration = 8.0):
$$N^* = -\frac{20{,}000{,}000 \times 5.5}{124{,}500 \times 8.0} = -\frac{110{,}000{,}000}{996{,}000} = -110.4.$$
4. **Round:** short **110 contracts** (negative ⇒ short, the usual hedge against rising rates).

Why B is correct: $$|N^*| \approx 110$$, and the negative sign means **short** futures.

- A (short 76) wrongly uses the **portfolio** duration (5.5) for $$D_F$$ as well, or otherwise mis-divides.
- C (short 138) swaps the durations (uses 6.4-style mix) or drops the CTD adjustment.
- D (long 110) has the right size but the **wrong direction** — hedging a long portfolio against rising rates requires **shorting** futures.

📌 Exam tip: $$N^* = -\frac{P D_P}{F_C D_F}$$, with $$D_F$$ = duration of the **CTD bond at expiry**, not the portfolio's. Negative ⇒ short. Hedging long bonds against rate rises ⇒ short futures.

**Q7.** A US **corporate** bond pays an 8% coupon semiannually on January 1 and July 1. For settlement on April 1, the accrued interest per $100 face under the correct convention is closest to:
- A. $2.00
- B. $1.96
- C. $2.04
- D. $1.78

**Answer: A**

Restate: compute accrued interest using the **30/360** convention (US corporate bonds).

1. **Convention:** US corporate bonds use **30/360** — each month = 30 days, year = 360.
2. **Days from Jan 1 to Apr 1 (30/360):** 3 full months $$= 3 \times 30 = 90$$ days. Coupon period Jan 1 → Jul 1 $$= 180$$ days.
3. **Semiannual coupon:** $$8\%/2 = 4$$ per 100 face.
4. **Accrued interest:**
$$\text{AI} = 4 \times \frac{90}{180} = 4 \times 0.5 = \$2.00.$$

Why A is correct: under 30/360, Jan 1→Apr 1 is exactly 90 of 180 days = half the period, so AI = half the coupon = $2.00.

- B ($1.96) uses **Actual/Actual** (90 actual days / 181 or 182), the wrong convention for a corporate bond.
- C ($2.04) over-counts actual days (e.g., 92/181 style).
- D ($1.78) mis-applies the day count (e.g., 80/180).

📌 Exam tip: US **corporates/munis = 30/360**; **Treasuries = Actual/Actual**; **money market (T-bills, SOFR) = Actual/360**. Pick the convention from the instrument before counting days.
