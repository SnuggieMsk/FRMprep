**Q1.** A 144-day Treasury bill with $100 face is quoted at a discount rate of 4.00%. Its price per $100 face is closest to:

- A. $96.00
- B. $98.40
- C. $98.42
- D. $98.36

**Answer: B**

The ask: price a T-bill from its quoted discount rate.

1. Formula: $$P = F\left(1 - d\cdot\frac{n}{360}\right)$$
2. Plug in: $$P = 100\left(1 - 0.04\times\frac{144}{360}\right).$$
3. Day fraction: $$144/360 = 0.40.$$
4. Discount: $$0.04 \times 0.40 = 0.016.$$
5. Price: $$100(1 - 0.016) = \$98.40.$$

Why B is correct: the discount-rate convention uses a 360-day year and applies the rate to face value.

Distractor traps:
- A ($96.00) annualizes incorrectly, applying the full 4% with no day-count fraction.
- C ($98.42) uses a 365-day year ($$144/365$$) — the bill convention is actual/360.
- D ($98.36) uses $$n = 148$$ or otherwise mis-counts days.

📌 Exam tip: T-bill discount rate uses actual/360 and applies to *face*, not price. This price formula is linear in d — no compounding.

**Q2.** Using the T-bill from Q1 (price $98.40, 144 days), its true annualized investment yield (bond-equivalent, actual/365 basis) is closest to:

- A. 4.00%
- B. 1.63%
- C. 4.12%
- D. 3.94%

**Answer: C**

The ask: convert the discount-rate price into a true (investment) yield and confirm it exceeds the discount rate.

1. Formula: $$y = \frac{F - P}{P}\times\frac{365}{n}.$$
2. Period return: $$\frac{100 - 98.40}{98.40} = \frac{1.60}{98.40} = 0.016260.$$
3. Annualize (actual/365): $$\frac{365}{144} = 2.5347.$$
4. Yield: $$0.016260 \times 2.5347 = 0.04121 = 4.12\%.$$

Why C is correct: dividing the gain by the *price paid* (not face) and annualizing on 365 days both raise the figure above the quoted 4.00%.

Distractor traps:
- A (4.00%) treats the quoted discount rate as the yield — the core trap; the discount rate always understates the true yield.
- B (1.63%) is the unannualized 144-day return (0.01626).
- D (3.94%) divides by face (100) instead of price, understating the yield.

📌 Exam tip: Discount rate < true yield, always — because (i) the gain is divided by face, not price, and (ii) a 360-day year shortens annualization. Never discount cash flows with a quoted discount rate.

**Q3.** Two Treasury bonds are observed. Bond A: 6-month maturity, 4% semiannual coupon, price $100.47. Bond B: 1-year maturity, 4% semiannual coupon, price $100.40. The implied discount factor $$d(1.0)$$ is closest to:

- A. 0.9852
- B. 0.9650
- C. 0.9608
- D. 0.9750

**Answer: B**

The ask: bootstrap the 1-year discount factor from two bond prices.

1. Bond A has one cash flow of $$100 + 2 = 102$$ at t = 0.5: $$102\cdot d(0.5) = 100.47.$$
2. Solve: $$d(0.5) = 100.47/102 = 0.9850.$$
3. Bond B cash flows: $$2$$ at t = 0.5 and $$102$$ at t = 1.0. Price equation: $$2\cdot d(0.5) + 102\cdot d(1.0) = 100.40.$$
4. Substitute: $$2(0.9850) + 102\cdot d(1.0) = 100.40 \Rightarrow 1.97 + 102\cdot d(1.0) = 100.40.$$
5. Solve: $$d(1.0) = \frac{100.40 - 1.97}{102} = \frac{98.43}{102} = 0.9650.$$

Why B is correct: bootstrap shortest maturity first, then substitute forward.

Distractor traps:
- A (0.9852) is $$d(0.5)$$, not $$d(1.0)$$ — solved only the first bond.
- C (0.9608) forgets the intermediate coupon, dividing $$100.40/102 - $$ adjustments incorrectly (omits the t = 0.5 coupon cash flow).
- D (0.9750) uses the wrong coupon (treats it as a 5% bond / mis-sums the cash flows).

📌 Exam tip: Bootstrap from the front. Each new bond adds exactly one unknown discount factor; substitute previously solved ones. Discount factors must *decline* with maturity for positive rates.

**Q4.** A Treasury bond pays a 6% annual coupon (3.00 semiannually per $100 face) and is quoted (clean) at $99.50. It is 90 days into a 182-day coupon period (actual/actual). The dirty (invoice) price the buyer pays is closest to:

- A. $99.50
- B. $98.02
- C. $100.98
- D. $102.50

**Answer: C**

The ask: compute the dirty (invoice) price from the clean quote and accrued interest.

1. Formula: $$\text{Dirty} = \text{Clean} + AI, \quad AI = \text{coupon payment}\times\frac{\text{days since last coupon}}{\text{days in period}}.$$
2. Semiannual coupon payment: $$6\%/2\times100 = 3.00.$$
3. Accrued fraction: $$90/182 = 0.49451.$$
4. Accrued interest: $$3.00 \times 0.49451 = 1.4835.$$
5. Dirty price: $$99.50 + 1.4835 = \$100.98.$$

Why C is correct: the buyer pays the clean quote plus accrued interest earned by the seller.

Distractor traps:
- A ($99.50) is the clean (quoted) price — what is *quoted*, not what is *paid*.
- B ($98.02) *subtracts* accrued interest — wrong sign (dirty = clean + AI).
- D ($102.50) adds a full coupon ($3.00) plus mis-adds, ignoring the day-count fraction.

📌 Exam tip: Dirty = Clean + AI. The buyer compensates the seller for accrued coupon. When replicating/arbitraging, always compare *dirty* against *dirty* — mixing clean and dirty manufactures phantom arbitrage.

**Q5.** Discount factors are $$d(0.5) = 0.985$$ and $$d(1.0) = 0.965$$. A 1-year Treasury paying a 5% semiannual coupon (cash flows 2.5 and 102.5) trades in the market at $100.00. The arbitrage-free fair price and the correct trade are:

- A. Fair $101.375; the bond is cheap, so buy the bond and short the replicating STRIPS.
- B. Fair $101.375; the bond is rich, so short the bond and buy the replicating STRIPS.
- C. Fair $98.66; the bond is rich, so short the bond.
- D. Fair $100.00; no arbitrage exists.

**Answer: A**

The ask: price the bond from discount factors, compare to market, and state the correct arbitrage legs.

1. Fair price: $$P = \sum_t CF_t\cdot d(t) = 2.5\,d(0.5) + 102.5\,d(1.0).$$
2. First flow: $$2.5\times0.985 = 2.4625.$$
3. Second flow: $$102.5\times0.965 = 98.9125.$$
4. Fair price: $$2.4625 + 98.9125 = \$101.375.$$
5. Market is $100.00 < $101.375, so the bond is **cheap by $1.375**.
6. Trade: buy the cheap bond at $100.00; short the replicating STRIPS package (sell 2.5 face of the 6-month STRIP and 102.5 face of the 1-year STRIP, receiving $101.375). Net cash today: $$+\$1.375$$ per $100 face; future flows net to zero.

Why A is correct: fair value exceeds the market price, and the rule is buy low / short high.

Distractor traps:
- B has the right fair price but reverses the legs — the bond is cheap, not rich; you buy it.
- C ($98.66) discounts only the final $102.5 cash flow, omitting the first coupon.
- D assumes market = fair; it does not (a no-arbitrage trap that ignores the computation).

📌 Exam tip: Direction discipline — buy LOW, short HIGH. Under pressure, candidates reverse the legs. Compute fair value, compare to market, then pick the trade that captures the positive cash difference today with zero future obligation.

**Q6.** Which statement about Treasury STRIPS and the law of one price is most accurate?

- A. C-STRIPS and P-STRIPS are coupon-bearing securities whose prices exceed face value.
- B. Each STRIP is a zero-coupon Treasury, so its price (per $100 face) equals a discount factor times 100, and stripping/reconstitution arbitrage pushes a bond's price toward the sum of its parts.
- C. Reconstitution converts a bond into separate STRIPS; stripping combines STRIPS into a bond.
- D. Because of stripping arbitrage, STRIPS and the underlying bond never deviate in price.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct statement on STRIPS mechanics and arbitrage.

Why B is correct: STRIPS are single-payment (zero-coupon) Treasuries, so each STRIP price *is* a discount factor (scaled by 100). Dealers can strip a bond into pieces or reconstitute pieces into a bond; this two-way arbitrage forces the bond's price toward the sum of its STRIP values.

Distractor traps:
- A is false — STRIPS are zero-coupon and trade at a *discount* to face.
- C reverses the definitions — *stripping* splits a bond into pieces; *reconstitution* reassembles pieces into a bond.
- D overstates the arbitrage — small deviations persist (P-STRIPS of liquid bonds can trade rich, long C-STRIPS cheap) due to supply/demand, liquidity, and frictions.

📌 Exam tip: STRIP price ÷ 100 = discount factor. Strip = take apart; reconstitute = put together. Arbitrage *bounds* deviations but does not eliminate them — frictions (bid-ask, repo, liquidity) leave small residual mispricings.

**Q7.** An apparent arbitrage between an off-the-run Treasury and a STRIPS package is computed at 0.05 per $100 face. The round-trip transaction costs (bid-ask spreads plus repo financing) total 0.12 per $100 face. The most accurate conclusion is:

- A. The 0.05 mispricing is a riskless profit and should be executed immediately.
- B. The mispricing is smaller than round-trip costs, so it is not exploitable; such frictions (bid-ask, financing, liquidity premia) are why deviations survive.
- C. On-the-run bonds always trade cheap to off-the-run bonds, so the trade is reversed.
- D. Liquidity premia make the true mispricing larger than computed, so the profit is understated.

**Answer: B**

The ask: judge whether a sub-cost mispricing is exploitable and why deviations persist.

Why B is correct: an apparent mispricing (0.05) smaller than the round-trip cost (0.12) cannot be monetized — executing would lose 0.07 net. Real-world frictions — bid-ask spreads, repo financing, balance-sheet/capital charges, liquidity premia, market segmentation — cap arbitrage and explain why small deviations survive.

Distractor traps:
- A ignores transaction costs — net of costs the trade *loses* money, so it is not riskless profit.
- C reverses the liquidity relationship — *on-the-run* (most recently issued) Treasuries trade *rich* (lower yield) to off-the-run because of liquidity and repo specialness.
- D invents extra mispricing — liquidity premia are part of why the deviation exists, not a reason the computed profit is understated.

📌 Exam tip: Arbitrage is only real if the gap exceeds round-trip costs. On-the-run trades RICH to off-the-run (liquidity/repo specialness). Frictions bound — but do not erase — pricing deviations.
