**Q1.** A business line records operational losses with a Poisson frequency of λ = 8 events per year and a severity distribution with a mean loss of $150,000 per event. The annual expected operational loss is closest to:

- A. $18,750
- B. $150,000
- C. $1,200,000
- D. $9,600,000

**Answer: C**

The ask: compute the expected annual aggregate operational loss in the loss distribution approach (LDA).

1. Formula (compound Poisson): $$E[\text{annual loss}] = \lambda \times E[\text{severity}].$$
2. Plug in: $$8 \times \$150{,}000.$$
3. Compute: $$\$1{,}200{,}000.$$

Why C is correct: expected aggregate loss is the expected number of events times the expected size per event.

Distractor traps:
- A ($18,750) divides severity by frequency ($$150{,}000/8$$) — inverted operation.
- B ($150,000) reports the mean severity per event, ignoring frequency.
- D ($9,600,000) multiplies by an extra factor (e.g., treats λ as monthly and annualizes incorrectly).

📌 Exam tip: Expected aggregate loss is simple (λ × mean severity). But this number says nothing about capital — the 99.9% tail is driven by the *severity tail* (the lognormal σ), so two units with identical EL can demand very different capital.

**Q2.** Under the Standardized Measurement Approach (SMA), a bank has a Business Indicator Component (BIC) of $3.0 billion and average annual operational losses over the past 10 years of $300 million. The Internal Loss Multiplier is $$ILM = \ln\!\left(e - 1 + \left(\tfrac{LC}{BIC}\right)^{0.8}\right)$$ with the loss component $$LC = 15 \times \text{average annual losses}.$$ The bank's operational risk capital is closest to:

- A. $3.00 billion
- B. $3.40 billion
- C. $4.50 billion
- D. $6.75 billion

**Answer: B**

The ask: compute SMA capital = BIC × ILM.

1. Loss component: $$LC = 15 \times \$300\text{M} = \$4.5\text{B}.$$
2. Ratio: $$LC/BIC = 4.5/3.0 = 1.5.$$
3. Raise to 0.8: $$1.5^{0.8} = e^{0.8\ln 1.5} = e^{0.8 \times 0.4055} = e^{0.3244} = 1.3832.$$
4. ILM: $$\ln(e - 1 + 1.3832) = \ln(2.7183 - 1 + 1.3832) = \ln(3.1015) = 1.1319.$$
5. Capital: $$BIC \times ILM = \$3.0\text{B} \times 1.1319 = \$3.40\text{B}.$$

Why B is correct: loss history above the neutral point (LC > BIC) scales capital up by ~13%.

Distractor traps:
- A ($3.00B) sets ILM = 1 — correct only if LC = BIC; here LC = 1.5 × BIC.
- C ($4.50B) reports LC itself ($$15\times300\text{M}$$) as if it were capital.
- D ($6.75B) skips the ILM log compression and multiplies BIC by the raw ratio (2.25), grossly overstating.

📌 Exam tip: ILM = 1 when LC = BIC. The log structure *compresses* the impact of loss history — even LC = 2 × BIC only lifts capital ~24%. Know the structure (size charge scaled by loss experience), not the coefficient minutiae.

**Q3.** Two banks have identical Business Indicator Components. Bank Y's 10-year average operational losses are double Bank Z's. Holding all else equal, which statement is correct?

- A. The two banks hold identical operational risk capital because BIC is the same.
- B. Bank Y holds more capital because its higher loss history raises its Internal Loss Multiplier.
- C. Bank Z holds more capital because lower losses signal under-reporting penalized by supervisors.
- D. Capital differs only if the banks use different business-line β factors.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify why capital differs under the SMA when BIC is equal.

Why B is correct: SMA capital = BIC × ILM, and the ILM rises with the bank's own loss component (LC = 15 × average annual losses). Worse loss history → higher ILM → more capital.

Distractor traps:
- A is false — BIC equal does not make capital equal; the ILM differentiates them.
- C reverses the relationship — higher losses (Bank Y) drive higher capital, not lower.
- D refers to β factors, which belong to the *legacy* Standardized (TSA) approach, not the current SMA.

📌 Exam tip: Under the SMA, the loss-experience lever is the ILM, not β coefficients. Beware moral hazard: allocating op-risk capital by loss history alone can tempt units to hide losses — allocation should also reward strong control environments.

**Q4.** Which of the following is the best example of a key risk indicator (KRI) rather than a realized operational loss event?

- A. A $2 million settlement failure on a foreign-exchange trade.
- B. A regulatory fine for anti-money-laundering deficiencies.
- C. A rising monthly count of unreconciled trades in the back office.
- D. A data-center fire that halts processing for two days.

**Answer: C**

The ask: distinguish a forward-looking KRI from a realized loss event.

Why C is correct: a KRI is a forward-looking metric tied to a risk *driver*, with thresholds that trigger escalation before losses materialize. A rising unreconciled-trade count flags deteriorating controls ahead of an actual loss.

Distractor traps:
- A is a realized loss event (execution/process management, type 7), not a leading indicator.
- B is a realized loss event (clients, products & business practices, type 4).
- D is a realized loss event (damage to physical assets / business disruption).

📌 Exam tip: KRIs are *leading* (staff turnover, system downtime, failed settlements, audit findings); loss data is *lagging* (realized events in the loss database). The exam loves to slip a realized loss into a "which is a KRI?" question.

**Q5.** Under the Basel definition, which of the following is NOT classified as operational risk?

- A. Losses from a rogue trader breaching position limits.
- B. A multi-billion-dollar fine for market manipulation.
- C. A decline in profitability after management enters a market that proves unprofitable.
- D. Losses from a cyber attack that disables trading systems.

**Answer: C**

The ask: identify the item excluded from the Basel operational risk definition.

Why C is correct: Basel defines operational risk as loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or external events. It explicitly *includes legal risk* but *excludes strategic and reputational risk*. A bad strategic market-entry decision is strategic risk — excluded.

Distractor traps:
- A is internal fraud (event type 1) — included.
- B is clients/products/business practices (type 4) — included, and historically the largest dollar losses.
- D is external fraud / business disruption (types 2 and 6) — included.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the boundary — legal risk is IN; strategic and reputational risk are OUT. GARP frequently tests this exact distinction with a strategic-decision distractor.

**Q6.** A risk manager argues that because the bank holds substantial operational risk capital, its operational risk has been "reduced." The most accurate critique is:

- A. Correct — capital directly lowers the probability of operational losses.
- B. Incorrect — capital absorbs losses when they occur; controls (segregation of duties, limits, BCP) reduce risk.
- C. Correct — but only for high-frequency, low-severity losses.
- D. Incorrect — operational risk can only be reduced by purchasing insurance.

**Answer: B**

The ask: critique the claim that holding capital reduces operational risk.

Why B is correct: capital is a *buffer* that absorbs losses after they occur; it does not change the likelihood or magnitude of an operational event. Risk is *reduced* by controls — segregation of duties, position limits, business-continuity planning — and partly transferred via insurance.

Distractor traps:
- A is exactly the misconception being tested — capital does not lower loss probability.
- C is wrong on the mechanism — capital absorbs regardless of frequency band; it never reduces risk.
- D overstates insurance — it transfers severity (subject to basis risk, payout delays, counterparty risk, and Basel caps), but it is not the *only* mitigant, and it does not reduce the underlying risk drivers.

📌 Exam tip: Capital *absorbs*, controls *reduce*, insurance *transfers*. Keep these verbs straight — GARP builds trap statements by swapping them.

**Q7.** Which combination correctly pairs operational risk inputs with their primary purpose in capital and management?

- A. Internal loss data is best for the extreme tail; scenario analysis covers high-frequency events.
- B. External (consortium) data is used unadjusted; firm size is irrelevant when pooling losses.
- C. Scenario analysis fills the tail where internal data is sparse; external data must be scaled for firm size; BEICFs adjust for current control quality.
- D. BEICFs measure realized losses; scenario analysis measures the frequency of routine processing errors.

**Answer: C**

The ask: match operational risk modeling inputs to their roles (the four AMA-era inputs, still used in management).

Why C is correct: internal loss data is short and truncated; scenario analysis (expert workshops) quantifies plausible low-frequency/high-severity tail events; external consortium data (e.g., ORX) must be scaled for the firm's size before use; and BEICFs (business environment and internal control factors) adjust the estimate for current control quality.

Distractor traps:
- A inverts the roles — internal data is thin in the tail; scenario analysis (not internal data) is what fills the tail.
- B is false — external data requires scaling for firm size; pooling raw losses across differently sized banks distorts severity.
- D mis-defines both — BEICFs are forward-looking control/environment factors, not realized losses; scenario analysis targets rare tail events, not routine high-frequency errors.

📌 Exam tip: The four inputs — internal loss data, external data, scenario analysis, BEICFs. Tail = scenario analysis; cross-firm comparability = scale external data; "how good are controls right now?" = BEICFs.
