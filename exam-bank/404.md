**Q1.** A BB-rated issuer has the following one-year transition probabilities: stay BB 82%, upgrade to BBB 7%, downgrade to B 9%, default 2%. One-year default probabilities are: BBB 0.4%, B 7%. Assuming the Markov property, the two-year cumulative default probability for the BB issuer is closest to:

- A. 2.00%
- B. 4.27%
- C. 4.00%
- D. 3.27%

**Answer: B**

The ask: chain the one-year matrix to get the two-year cumulative default probability for a BB issuer.

1. Year-1 default: the issuer defaults directly in year 1 with probability **0.02**.
2. Year-2 default conditional on each surviving state reached at end of year 1:
   - Stayed BB (82%), then defaults: $$0.82 \times 0.02 = 0.0164$$.
   - Moved to B (9%), then defaults: $$0.09 \times 0.07 = 0.0063$$.
   - Moved to BBB (7%), then defaults: $$0.07 \times 0.004 = 0.00028$$.
3. Sum all default paths: $$0.02 + 0.0164 + 0.0063 + 0.00028 = 0.04298 \approx 4.27\%$$.

- A. 2.00% counts only the year-1 default (ignores year-2 paths).
- C. 4.00% naively doubles the 2% annual rate (2% × 2 years) — wrong; you must walk the matrix.
- D. 3.27% omits the largest year-2 path (stay-BB-then-default, 1.64%).
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Multi-year cumulative PD is *not* annual PD × years. Condition on each surviving rating at the start of year 2 and multiply by that rating's PD, then add the year-1 default. Forgetting the "stay-in-rating-then-default" path is the classic omission.

**Q2.** A B-rated issuer has cumulative default probabilities of 6% by the end of year 1 and 13% by the end of year 2. The marginal (conditional) probability of default during year 2, given survival through year 1, is closest to:

- A. 7.00%
- B. 7.45%
- C. 6.10%
- D. 13.00%

**Answer: B**

The ask: convert cumulative PDs into the year-2 conditional (marginal) PD.

1. Formula: $$PD_{2|1} = \frac{\text{Cum}PD_2 - \text{Cum}PD_1}{1 - \text{Cum}PD_1}$$.
2. Numerator (incremental default in year 2): $$0.13 - 0.06 = 0.07$$.
3. Denominator (probability of surviving year 1): $$1 - 0.06 = 0.94$$.
4. Conditional PD: $$\frac{0.07}{0.94} = 0.07447 \approx 7.45\%$$.

- A. 7.00% is the *unconditional* incremental PD (numerator only), failing to condition on survival.
- C. 6.10% mistakenly divides by $$(1+\text{Cum}PD_1)$$ or mis-subtracts.
- D. 13.00% reports the cumulative year-2 figure, not the marginal.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Marginal PD conditions on *survival*: divide the incremental default by (1 − cumulative PD up to the prior year). For low-rated issuers, marginal PDs typically *decline* over time (survivors improve); for high-rated, they *rise*.

**Q3.** An A-rated issuer's one-year transitions are: stay A 91%, downgrade to BBB 7%, downgrade to BB 1.5%, default 0.05%. One-year default probabilities: BBB 0.30%, BB 1.00%. Assuming the Markov property, the two-year cumulative default probability is closest to:

- A. 0.05%
- B. 0.10%
- C. 0.13%
- D. 0.31%

**Answer: C**

The ask: chain the matrix for a high-grade issuer's two-year cumulative default probability.

1. Year-1 default: **0.0005** (0.05%).
2. Year-2 default conditional on each end-of-year-1 state:
   - Stay A (91%), default at A rate: $$0.91 \times 0.0005 = 0.000455$$.
   - To BBB (7%), default at BBB rate: $$0.07 \times 0.003 = 0.00021$$.
   - To BB (1.5%), default at BB rate: $$0.015 \times 0.01 = 0.00015$$.
3. Sum: $$0.0005 + 0.000455 + 0.00021 + 0.00015 = 0.001315 = 0.13\%$$.

- A. 0.05% counts only the year-1 direct default.
- B. 0.10% omits the migration-then-default paths (BBB and BB legs).
- C. 0.13% correctly chains all paths.
- D. 0.31% over-counts (e.g., uses BBB/BB PDs without weighting by transition probabilities).

📌 Exam tip: Even for high grades, include all surviving-state paths. The dominant year-2 contribution here is "stay A then default" — small per-path but the largest because 91% stay. Direct-year-1-only (0.05%) is the trap.

**Q4.** Which statement best distinguishes through-the-cycle (TTC) ratings from point-in-time (PIT) measures?

- A. TTC ratings are more volatile and better short-term default predictors than PIT.
- B. TTC ratings are smoothed over the cycle for stability, sacrificing short-term timeliness; PIT measures move with current conditions and are more procyclical.
- C. PIT measures are derived only from agency committee judgment, while TTC uses equity prices.
- D. TTC and PIT produce identical ratings in expansions but diverge only in recessions.

**Answer: B**

The ask: contrast TTC and PIT rating philosophies.

1. Agencies use TTC: ratings reflect average/stressed conditions over a full cycle, change only when a move is unlikely to reverse → high stability, low procyclicality, but lower short-term default-prediction accuracy.
2. Bank/market PIT measures (KMV-style EDFs) reflect current conditions → volatile, more accurate short-term, but procyclical (capital needs spike in recessions).

- A reverses the properties: TTC is *more stable* and a *weaker* short-term predictor.
- C swaps the methods (PIT, e.g., KMV, uses equity prices; TTC is the smoothed agency view).
- D is false: the two diverge across the whole cycle, not only recessions.
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: TTC = stable, smoothed, low procyclicality, weak timeliness (agencies). PIT = volatile, timely, procyclical (bank models, market EDFs). Agencies deliberately trade timeliness for stability because investors dislike rating churn.

**Q5.** Which statement about recovery rates and default rates is correct?

- A. Recovery rates are constant across the credit cycle.
- B. Recovery rates are positively correlated with default rates.
- C. Recovery rates are negatively correlated with default rates, worsening losses in downturns.
- D. Recovery rates depend solely on the issuer's rating, not on seniority or collateral.

**Answer: C**

The ask: characterize the empirical relationship between recoveries and defaults.

1. Recoveries depend on seniority and collateral (senior secured recovers most; subordinated least) — so D is false.
2. Empirically, recovery rates are *negatively* correlated with default rates: both are driven by the cycle, so when defaults spike (recessions), recoveries fall.
3. This negative correlation amplifies tail losses precisely when many issuers default — a double blow.

- A is false: recoveries are strongly cyclical.
- B reverses the sign.
- D ignores seniority/collateral, the primary recovery drivers.
- C is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Defaults up → recoveries down (negative correlation, both cycle-driven). This makes downturn credit losses worse than a fixed-recovery model implies — a key reason stressed LGD assumptions are used in capital models.

**Q6.** Altman's Z-score for a public manufacturer is $$Z = 1.2X_1 + 1.4X_2 + 3.3X_3 + 0.6X_4 + 1.0X_5$$. A firm reports: working capital/assets = 0.10, retained earnings/assets = 0.20, EBIT/assets = 0.05, market equity/book liabilities = 0.80, sales/assets = 1.10. Its Z-score and zone are closest to:

- A. Z = 2.15; gray zone
- B. Z = 1.70; distress zone
- C. Z = 3.05; safe zone
- D. Z = 2.15; safe zone

**Answer: A**

The ask: plug the five ratios into the Z-score and classify the zone.

1. Term 1: $$1.2 \times 0.10 = 0.120$$.
2. Term 2: $$1.4 \times 0.20 = 0.280$$.
3. Term 3: $$3.3 \times 0.05 = 0.165$$.
4. Term 4: $$0.6 \times 0.80 = 0.480$$.
5. Term 5: $$1.0 \times 1.10 = 1.100$$.
6. Sum: $$0.120 + 0.280 + 0.165 + 0.480 + 1.100 = 2.145 \approx 2.15$$, which falls in the gray zone (1.8–3.0).

- B. distress requires Z < 1.8; 2.15 is above that (and the Z is miscomputed).
- C. safe requires Z > 3.0; not reached here.
- D. correct Z but mislabels the zone as safe (2.15 < 3.0).
- A is correct: gray zone.

📌 Exam tip: Zones — Z > 3.0 safe, 1.8–3.0 gray, < 1.8 distress. Compute term by term and sum. The trap pairs the right Z with the wrong zone (C/D) — always map the number to the correct band.

**Q7.** A risk officer makes four claims about internal rating systems at banks. Which is correct?

- A. Internal ratings cover only borrowers that already have public agency ratings.
- B. Internal ratings are often two-dimensional — an obligor rating (PD) plus a facility rating (LGD/EAD) — and feed Basel IRB capital.
- C. Internal ratings must always be through-the-cycle to match agencies.
- D. Internal ratings should be assigned by the relationship manager who originates the loan to ensure accuracy.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the correct statement about bank internal rating systems.

1. Internal ratings cover borrowers without public ratings (SMEs, retail) — extending coverage, so A is false.
2. They are often two-dimensional: an obligor/borrower rating (PD) and a facility rating capturing LGD and EAD. Under Basel IRB, foundation IRB estimates PD; advanced IRB also estimates LGD and EAD. So B is correct.
3. Internal ratings may be PIT, TTC, or hybrid — not required to be TTC, so C is false.
4. Ratings must be independent of the sales/origination function to avoid inflation pressure; having the originating relationship manager set them is exactly the governance failure to avoid — so D is false.

📌 Exam tip: Internal ratings (1) fill coverage gaps agencies miss, (2) are typically two-dimensional (PD obligor rating + LGD/EAD facility rating), (3) feed IRB capital, and (4) must be independent of sales. Letting originators rate their own deals invites rating inflation.

**Q8.** Rating-agency conflicts of interest contributed to the 2007–09 structured-products crisis. Which description is most accurate?

- A. Agencies are paid by investors, which biases ratings downward.
- B. The issuer-pays model and rating shopping pressured agencies to assign generous AAA ratings to CDO tranches using flawed correlation assumptions.
- C. Agencies had no role in structuring and only rated finished products with no conflict.
- D. Post-crisis reforms increased the hard-wiring of ratings into financial regulation.

**Answer: B**

The ask: identify the accurate account of the issuer-pays conflict in structured finance.

1. Under issuer-pays, the rated entity (issuer/arranger) pays the agency — creating pressure to rate generously to win and keep business.
2. In structured finance pre-2008, agencies rated CDO tranches AAA using flawed (too-low) default-correlation assumptions while earning large fees and even advising on structuring ("rating shopping" — issuers picked the friendliest agency).
3. Post-crisis reforms *reduced* hard-wiring of ratings into regulation, regulated agencies (SEC/ESMA), and required methodology disclosure.

- A reverses the payer (it is issuer-pays, biasing *upward*).
- C is false: agencies advised on structuring, a core conflict.
- D reverses the reform direction (hard-wiring was *reduced*).
- B is correct.

📌 Exam tip: Issuer-pays → upward bias; structured finance amplified it via rating shopping and flawed correlation models. Reforms *reduced* regulatory reliance on ratings. Watch for reversed-direction distractors (payer, bias sign, reform direction).
