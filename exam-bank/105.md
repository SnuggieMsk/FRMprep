**Q1.** An investor combines 70% in equity fund A ($$E[R]=10\%$$, $$\sigma=20\%$$) and 30% in bond fund B ($$E[R]=6\%$$, $$\sigma=10\%$$). The two funds' returns are statistically independent ($$\rho=0$$). What are the portfolio's expected return and standard deviation, and how does the standard deviation compare with the weighted-average volatility?

- A. $$E[R_p]=8.8\%$$, $$\sigma_p=14.32\%$$, which is below the weighted-average volatility of 17%
- B. $$E[R_p]=8.8\%$$, $$\sigma_p=17.00\%$$, equal to the weighted-average volatility
- C. $$E[R_p]=8.0\%$$, $$\sigma_p=14.32\%$$, below the weighted-average volatility of 15%
- D. $$E[R_p]=8.8\%$$, $$\sigma_p=15.20\%$$, below the weighted-average volatility of 17%

**Answer: A**

The question asks for two-asset portfolio return and risk with zero correlation, and the diversification comparison.

1. Expected return is the weighted average:
$$E[R_p] = 0.7(10\%) + 0.3(6\%) = 7\% + 1.8\% = 8.8\%$$
2. Variance with $$\rho=0$$ drops the cross term:
$$\sigma_p^2 = (0.7)^2(0.20)^2 + (0.3)^2(0.10)^2 + 2(0.7)(0.3)(0)(0.20)(0.10)$$
$$\sigma_p^2 = 0.0196 + 0.0009 + 0 = 0.0205$$
3. Standard deviation:
$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.0205} \approx 14.32\%$$
4. Weighted-average volatility:
$$0.7(20\%) + 0.3(10\%) = 14\% + 3\% = 17\%$$
Since $$\rho<1$$, $$\sigma_p = 14.32\% < 17\%$$ — the diversification benefit. That is A.

- B forgets the diversification effect and sets $$\sigma_p$$ equal to the 17% weighted average (only true if $$\rho=+1$$).
- C uses the right $$\sigma_p$$ but mis-computes $$E[R_p]$$ as 8.0% (it averaged 10 and 6 equally instead of weighting 70/30) and mislabels the weighted-average volatility as 15%.
- D keeps the cross term as if $$\rho>0$$, getting 15.20% instead of 14.32%.

📌 Exam tip: when $$\rho=0$$, the covariance term vanishes — but variance still adds (it is *not* the weighted average of the individual variances or sigmas). The weighted average of sigmas (17%) is the *no-diversification* benchmark you only hit when $$\rho=+1$$.

**Q2.** Stock M has a covariance with the market of 0.048 and the market variance is 0.04 ($$\sigma_m=20\%$$). The risk-free rate is 2.5% and the market's expected return is 8.5%. An analyst forecasts that M will return 9.0% next year. What is M's CAPM required return, and is the stock attractive?

- A. Required return 9.7%; the stock is unattractive (negative alpha of −0.7%)
- B. Required return 8.5%; the stock is attractive (positive alpha of +0.5%)
- C. Required return 9.7%; the stock is attractive (positive alpha of +0.7%)
- D. Required return 7.2%; the stock is attractive (positive alpha of +1.8%)

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the CAPM required return and the resulting alpha sign.

1. Compute beta from covariance and market variance:
$$\beta_M = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R_M,R_m)}{\sigma_m^2} = \frac{0.048}{0.04} = 1.2$$
2. CAPM required return:
$$E[R_M] = R_f + \beta_M(E[R_m]-R_f) = 2.5\% + 1.2(8.5\% - 2.5\%)$$
$$= 2.5\% + 1.2(6.0\%) = 2.5\% + 7.2\% = 9.7\%$$
3. Alpha = forecast − required = $$9.0\% - 9.7\% = -0.7\%$$. The forecast falls short of the required return → **negative alpha → unattractive** (plots below the SML). That is A.

- B uses beta = 1.0 (it divided covariance by $$\sigma_m$$ = 0.20 instead of $$\sigma_m^2$$ = 0.04, or just assumed the market beta), giving 8.5% and a false +0.5% alpha.
- C uses the right 9.7% but flips the alpha sign, calling −0.7% a +0.7% (a sign flip — the trap).
- D forgets to add back $$R_f$$ inside the premium or uses 7.2% (the risk premium term alone) as the required return, then computes a spurious +1.8% alpha.

📌 Exam tip: beta = covariance ÷ market **variance** ($$\sigma_m^2$$), not ÷ $$\sigma_m$$ — dividing by the standard deviation is a classic error that yields beta = 1.2 vs. the wrong 1.0×scale. Alpha = forecast − required; a *negative* alpha means the stock is overpriced/unattractive.

**Q3.** A portfolio is allocated 40% to a stock with $$\beta=1.5$$, 25% to a stock with $$\beta=0.7$$, and 35% to Treasury bills. The risk-free rate is 3% and the market risk premium is 5.5%. What is the portfolio's CAPM expected return?

- A. 8.05%
- B. 7.26%
- C. 4.26%
- D. 10.50%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the portfolio's expected return via portfolio beta and the SML.

1. Portfolio beta is the weighted average of component betas (T-bills have $$\beta=0$$):
$$\beta_p = 0.40(1.5) + 0.25(0.7) + 0.35(0) = 0.60 + 0.175 + 0 = 0.775$$
2. Apply CAPM with the market premium of 5.5%:
$$E[R_p] = R_f + \beta_p \times (\text{premium}) = 3\% + 0.775(5.5\%)$$
$$= 3\% + 4.2625\% = 7.2625\% \approx 7.26\%$$

That is B.

- A (8.05%) ignores the T-bill weight when computing beta — it averages only the two stocks' betas as if they were 100% of the portfolio ($$0.4/0.65$$-type rescaling or treating weights as summing without cash), inflating beta.
- C (4.26%) forgets to add the risk-free rate ($$0.775 \times 5.5\% = 4.26\%$$ is just the premium term).
- D (10.50%) uses the highest beta (1.5) for the whole portfolio rather than the weighted average.

📌 Exam tip: include the risk-free/T-bill sleeve at $$\beta=0$$ when forming $$\beta_p$$ — it *drags beta down*, it does not disappear. Then never forget to add $$R_f$$ back: $$E[R_p]=R_f+\beta_p\times\text{premium}$$.

**Q4.** Stock X has a total volatility of 40% and a correlation of 0.40 with the market. The market has volatility 16% and expected return 10%; the risk-free rate is 3%. A junior analyst computes X's CAPM expected return as 21% by plugging X's 40% volatility directly into the capital market line. What is X's *correct* CAPM expected return, and what mistake did the analyst make?

- A. 17.5%; the analyst should have used the SML with beta, but used the right line
- B. 10.0%; the analyst used total volatility in the CML instead of computing beta and using the SML
- C. 21.0%; the analyst's computation is correct
- D. 7.0%; the analyst forgot the risk-free rate

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the correct CAPM expected return and to identify the analyst's error.

1. The CAPM/SML prices only *systematic* risk, so first compute beta — not total volatility:
$$\beta_X = \rho_{Xm}\frac{\sigma_X}{\sigma_m} = 0.40 \times \frac{0.40}{0.16} = 0.40 \times 2.5 = 1.0$$
2. Apply the SML:
$$E[R_X] = R_f + \beta_X(E[R_m]-R_f) = 3\% + 1.0(10\% - 3\%) = 3\% + 7\% = 10\%$$
3. The analyst's error: he treated the stock's *total* volatility (40%) as if it belonged on the **capital market line**, which applies only to *efficient* portfolios and uses total risk. For an individual stock you need **beta** on the **SML**; most of X's 40% volatility is diversifiable and earns no premium.

So the correct answer is 10% with the diagnosis in B.

- A reports 17.5% (it used some partial beta or mixed inputs) and wrongly says the analyst used the right line.
- C endorses the 21% error (using $$\sigma$$ in the CML for a single stock).
- D gives 7%, dropping $$R_f$$ — a different error, not the one made.

📌 Exam tip: for an *individual asset*, CAPM uses **beta** on the **SML**. Plugging an individual stock's total $$\sigma$$ into the CML is the signature trap (a single stock with idiosyncratic risk sits *below* the CML). Beta $$= \rho\,\sigma_i/\sigma_m$$ — high total volatility with low correlation can still give beta = 1.

**Q5.** Two stocks each have a standard deviation of 30%. An equally weighted portfolio of the two is observed to also have a standard deviation of exactly 30%. What does this imply about the correlation between the two stocks, and what would happen to portfolio volatility if the correlation were instead −1?

- A. The correlation is +1; at $$\rho=-1$$ an equally weighted portfolio would have 0% volatility
- B. The correlation is 0; at $$\rho=-1$$ volatility would rise to 42.4%
- C. The correlation is +1; at $$\rho=-1$$ volatility would rise to 60%
- D. The correlation is −1; at $$\rho=+1$$ volatility would be 0%

**Answer: A**

The question asks what correlation makes an equally weighted, equal-sigma portfolio's volatility equal the individual sigma, and what $$\rho=-1$$ would do.

1. Equal weights $$w_1=w_2=0.5$$, equal $$\sigma_1=\sigma_2=30\%$$. Portfolio variance:
$$\sigma_p^2 = 0.25(0.09) + 0.25(0.09) + 2(0.5)(0.5)\rho(0.30)(0.30)$$
$$= 0.0225 + 0.0225 + 0.045\rho = 0.045 + 0.045\rho$$
2. Set $$\sigma_p = 30\%$$ so $$\sigma_p^2 = 0.09$$:
$$0.045 + 0.045\rho = 0.09 \Rightarrow 0.045\rho = 0.045 \Rightarrow \rho = +1$$
Only at $$\rho=+1$$ does the portfolio sigma equal the (common) individual sigma — there is no diversification benefit.
3. At $$\rho=-1$$: $$\sigma_p^2 = 0.045 + 0.045(-1) = 0$$, so $$\sigma_p = 0\%$$ — equal weights of two perfectly negatively correlated, equal-volatility assets fully cancel.

That is A.

- B says $$\rho=0$$ (would give $$\sqrt{0.045}=21.2\%$$, not 30%) and absurdly claims $$\rho=-1$$ *raises* volatility.
- C correctly identifies $$\rho=+1$$ but claims $$\rho=-1$$ raises volatility to 60% — negative correlation reduces, never amplifies, risk.
- D inverts the correlations entirely.

📌 Exam tip: with equal weights and equal sigmas, $$\rho=+1$$ is the *only* case where the portfolio sigma equals the individual sigma (no diversification), and $$\rho=-1$$ drives it to *zero*. Lower correlation always reduces portfolio risk; it can never increase it above the weighted average.

**Q6.** An investor wants an expected return of 11% by holding a combination of the risk-free asset (3%) and the market portfolio ($$E[R_m]=9\%$$, $$\sigma_m=15\%$$) along the capital market line. What portfolio standard deviation must the investor accept, and what does the required market weight imply?

- A. $$\sigma_p=20\%$$, achieved by borrowing at the risk-free rate to hold 133.3% in the market
- B. $$\sigma_p=15\%$$, achieved by holding 100% in the market
- C. $$\sigma_p=11\%$$, achieved by holding 73.3% in the market
- D. $$\sigma_p=18.3\%$$, achieved by holding 122% in the market

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the CML standard deviation needed to reach an 11% target return, and what weight that implies.

1. On the CML, expected return is a linear blend of $$R_f$$ and the market: $$E[R_p]=R_f + w(E[R_m]-R_f)$$ where $$w$$ is the weight in the market. Solve for $$w$$:
$$11\% = 3\% + w(9\% - 3\%) \Rightarrow 8\% = w(6\%) \Rightarrow w = \frac{8}{6} = 1.333$$
2. $$w=133.3\% > 100\%$$ means the investor **borrows** at the risk-free rate (weight $$-33.3\%$$ in the risk-free asset) to lever up the market position.
3. Since the risk-free asset has zero volatility, portfolio volatility scales with the market weight:
$$\sigma_p = w\,\sigma_m = 1.333 \times 15\% = 20\%$$

That is A.

- B assumes 100% market (which only gives the 9% market return, not 11%).
- C sets $$\sigma_p$$ equal to the target return number (11%) — confusing return with risk — and uses a weight below 100%.
- D mis-solves the weight (122%) and the resulting sigma.

📌 Exam tip: on the CML, the return target sets the *market weight* $$w=(E[R_p]-R_f)/(E[R_m]-R_f)$$; a target above the market return forces $$w>1$$ = leverage (borrow at $$R_f$$). Then $$\sigma_p=w\,\sigma_m$$ because the risk-free leg adds no volatility. Don't equate the target return with the standard deviation.

**Q7.** Which of the following statements about the CML, the SML, and the underlying CAPM assumptions is correct?

- A. The slope of the SML is the market Sharpe ratio, while the slope of the CML is the market risk premium
- B. The CML applies to all assets and portfolios, while the SML applies only to efficient portfolios
- C. A fairly priced individual stock with large idiosyncratic risk plots on the SML but below the CML; the CAPM does not assume investors have heterogeneous multi-period horizons
- D. Under the CAPM, a stock with $$\beta<0$$ must have an expected return above the risk-free rate because all risk is rewarded

**Answer: C**

The question tests the CML/SML distinction and CAPM assumptions.

1. All *fairly priced* assets lie on the **SML** (which uses systematic risk, beta). Only *efficient* portfolios (mixes of $$R_f$$ and the market) lie on the **CML** (which uses total risk). An individual stock with large idiosyncratic risk is fairly priced on the SML but sits *inside* the frontier, hence *below* the CML.
2. The CAPM assumes a **single common period** and **homogeneous expectations** — it does *not* assume heterogeneous, multi-period horizons.
Both clauses of C are correct, so C is the answer.

- A swaps the slopes: the **CML** slope is the market Sharpe ratio $$(E[R_m]-R_f)/\sigma_m$$; the **SML** slope is the market risk premium $$(E[R_m]-R_f)$$.
- B reverses the domains: the **SML** applies to all assets/portfolios; the **CML** applies only to efficient portfolios.
- D is false: a stock with $$\beta<0$$ has expected return *below* $$R_f$$ (a hedge asset). And only *systematic* risk is rewarded, not "all risk."

📌 Exam tip: SML = beta axis, slope = market risk premium, applies to *everything*; CML = total-risk axis, slope = market Sharpe ratio, applies to *efficient portfolios only*. $$\beta<0$$ → expected return *below* $$R_f$$. CAPM assumes single-period, homogeneous expectations.
