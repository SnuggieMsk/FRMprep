**Q1.** A U.S. industrial firm has signed a contract to receive €50 million in 90 days from a European customer. It also operates a German plant whose euro-denominated assets and liabilities are consolidated into its USD financial statements, and it competes long-term against European exporters whose costs are in euros. The treasurer wants to hedge the €50 million receivable with a 90-day FX forward but is unsure how to treat the other two exposures. Which classification and treatment is most consistent with the curriculum?

- A. All three are transaction risk and should be hedged with the same forward
- B. The receivable is transaction risk (hedge with the forward); the consolidation of the German plant is translation risk (often left unhedged or hedged with FX debt); the competitive exposure is economic risk (managed structurally)
- C. The receivable is economic risk; the German plant is transaction risk; the competitive exposure is translation risk
- D. The receivable is transaction risk; both the German plant and the competitive exposure are translation risk and should be hedged with FX options

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to sort three FX exposures into the curriculum's three layers and match each to its typical treatment.

1. The €50m receivable is a **known future FX cash flow** — that is **transaction risk**, hedged with FX forwards/futures, a money-market hedge, or options. The forward is appropriate.
2. Consolidating the German plant's euro assets/liabilities into USD statements is an **accounting** exposure — **translation risk** — which is often left unhedged or hedged with FX-denominated debt.
3. The long-run competitive exposure to the level of the euro is **economic risk**, managed *structurally* (where to locate production, where to source), not with a single forward.

That mapping is B.

- A collapses all three into transaction risk; only the receivable is.
- C scrambles all three labels.
- D correctly tags the receivable but lumps the structural competitive exposure into translation risk and prescribes options for both — economic risk is not an accounting-consolidation issue and is not typically hedged with FX options.

📌 Exam tip: transaction = a *known* future FX cash flow; translation = *accounting consolidation* of foreign subs; economic = *long-run competitive* position. The treatments differ: forwards/options, FX debt/often unhedged, and structural decisions respectively.

**Q2.** In a Modigliani–Miller world with no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, and frictionless markets, a CFO argues the firm should run a large currency hedging program "to add shareholder value by reducing earnings volatility." Under MM assumptions, which response is correct, and what is the single strongest *real-world* justification that would actually create value?

- A. The CFO is right under MM; the strongest justification is that hedging removes systematic risk from the stock
- B. The CFO is wrong under MM (hedging adds no value because shareholders can hedge themselves); the strongest value-creating friction is reducing expected financial-distress and bankruptcy costs
- C. The CFO is right under MM; the strongest justification is that hedging always raises expected cash flows
- D. The CFO is wrong under MM; the strongest justification is that shareholders cannot diversify firm-specific risk

**Answer: B**

The question has two parts: what MM implies, and which friction most credibly makes hedging value-additive.

1. Under MM (no taxes, no distress costs, frictionless), hedging adds **no value** — shareholders can replicate or undo any corporate hedge on their own account at the same cost. So the CFO is **wrong** under MM.
2. Value appears only through real-world frictions. The strongest, most-cited one is that hedging **reduces the probability and cost of financial distress and bankruptcy** (also lower expected taxes under a convex schedule, and avoiding underinvestment). Reducing distress costs is the canonical answer.

That is B.

- A says the CFO is right under MM (false) and claims hedging removes *systematic* risk — corporate FX hedging does not strip systematic risk from equity.
- C asserts hedging "always raises expected cash flows," which is not generally true; hedging mainly reduces variability and has direct costs.
- D correctly says the CFO is wrong but gives a false justification: shareholders *can* diversify firm-specific risk themselves, which is exactly why that argument fails.

📌 Exam tip: MM benchmark → hedging is value-neutral; value comes only from frictions. The headline friction tested is *financial-distress/bankruptcy cost reduction*. "Shareholders can't diversify firm-specific risk" is a classic *wrong* answer — they can.

**Q3.** A refiner with a 10-year fixed-price commitment to deliver heating oil hedges by buying a stack of near-month crude futures and rolling them forward (a stack-and-roll). Over six months crude prices fall by 35%. The economic hedge is sound — the lower input cost on the future physical purchases offsets the futures losses at maturity. Yet the firm faces an acute crisis. What is the mechanism, and what risk does it illustrate?

- A. Basis risk between crude and heating oil destroyed the hedge's correlation, producing permanent economic loss
- B. Daily variation margin on the marked-to-market futures losses must be paid in cash now, while the offsetting gain on the physical commitment is realized only over the next 10 years — a funding liquidity squeeze (the Metallgesellschaft problem)
- C. Counterparty default on the exchange-traded futures left the firm unhedged
- D. The firm lost the upside on the physical commitment, reducing expected profit

**Answer: B**

This is the Metallgesellschaft scenario. The question asks for the *mechanism* of the crisis and the risk it illustrates, given that the economic hedge is sound.

1. Futures are **marked to market daily**. When crude falls 35%, the long futures show large losses *now* and the firm must post **variation margin in cash immediately**.
2. The offsetting gain — buying physical crude cheaper — is realized only *gradually over the 10-year delivery schedule*. So cash outflows are front-loaded and cash inflows are back-loaded.
3. This timing mismatch can exhaust the firm's liquidity before maturity even though the position is economically hedged → **funding liquidity risk**, the most immediate danger.

That is B.

- A invokes basis risk; the stem states the economic hedge is sound, so correlation/basis is not the issue.
- C invokes counterparty default on *exchange-traded* futures, which are margined and nearly free of counterparty risk — the opposite of the problem.
- D describes giving up upside, which is a feature of any forward/futures hedge and not the cause of the acute crisis.

📌 Exam tip: a hedge can be economically correct yet kill the firm via **funding liquidity** when the hedge is marked daily but the underlying pays off later. The keyword cluster "stack-and-roll / margin calls / long-dated commitment" should immediately flag Metallgesellschaft and funding liquidity risk.

**Q4.** A manufacturer hedges its forecast jet-fuel cost with crude oil futures (the cleanest liquid contract available). Over the hedge period, jet-fuel prices rise 12% but crude rises only 8%, so the futures gain does not fully offset the higher fuel bill. Separately, the firm's CFO notes that even though the economic hedge reduced cash-flow risk, reported quarterly earnings became *more* volatile. Which pair of effects is being described?

- A. Basis risk (grade mismatch between crude and jet fuel) and accounting earnings volatility from marking the derivative to market while the underlying is on accrual
- B. Funding liquidity risk and translation risk
- C. Rollover risk and adverse selection
- D. Counterparty credit risk and economic risk

**Answer: A**

The question asks you to name two separate effects: the imperfect offset, and the rise in reported earnings volatility despite a sound economic hedge.

1. The hedge instrument (crude) does not track the exposure (jet fuel) perfectly — different grade — so the offset is incomplete. That is **basis risk** (the hedge instrument differs in grade, location, maturity, or index from the exposure).
2. The derivative is **marked to market through earnings** each quarter, while the underlying fuel cost hits the P&L only when incurred (accrual). The mismatch in accounting timing produces *reported* earnings volatility even though the economic hedge is sound.

That pair is A.

- B names funding liquidity and translation risk — neither describes a grade mismatch or an accounting-timing effect.
- C names rollover risk (a maturity-mismatch issue, not present here) and adverse selection (unrelated).
- D names counterparty credit risk (futures are margined) and economic risk (a long-run competitive concept) — neither fits.

📌 Exam tip: imperfect tracking of a hedge = basis risk. And remember the curriculum's warning: a hedge that lowers economic risk can still *raise reported earnings volatility* because the derivative marks to market while the hedged item accrues — do not equate "earnings got more volatile" with "the hedge failed."

**Q5.** A board approves a risk appetite statement specifying a maximum one-year earnings-at-risk of $200 million and a minimum CET1 ratio of 11%. The firm's maximum absorbable loss before breaching regulatory minimums is estimated at $350 million, and its current measured exposure implies $180 million of earnings-at-risk. Match the three figures to the correct concept.

- A. $200m = risk capacity; $350m = risk appetite; $180m = risk profile
- B. $200m = risk appetite; $350m = risk capacity; $180m = risk profile
- C. $200m = risk profile; $350m = risk appetite; $180m = risk capacity
- D. $200m = risk appetite; $350m = risk profile; $180m = risk capacity

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to assign three numbers to risk appetite, capacity, and profile.

1. $200m is the amount of risk the board is **willing** to take in pursuit of objectives (a board-set limit in the appetite statement) → **risk appetite**.
2. $350m is the **maximum** the firm *can* absorb given capital before breaching minimums → **risk capacity**.
3. $180m is the risk **actually being taken right now** (current measured exposure) → **risk profile**.

The correct ordering — appetite $200m, capacity $350m, profile $180m — is B. Note the sensible relationship: profile ($180m) ≤ appetite ($200m) ≤ capacity ($350m), exactly as the curriculum requires (appetite sits within capacity; the risk function keeps the profile within appetite).

- A swaps appetite and capacity.
- C and D scramble the labels (e.g., calling the board's chosen $200m a "profile," or the live $180m exposure "capacity").

📌 Exam tip: appetite = *willing* to take (board choice); capacity = *can* absorb (the hard ceiling); profile = *actually* taking now. The ordering profile ≤ appetite ≤ capacity is the quick sanity check — if your assignment violates it, you mislabeled.

**Q6.** A treasurer must choose a single instrument to manage a forecast input-cost exposure where (a) the firm wants to retain the benefit if prices fall, (b) is willing to pay upfront for that asymmetry, and (c) needs no multi-period cash-flow transformation. A second desk must convert a stream of floating-rate interest payments into fixed with no upfront premium. Which instrument pairing is correct?

- A. Treasurer: forward; Desk: interest rate cap
- B. Treasurer: option (cap); Desk: interest rate swap
- C. Treasurer: interest rate swap; Desk: forward
- D. Treasurer: option (cap); Desk: forward rate agreement on a single period

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to match two needs to instruments by payoff shape, cost, and tenor.

1. Treasurer's needs: keep the favorable side (price falls), willing to pay upfront, single exposure. That is an **asymmetric payoff with an upfront premium** = an **option (cap)**, which insures against adverse moves while keeping the upside.
2. Second desk: transform a *multi-period* floating stream into fixed, with **no upfront cost**. That is the textbook **interest rate swap** — linear, symmetric, multi-period, no premium.

That pairing is B.

- A gives the treasurer a forward, which removes *both* upside and downside — contradicting requirement (a) to keep the favorable side.
- C gives the treasurer a swap (no upside retention, multi-period — wrong on both counts) and the desk a forward (single period — cannot convert a *stream*).
- D correctly gives the treasurer a cap but gives the desk a single-period FRA, which cannot transform a multi-period floating *stream* into fixed (a swap is the multi-period instrument).

📌 Exam tip: "keep the upside, pay a premium" = option/cap (asymmetric). "Transform a multi-period cash-flow profile, no premium" = swap (symmetric, linear, multi-period). Forwards/FRAs lock a single price/rate symmetrically and give up the favorable side.

**Q7.** A firm's market risk policy sets a desk VaR limit, but over the past year the limit has been breached eight times, each time waived by the desk head with informal approval. Risk management argues this is a governance failure, not merely a measurement issue. Which statement best captures the curriculum's view of what has gone wrong?

- A. The VaR limit was set too low; raising it would resolve the problem
- B. A limit that is routinely waived is not a limit; effective limit regimes require clear ownership, monitoring, and escalation for breaches
- C. VaR is the wrong measure; the desk should have used expected shortfall, which cannot be breached
- D. Breaches are acceptable because derivatives mark to market, so the limit is only an accounting construct

**Answer: B**

The question asks what the *governance* defect is when a limit is repeatedly waived.

1. The curriculum states risk limits "convert appetite into enforceable constraints" and that good limit regimes have **clear ownership, monitoring, and escalation for breaches**.
2. It states directly: "a limit that is routinely waived is not a limit." Eight informal waivers in a year mean the constraint is not binding — the framework has failed, regardless of the VaR number itself.

That is B.

- A treats it as a calibration problem; the issue is enforcement/escalation, not the level. Raising the limit to avoid breaches would just hide the appetite breach.
- C claims expected shortfall "cannot be breached" — false; ES is a risk measure with limits that can also be breached, and switching measures does not fix a waiver culture.
- D wrongly dismisses limits as "only an accounting construct"; limits are the transmission of risk appetite to the desk, fully economic.

📌 Exam tip: limit-framework questions hinge on *enforcement* — ownership, monitoring, escalation. The signature phrase "a limit that is routinely waived is not a limit" flags that the defect is governance/incentives, not the choice of risk metric or its calibration.
