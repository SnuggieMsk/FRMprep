**Q1.** Bank X has five derivative trades with counterparty Y, with current values *to Bank X* of +$14M, −$9M, +$7M, −$3M, and +$5M, all under a single ISDA master agreement with close-out netting. If Y defaults today, what are Bank X's credit exposures *with* and *without* enforceable netting, respectively?

- A. $14M with netting; $26M without
- B. $26M with netting; $14M without
- C. $14M with netting; $14M without
- D. $12M with netting; $26M without

**Answer: A**

Restate the ask: compute current credit exposure under close-out netting (net the whole portfolio, floored at zero) versus no netting (only positive-value trades count).

1. **With netting:** sum all values, floor at zero: $$\max(14 - 9 + 7 - 3 + 5, \, 0) = \max(14, 0) = \$14M$$.
2. **Without netting:** on default, X collects only the positive-value trades but must still pay the negative ones, so exposure = sum of the *positive* values only: $$14 + 7 + 5 = \$26M$$.

Why A is correct: netting nets to $14M; without netting the negative trades cannot offset, leaving $26M of positive-value exposure.

Distractor traps:
- B swaps the two figures.
- C wrongly applies netting to both — without netting the −$9M and −$3M cannot reduce exposure.
- D miscomputes the net sum (14 − 9 + 7 − 3 + 5 = 14, not 12).

📌 Exam tip: With netting, exposure = max(Σ all MTM, 0). Without netting, exposure = Σ (positive MTM only) — you lose the in-the-money trades but still owe the out-of-the-money ones. Netting always reduces (never increases) exposure.

**Q2.** A CSA between two dealers specifies a $5M threshold, a $1M independent amount, and a $0.5M minimum transfer amount. Party A's portfolio with Party B currently has a value of +$18M to Party B (i.e., −$18M to Party A). How much collateral must Party A post to Party B (assume cash collateral, no haircut, and that the previous posted balance was zero)?

- A. $18M
- B. $13M
- C. $14M
- D. $12M

**Answer: C**

Restate the ask: compute the collateral Party A (the out-of-the-money party) must post given a threshold, an independent amount, and a minimum transfer amount.

1. Mark-to-market exposure that B has to A = $18M.
2. The **threshold** is uncollateralized exposure allowed before collateral is required: required variation collateral = exposure − threshold = $18M − $5M = $13M.
3. The **independent amount** is extra collateral posted *regardless* of MTM (like initial margin), so add it: $13M + $1M = **$14M**.
4. The required transfer ($14M) exceeds the **minimum transfer amount** ($0.5M), so the full call is made.

Why C is correct: $18M − $5M threshold + $1M independent amount = $14M.

Distractor traps:
- A ignores both the threshold and adds nothing — full $18M is not required because the first $5M is uncollateralized.
- B applies the threshold but forgets the independent amount ($13M).
- D subtracts the independent amount instead of adding it ($13M − $1M), misreading its direction.

📌 Exam tip: Collateral = (exposure − threshold) + independent amount, provided this exceeds the minimum transfer amount. Threshold *reduces* the call; the independent amount *adds* to it. Haircuts (if any) increase the amount of non-cash collateral needed.

**Q3.** Under **novation** by a central counterparty (CCP) on an exchange:

- A. The original counterparties remain liable to each other, with the clearing house acting only as a guarantor.
- B. The original contract is replaced by two new contracts — the CCP becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer — so members face only the CCP.
- C. Offsetting positions are netted multilaterally among clearing-ring members without any contract substitution.
- D. The buyer's obligations are transferred to a bankruptcy-remote SPV.

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: define novation as performed by a CCP.

1. **Novation** legally *tears up* the original bilateral contract and substitutes **two** new contracts.
2. The CCP interposes itself: it becomes the **buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer**, so each member's only counterparty is the CCP.

Why B is correct: that is the precise definition of complete clearing via novation.

Distractor traps:
- A describes a mere *guarantee* — but novation actually replaces the original contract; the original parties no longer face each other.
- C describes *clearing rings* (an earlier, partial stage), not novation by a CCP.
- D confuses novation with an SPV structure used in OTC markets.

📌 Exam tip: Novation = original contract replaced by two CCP contracts; members face only the clearing house. This (plus margining, netting, and the default fund) is how exchanges remove counterparty risk.

**Q4.** During 2008, monoline insurers that had sold credit protection (e.g., on structured products) failed as counterparties. The mechanism most often cited is:

- A. They posted excessive collateral up front, draining liquidity before any losses occurred.
- B. Their exposures were fully netted under ISDA agreements, which paradoxically increased risk.
- C. They posted little or no collateral while AAA-rated; when structured-product losses triggered downgrades, ratings-based collateral triggers forced huge calls they could not meet — so the protection failed exactly when it was needed.
- D. Mandatory central clearing forced them to exit the market, defaulting on open trades.

**Answer: C**

Restate the ask: identify the monoline failure mechanism.

1. Monolines were **highly rated (AAA)** and therefore posted **little or no collateral** against the protection they sold.
2. As structured-product losses mounted, they were **downgraded**.
3. Their collateral agreements had **ratings-based triggers**: a downgrade forced **massive collateral calls** precisely when they were weakest — a cliff-edge, wrong-way risk.
4. Unable to meet the calls, they failed, so protection buyers' "hedge" was worthless **exactly when it was needed**.

Why C is correct: it captures the no-collateral-while-AAA → downgrade → collateral-call death spiral.

Distractor traps:
- A reverses reality — they posted *little* collateral, not excessive amounts.
- B invents a netting-increases-risk paradox; netting reduces exposure.
- D is anachronistic — central clearing mandates came *after* the crisis and did not cause monoline failures.

📌 Exam tip: The monoline lesson = ratings-based collateral triggers create wrong-way, cliff-edge risk: a counterparty posting nothing while AAA must suddenly post huge collateral right when downgraded and least able to pay. Protection fails when most valuable.

**Q5.** Which of the following was **NOT** part of the 2009 G20 post-crisis reforms of OTC derivatives markets (implemented via Dodd-Frank and EMIR)?

- A. Mandatory central clearing of standardized OTC derivatives through CCPs.
- B. Reporting of all OTC derivatives to trade repositories.
- C. A requirement that all OTC derivatives be converted into exchange-traded futures, eliminating bespoke OTC contracts.
- D. Higher capital and bilateral margin requirements for derivatives that remain non-centrally cleared.

**Answer: C**

Restate the ask: identify the statement that is *not* a real post-crisis reform.

1. Real reforms: (A) mandatory central clearing of standardized OTC derivatives; (B) trade reporting to repositories; (D) higher capital/margin on non-cleared trades; plus trading standardized contracts on **swap execution facilities (SEFs)** where appropriate.
2. **C is false:** bespoke OTC contracts still exist. The reforms pushed *standardized* contracts toward clearing and electronic platforms, but did **not** force conversion of everything into exchange-traded futures.

Why C is correct (as the NOT): no rule mandated converting all OTC derivatives into futures.

Distractor traps:
- A, B, and D are all genuine G20/Dodd-Frank/EMIR reforms — choosing any of them misreads the "NOT."

📌 Exam tip: The four pillars are (1) clearing of standardized OTC derivatives, (2) trade reporting, (3) SEF trading where appropriate, (4) higher capital/margin on non-cleared trades. Customized OTC contracts survive — nothing forced them into futures.

**Q6.** A clearing house sets initial margin to cover potential losses over the close-out period at a high confidence level, and collects variation margin daily. Which statement is most accurate?

- A. Initial margin is driven mainly by the underlying's volatility and the close-out horizon; variation margin settles daily mark-to-market gains and losses, resetting each contract's value to zero so exposure cannot accumulate.
- B. Variation margin is posted once at inception; initial margin is paid daily by the losing side.
- C. Both initial and variation margin are fixed amounts unrelated to the underlying's volatility.
- D. Variation margin is collected only from non-clearing members, while clearing members post only initial margin.

**Answer: A**

Restate the ask: distinguish initial margin (IM) from variation margin (VM) and state their determinants.

1. **Initial margin** is posted when the position opens, sized to cover potential losses over the **close-out period** at a high confidence level — so it is driven by the **volatility of the underlying** and the close-out horizon.
2. **Variation margin** is the **daily** (or intraday) settlement of mark-to-market gains and losses: losers pay, winners receive, which **resets each contract's value to zero daily**, preventing exposure from accumulating.

Why A is correct: it correctly pairs IM (volatility/horizon driven, posted at open) and VM (daily MTM settlement that resets value to zero).

Distractor traps:
- B reverses the two: VM is daily (not once at inception); IM is posted at the open (not daily by the loser).
- C wrongly claims both are volatility-independent — IM is explicitly volatility-driven.
- D garbles the member structure: VM applies to all positions; non-members clear *through* members.

📌 Exam tip: IM = posted at open, sized by volatility × close-out horizon (covers gap risk). VM = daily MTM cash settlement that zeroes out accumulated exposure. Netting reduces both.

**Q7.** A dealer wishes to give a particular OTC counterparty priority over its other creditors by isolating certain transactions and collateral in a bankruptcy-remote entity. This describes a ______, whose principal residual risk is ______.

- A. derivative product company (DPC); that its rating is tied to the parent and it is wound down if the parent fails
- B. special purpose vehicle (SPV); legal risk, since courts may consolidate the SPV with the sponsor
- C. central counterparty (CCP); that novation eliminates all counterparty risk
- D. monoline insurer; that it posts too much collateral

**Answer: B**

Restate the ask: identify the structure that isolates transactions/collateral in a bankruptcy-remote entity to give one counterparty priority, and its key residual risk.

1. A **special purpose vehicle (SPV)** is a bankruptcy-remote entity that holds collateral/transactions so a chosen counterparty ranks ahead of the sponsor's other creditors.
2. Its principal residual risk is **legal risk**: a court may **consolidate** the SPV with the sponsor (piercing bankruptcy-remoteness), defeating the priority — it *transforms* counterparty risk into legal risk rather than eliminating it.

Why B is correct: SPV = bankruptcy-remote priority vehicle; main residual risk is legal/consolidation risk.

Distractor traps:
- A describes a **DPC** (a separately capitalized AAA dealer subsidiary) — a different structure; the prompt's "isolate collateral for priority" is the SPV.
- C is a CCP, which manages risk via novation/margin, not by giving one counterparty priority — and novation does not "eliminate all" risk.
- D is a monoline, an insurer selling protection, not a bankruptcy-remote holding vehicle.

📌 Exam tip: SPV = bankruptcy-remote, gives a counterparty priority over the sponsor's creditors → residual *legal/consolidation* risk. DPC = capitalized AAA subsidiary → rating tied to parent. Monoline = AAA protection seller → ratings-trigger cliff risk. Know which is which.
