# 2. Insurance Companies and Pension Plans

GARP tests product definitions (life vs. P&C vs. health), two calculations — breakeven premiums from a mortality table and the P&C ratio chain — plus adverse selection vs. moral hazard, insurer regulation vs. bank regulation, and DB vs. DC pensions.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the key features of the major life insurance products.
- Describe property & casualty and health insurance.
- Describe a mortality table and use it to calculate the premium on a term-life policy.
- Distinguish adverse selection from moral hazard and describe mitigants for each.
- Calculate and interpret the loss ratio, expense ratio, combined ratio, combined ratio after dividends, and operating ratio of a P&C insurer.
- Describe CAT bonds.
- Describe the capital requirements and regulation of insurers, and contrast the insurance guaranty system with bank deposit insurance.
- Distinguish mutual from stock insurance companies.
- Compare defined benefit and defined contribution pension plans and identify who bears the risk in each.

## Categories of insurance

### Life insurance

| Product | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Term life** | Pays only if death occurs during a fixed term (e.g., 10 years); pure mortality protection, no investment component; cheapest |
| **Whole life** | Coverage for entire life; level premiums overpay early and underpay late, building a **cash surrender value**; tax-advantaged investment component |
| **Variable life** | Whole life where the policyholder chooses how the cash value is invested; payout varies with investment performance, usually with a guaranteed minimum |
| **Universal life** | Flexible premiums above a minimum; insurer credits returns on the cash value (guaranteed floor plus excess) |
| **Endowment** | Pays at the earlier of death or the end of the term — pays even if the insured survives |
| **Annuities** | The reverse of life insurance: a lump sum is converted into income for life; the insurer loses if the annuitant lives *longer* than expected (longevity risk) |

Life insurers face **mortality risk** (policyholders die earlier than expected — hurts life policies) and **longevity risk** (they live longer — hurts annuities). The two partially offset each other, a natural hedge.

### Property & casualty and health

- **P&C**: property (fire, theft, flood) and casualty/liability (auto, malpractice). Policies are typically one-year renewable; key risk is **catastrophe (CAT) risk** — losses from one event (hurricane, earthquake) are highly correlated across policies, so the law of large numbers fails.
- **Health**: medical costs. In the US (post-ACA), premiums cannot be raised because an individual's health deteriorates; like P&C in contract length, like life in that future premiums are constrained.

## Mortality tables and premium calculation

A mortality table gives, for each age, the probability of dying within one year ($$q_x$$), the probability of surviving to that age, and remaining life expectancy. Conditional probabilities chain: the probability that a person now aged $$x$$ dies in the *second* year is $$(1-q_x)\,q_{x+1}$$.

### Worked example: breakeven premium on a 2-year term policy

A woman aged 60 buys a 2-year, $100,000 term policy. From the mortality table: $$q_{60} = 0.005$$, $$q_{61} = 0.006$$. Payouts occur at the end of the year of death; premiums are paid at the start of each year (the second only if she is alive); the discount rate is 4% (annual compounding).

PV of expected payouts:

$$
\text{PV} = \frac{0.005 \times 100{,}000}{1.04} + \frac{(0.995)(0.006) \times 100{,}000}{1.04^2} = 480.77 + 551.96 = 1{,}032.73
$$

PV of premiums $$X$$ (paid at $$t=0$$ and, with probability 0.995, at $$t=1$$):

$$
X\left(1 + \frac{0.995}{1.04}\right) = 1.9567X
$$

Breakeven: $$X = 1{,}032.73 / 1.9567 \approx \$527.78$$ per year. Actual premiums exceed breakeven to cover expenses and profit.

{: .tip }
Always multiply the year-2 death probability by the year-1 survival probability, and remember the second premium is contingent on survival. Both adjustments use the same factor $$(1-q_{60})$$.

## Adverse selection vs. moral hazard

| | Adverse selection | Moral hazard |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | **Before** the contract | **After** the contract |
| Problem | High-risk individuals are most eager to buy; uniform pricing attracts a worse-than-average pool | Being insured changes behavior — less care, more risk-taking |
| Insurance example | Smokers and the seriously ill disproportionately buy life insurance | A driver with full coverage parks carelessly; an insured homeowner skips smoke detectors |
| Mitigants | Underwriting: medical exams, questionnaires, risk-based pricing | Deductibles, coinsurance, policy limits, no-claims discounts |

## P&C ratios

For a P&C insurer (all as a percentage of premiums earned):

- **Loss ratio** = losses incurred / premiums
- **Expense ratio** = expenses (selling, administration) / premiums
- **Combined ratio** = loss ratio + expense ratio
- **Combined ratio after dividends** = combined ratio + policyholder dividends
- **Operating ratio** = combined ratio after dividends − investment income ratio

### Worked example

Premiums $100M; losses $72M; expenses $28M; dividends to policyholders $2M; investment income $8M.

| Ratio | Calculation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Loss ratio | 72 / 100 | 72% |
| Expense ratio | 28 / 100 | 28% |
| Combined ratio | 72% + 28% | 100% |
| Combined after dividends | 100% + 2% | 102% |
| Operating ratio | 102% − 8% | 94% |

Underwriting alone breaks even (100%) and loses money after dividends (102%), but investment income on the **float** (premiums held before claims are paid) makes the business profitable overall (94% < 100%).

{: .warning }
Exam trap: a combined ratio above 100% does *not* mean the insurer is unprofitable — subtract investment income to get the operating ratio first. Also watch the sign conventions: dividends are *added*, investment income is *subtracted*.

## CAT bonds

A **catastrophe bond** lets a P&C insurer transfer extreme tail risk to capital markets: the bond pays a high coupon, but interest and/or principal is forgiven if a defined catastrophe loss exceeds a trigger. Investors like CAT bonds because catastrophe losses have low correlation with financial markets — good diversification. For the insurer they work as an alternative to reinsurance.

## Regulation and capital

- In the US, insurers are regulated by the **states** (coordinated by the NAIC), not federally — unlike banks. In Europe, **Solvency II** sets capital at two levels: a minimum capital requirement (MCR) and a higher solvency capital requirement (SCR), with supervisory intervention between them.
- Solvency capital must absorb unexpected underwriting and investment losses; life insurers also hold policy reserves (actuarial liabilities) against future payouts.
- **Guaranty system vs. deposit insurance**: when an insurer fails, state guaranty associations cover policyholders (up to limits) by levying **post-insolvency assessments on the surviving insurers** in that state. The FDIC, by contrast, runs a **permanent, pre-funded** fund financed by ongoing bank premiums. Insurance failures are typically slower-moving (no "runs"), which is why an after-the-fact levy is workable.

## Mutual vs. stock insurance companies

- **Mutual company**: owned by its policyholders; no shareholders; profits accrue to policyholders (dividends/lower premiums). No conflict between policyholders and owners, but limited access to capital.
- **Stock company**: owned by shareholders; profits to shareholders; easier capital raising but a potential owner-policyholder conflict. Many mutuals have **demutualized** to access equity markets.

## Pension plans: DB vs. DC

| | Defined benefit (DB) | Defined contribution (DC) |
|---|---|---|
| Promise | Formula-based retirement income (e.g., 2% × years of service × final salary) | Only the contributions; payout = contributions + investment performance |
| Investment risk | **Employer** (must fund any deficit) | **Employee** |
| Longevity risk | Employer (pays for life) | Employee (may outlive savings) |
| Portability | Poor | Good — account moves with the employee |
| Balance-sheet impact | Underfunded plans are a corporate liability | None beyond contributions |

DB plans can become severely underfunded when asset returns are low or longevity rises; the trend is decisively toward DC.

## Quick quiz

1. Using $$q_{70} = 0.02$$ and $$q_{71} = 0.022$$, the probability that a person aged 70 dies during the *second* year is closest to:
   - A. 0.0220
   - B. 0.0216
   - C. 0.0200
   - D. 0.0420

2. A P&C insurer has a loss ratio of 75%, an expense ratio of 27%, policyholder dividends of 1% of premiums, and investment income of 9% of premiums. Its operating ratio is:
   - A. 94%
   - B. 102%
   - C. 103%
   - D. 112%

3. Requiring a medical exam before issuing a life policy primarily mitigates:
   - A. Moral hazard
   - B. Adverse selection
   - C. Longevity risk
   - D. Catastrophe risk

4. In a defined contribution pension plan, the investment and longevity risks are borne by:
   - A. The employer
   - B. The employee
   - C. The guaranty association
   - D. Shared equally by contract

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — survive year 1 then die: (1 − 0.02) × 0.022 = 0.98 × 0.022 = 0.02156 ≈ 0.0216.
2. **A** — combined = 75% + 27% = 102%; after dividends = 103%; operating = 103% − 9% = 94%.
3. **B** — underwriting screens out (or reprices) high-risk applicants *before* the contract; moral hazard is post-contract behavior.
4. **B** — the employer's obligation ends with the contribution; the employee bears market and longevity risk.
</details>
