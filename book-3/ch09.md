# 9. Foreign Exchange Markets

GARP tests quote conventions, cross rates and triangular arbitrage, the three FX risks of a multinational and how each is hedged, and — most heavily — covered interest rate parity calculations and the arbitrage when it fails.

## Learning objectives

- Explain FX quote conventions: base vs quote currency, direct vs indirect quotes, bid-ask spreads, and pips.
- Compare the spot, outright forward, FX swap, and futures markets for currency.
- Calculate cross rates and identify triangular arbitrage when quotes are inconsistent.
- Distinguish transaction, translation, and economic risk for a multinational and describe how each is hedged.
- Distinguish nominal from real exchange rates and state purchasing power parity.
- Explain and apply covered interest rate parity; construct the arbitrage when the market forward violates it.
- Explain uncovered interest rate parity and why the carry trade exploits its empirical failure.

## Quote conventions

A quote EUR/USD = 1.1000 means 1 unit of the **base currency** (EUR, listed first) costs 1.1000 units of the **quote currency** (USD). The price is always expressed in the quote currency.

- **Direct quote:** domestic currency per unit of foreign currency (a US investor quoting USD per EUR).
- **Indirect quote:** foreign currency per unit of domestic currency (the reciprocal).
- **Bid-ask:** the dealer buys the base currency at the bid and sells it at the (higher) ask. A customer buying EUR pays the ask.
- **Pip:** the smallest standard price increment, usually the 4th decimal (0.0001) for most pairs, the 2nd decimal for JPY pairs. A move from 1.1000 to 1.1025 is 25 pips.

{: .note }
Forwards are often quoted as **forward points** (pips) to add to or subtract from spot: spot 1.1000 plus 30 forward points gives an outright forward of 1.1030.

## Market instruments

| Instrument | Description | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Spot** | Exchange of currencies, usually settling T+2 | Immediate needs |
| **Outright forward** | Custom OTC agreement to exchange at a set rate on a future date | Hedging a single future cash flow |
| **FX swap** | Spot exchange combined with a reverse forward exchange | Rolling hedges, short-term funding in another currency |
| **Futures** | Exchange-traded, standardized, daily settlement via CCP | Hedging/speculation with no counterparty credit exposure |

An FX swap is a single contract with two legs; it is the most heavily traded FX instrument and is economically a collateralized borrowing of one currency against another.

## Cross rates and triangular arbitrage

Most currencies are quoted against the USD; a **cross rate** between two non-USD currencies is implied by their USD quotes. If USD/GBP (USD per £) is 1.2500 and USD/EUR (USD per €) is 1.1000, the implied cross is

$$\text{EUR per GBP} = \frac{1.2500}{1.1000} = 1.1364$$

If a dealer quotes a cross rate different from the implied one, **triangular arbitrage** — converting through three currencies and back — locks in a riskless profit (see Worked example 2).

## FX risk for a multinational

| Risk | What it is | How it is hedged |
|---|---|---|
| **Transaction risk** | Known future foreign-currency cash flows (receivables, payables) change domestic value with FX moves | Forwards, futures, options, money-market hedges |
| **Translation risk** | Accounting exposure: foreign assets and liabilities are translated into the home currency for consolidated statements | Finance foreign assets with liabilities in the **same currency** so gains and losses offset |
| **Economic risk** | Long-run competitive exposure: FX moves change future revenues and costs even with no booked transactions | Operational diversification of production, sourcing, and sales across currencies |

{: .important }
Hedging translation risk with derivatives is **speculative** if the foreign assets are not actually going to be sold: the derivative produces real cash gains/losses against a paper accounting exposure. The sound hedge is matched-currency financing.

## Nominal vs real exchange rates and PPP

The **nominal** rate is the quoted market rate. The **real** exchange rate adjusts for relative price levels: $$E_{real} = E_{nominal} \times P_{foreign}/P_{domestic}$$. **Purchasing power parity** says nominal rates adjust over time to offset inflation differentials, keeping the real rate constant — a useful long-run anchor, a poor short-run predictor.

## Covered interest rate parity (CIP)

With S and F quoted as **domestic per unit of foreign**, r the domestic rate, and the foreign rate continuously compounded:

$$F = S e^{(r - r_f)T}$$

Discrete-compounding version:

$$F = S\,\frac{(1+r)^T}{(1+r_f)^T}$$

Intuition: investing domestically must earn the same as converting to foreign currency, investing at the foreign rate, and locking in the conversion back via the forward. If not, riskless arbitrage exists — CIP is **enforced by arbitrage**.

{: .warning }
Direction trap: in $$F = Se^{(r-r_f)T}$$ the currency with the **higher** interest rate trades at a forward **discount** (it depreciates forward) — its rate advantage must be given back through the forward, or arbitrage exists. And keep the quote direction consistent (domestic per foreign, domestic rate first in the exponent); flip the quote and the formula inverts. Exams routinely offer the inverted answer as a distractor.

## Uncovered interest rate parity (UIP) and the carry trade

UIP replaces the forward with the **expected future spot**: $$E[S_T] = S e^{(r - r_f)T}$$. Nothing enforces it — there is no arbitrage in an expectation — and empirically high-rate currencies do *not* depreciate as much as UIP predicts. The **carry trade** exploits this: borrow the low-rate currency, invest in the high-rate currency, and earn the differential. It earns steadily in calm markets but suffers severe losses when the funding currency appreciates sharply ("up the stairs, down the elevator").

| Parity | Compares F or E[S] with spot | Enforced by arbitrage? |
|---|---|---|
| Covered (CIP) | Forward rate F | **Yes** — riskless round trip |
| Uncovered (UIP) | Expected future spot $$E[S_T]$$ | **No** — expectation only |

{: .tip }
For small rates and short horizons, the forward premium/discount on the foreign currency is approximately the interest differential: $$\frac{F-S}{S} \approx (r - r_f)T$$. This quick check catches sign errors before you commit to an answer.

### Worked example: covered interest parity arbitrage

Spot USD/GBP = 1.3000 (USD per £). The 1-year USD rate is 3% and the 1-year GBP rate is 5%, both continuously compounded. The no-arbitrage forward is

$$F = 1.3000\, e^{(0.03 - 0.05)\times 1} = 1.3000 \times e^{-0.02} = 1.3000 \times 0.9802 = 1.2743$$

GBP, the higher-rate currency, is at a forward discount. Suppose the market forward is instead **1.2900** — too high, so sell GBP forward and carry it:

1. Borrow $13,000,000 at 3% for one year.
2. Buy £10,000,000 spot at 1.3000; invest at 5% → grows to 10,000,000 × $$e^{0.05}$$ = 10,000,000 × 1.0513 = £10,513,000.
3. Sell £10,513,000 forward at 1.2900.

At maturity: forward delivery brings 10,513,000 × 1.2900 = $13,561,770. Repay the loan: 13,000,000 × $$e^{0.03}$$ = 13,000,000 × 1.0305 = $13,396,500.

Riskless profit = 13,561,770 − 13,396,500 = **$165,270**, consistent with (1.2900 − 1.2743) ≈ 0.0157 per pound carried.

### Worked example: cross rate and triangular arbitrage

USD/EUR = 1.1000 (USD per €) and USD/GBP = 1.2500 (USD per £). Implied cross: 1.2500 / 1.1000 = **1.1364 EUR per GBP**. A dealer quotes the cross at 1.1500 — GBP is too expensive in EUR terms, so *sell* GBP for EUR at the dealer's rate. Starting with €1,000,000:

1. EUR → USD: 1,000,000 × 1.1000 = $1,100,000.
2. USD → GBP: 1,100,000 / 1.2500 = £880,000.
3. GBP → EUR at the quoted cross: 880,000 × 1.1500 = €1,012,000.

Riskless profit = **€12,000** (1.2% — exactly the 1.1500/1.1364 misalignment). Real-world spreads and speed eliminate such gaps almost instantly.

## Quick quiz

1. Spot USD/EUR = 1.2000 (USD per €), the 6-month USD rate is 2% and the EUR rate is 4% (continuous). The 6-month no-arbitrage forward is closest to:
   - A. 1.1880
   - B. 1.2000
   - C. 1.2121
   - D. 1.2240

2. A US multinational owns a factory in Brazil that it has no intention of selling. The exposure of its consolidated balance sheet to BRL is best managed by:
   - A. Selling BRL forward each quarter
   - B. Buying BRL put options
   - C. Financing the factory with BRL-denominated debt
   - D. Doing nothing, since economic risk cannot be hedged

3. Under covered interest parity, the currency with the higher interest rate:
   - A. Trades at a forward premium
   - B. Trades at a forward discount
   - C. Must appreciate in the spot market
   - D. Has no determinate forward relationship

4. USD/EUR = 1.0800 (USD per €) and CHF/USD = 0.9000 (CHF per $). The implied CHF per EUR cross rate is closest to:
   - A. 0.8333
   - B. 0.9720
   - C. 1.2000
   - D. 1.9800

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **A** — $$F = 1.2000\,e^{(0.02-0.04)\times 0.5} = 1.2000 \times e^{-0.01} = 1.2000 \times 0.9900 = 1.1880$$; C is the inverted-formula distractor.
2. **C** — translation risk is hedged by matched-currency financing; derivative hedges of unsold assets are speculative.
3. **B** — the rate advantage is offset by forward depreciation, otherwise riskless arbitrage exists.
4. **B** — CHF per EUR = (USD per EUR) × (CHF per USD) = 1.0800 × 0.9000 = 0.9720.
