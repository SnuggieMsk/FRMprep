# 3. Fund Management

GARP tests the mutual fund / closed-end fund / ETF distinctions, NAV and fee arithmetic, the abusive trading practices, and — most heavily — hedge fund fee mechanics (high-water marks, hurdles) and the biases that inflate reported hedge fund performance.

## Learning objectives

- Differentiate open-end mutual funds, closed-end funds, and ETFs.
- Calculate the net asset value (NAV) of an open-end fund.
- Explain index funds and the sources of tracking error.
- Describe mutual fund fees (front-end load, back-end load, expense ratio) and their effect on returns.
- Describe the undesirable trading behaviors: late trading, market timing, front running, and directed brokerage.
- Explain the key differences between hedge funds and mutual funds.
- Calculate hedge fund fees, including incentive fees with hurdle rates, high-water marks, and clawbacks.
- Describe the role of prime brokers.
- Describe the main hedge fund strategies.
- Describe the biases in reported hedge fund performance.

## Open-end funds, closed-end funds, and ETFs

| | Open-end mutual fund | Closed-end fund | ETF |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shares outstanding | Variable — fund creates/redeems daily | Fixed after IPO | Variable — created/redeemed in large blocks by institutions |
| Trading | With the fund, once per day at 4 p.m. NAV | On an exchange, intraday | On an exchange, intraday |
| Price vs. NAV | Always exactly NAV | Can trade at a **premium or (usually) discount** to NAV | Stays very close to NAV — the creation/redemption arbitrage keeps it in line |
| Shorting / intraday limit orders | No | Yes | Yes |
| Holdings disclosure | Quarterly/semiannual | Periodic | Typically daily |

### NAV calculation

$$
\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}
$$

Example: a fund holds $250 million of securities and cash, owes $10 million (accrued fees and payables), and has 12 million shares: NAV = (250 − 10)/12 = **$20.00 per share**. All purchases and redemptions that day transact at $20.00.

## Index funds and tracking error

An **index fund** passively replicates an index by full replication or by sampling. **Tracking error** is the deviation of the fund's return from the index return (often quoted as the standard deviation of the return difference). Sources: fees and expenses, sampling rather than full replication, cash drag, transaction costs around index rebalancing, and dividend-timing differences.

## Mutual fund fees

- **Front-end load**: charged on purchase. A 5% load on a $10,000 investment puts only $9,500 to work — the fund must earn 5.26% before the investor breaks even.
- **Back-end load**: charged on redemption, often declining with holding period (deters early exit).
- **Expense ratio**: annual management plus operating (and 12b-1 marketing) costs as a percentage of assets — it compounds against the investor every year, so it is usually the most damaging fee over long horizons.

## Undesirable trading practices

| Practice | What it is | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Late trading** | Placing orders *after* the 4 p.m. cutoff but still receiving today's NAV — trading on post-close news | Illegal |
| **Market timing** | Rapid in-and-out trading exploiting **stale prices** in NAV (e.g., Asian closing prices in a US fund); dilutes long-term holders | Prohibited by fund rules |
| **Front running** | Trading for one's own account ahead of a known large fund order | Illegal |
| **Directed brokerage** | Fund directs trades to a broker in exchange for the broker pushing the fund's shares — a kickback arrangement | Banned |

## Hedge funds vs. mutual funds

Hedge funds are lightly regulated because they sell only to accredited/qualified investors. Compared with mutual funds they: are not required to offer daily redemption (impose **lock-up periods**, redemption gates, and notice periods); disclose little; can short, lever, concentrate, and use derivatives freely; and charge performance fees.

## Hedge fund fees: 2-and-20, hurdles, high-water marks

A typical structure is "**2 and 20**": a 2% annual management fee on assets plus a 20% **incentive fee** on profits. Refinements:

- **Hurdle rate**: incentive fee applies only to returns above a benchmark (e.g., SOFR or a fixed rate).
- **High-water mark (HWM)**: incentive fees are paid only on gains above the highest NAV on which fees have already been paid — losses must be recouped before new incentive fees accrue.
- **Clawback**: part of past incentive fees is held in escrow and returned to investors if later losses occur.

### Worked example: incentive fees with a high-water mark

A fund starts at $100M with a 20% incentive fee above a HWM (initially $100M). Ignore the management fee for clarity.

| Year | Gross return | Pre-fee AUM | HWM at start | Gain above HWM | Incentive fee | End AUM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +25% | 125.0 | 100.0 | 25.0 | 5.00 | 120.00 |
| 2 | −10% | 108.0 | 120.0 | 0 | 0 | 108.00 |
| 3 | +15% | 124.2 | 120.0 | 4.2 | 0.84 | 123.36 |

After year 1 the HWM rises to 120. In year 3 the fund earns a 16.2 gain, but only the 4.2 *above the old peak of 120* is fee-bearing. Total incentive fees: $5.84M; without a HWM they would have been 5.00 + 0 + 0.20 × 16.2 = $8.24M.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: the incentive fee is charged on the excess over the **high-water mark**, not on the full year's gain after a losing year. Also note the option-like asymmetry of "2 and 20": the manager shares gains but not losses, an incentive to add volatility — the HWM and clawbacks only partially offset this.

## Prime brokers

Large banks acting as **prime brokers** provide hedge funds with trade clearing and settlement, custody, margin financing (leverage), securities lending for short sales, and capital introduction. The prime broker takes collateral and monitors the fund's positions — it is often the first to see a fund in distress.

## Hedge fund strategies

| Strategy | One-line description |
|---|---|
| Long/short equity | Long undervalued stocks, short overvalued ones; net exposure can vary (equity-market-neutral targets zero beta) |
| Dedicated short | Net short equities, hunting overvalued or fraudulent companies |
| Distressed securities | Buy debt of bankrupt/near-bankrupt firms below recovery value; may seek control in restructuring |
| Merger arbitrage | After a deal is announced, buy the target (and short the acquirer in share deals); profits if the deal closes |
| Convertible arbitrage | Long underpriced convertible bonds, short the underlying equity hedge |
| Fixed income arbitrage | Exploit small mispricings between related rate instruments (e.g., on-the-run vs. off-the-run Treasuries), highly levered |
| Emerging markets | Debt and equity of developing economies |
| Global macro | Directional bets on macro variables — rates, FX, commodities — often levered |
| Managed futures | Systematic (often trend-following) trading of futures markets |

## Performance and biases in hedge fund data

Reported hedge fund index returns overstate the investable experience:

- **Measurement bias**: returns of illiquid assets are based on manager-marked (smoothed) valuations, understating volatility and correlation.
- **Backfill bias**: funds join databases voluntarily *after* good performance and import their favorable history.
- **Survivorship bias**: failed funds drop out of databases, so averages reflect only the winners.

## Quick quiz

1. A closed-end fund holds assets worth $360M with $24M of liabilities and 16M shares outstanding. Its shares trade at $18.90. The fund trades at:
   - A. NAV of $18.90 exactly, by construction
   - B. A 10% discount to its $21.00 NAV
   - C. A 10% premium to its $21.00 NAV
   - D. A 16% discount to its $22.50 NAV

2. A hedge fund charges a 20% incentive fee subject to a high-water mark of $125M. After a losing year its AUM is $110M; this year it gains 20% (pre-fee). The incentive fee is closest to:
   - A. $0
   - B. $1.4M
   - C. $4.4M
   - D. $26.4M

3. Accepting orders after the 4 p.m. close while still granting that day's NAV is:
   - A. Market timing — discouraged but legal
   - B. Late trading — illegal
   - C. Front running — legal if disclosed
   - D. Directed brokerage

4. Hedge fund databases that add a fund's pre-entry track record once it starts reporting suffer from:
   - A. Survivorship bias
   - B. Measurement bias
   - C. Backfill bias
   - D. Look-ahead bias

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — NAV = (360 − 24)/16 = $21.00; price $18.90 is 18.90/21.00 = 90% of NAV, a 10% discount (typical for closed-end funds).
2. **B** — pre-fee AUM = 110 × 1.20 = $132M; fee-bearing gain = 132 − 125 = $7M above the HWM; fee = 20% × 7 = $1.4M.
3. **B** — late trading lets the trader exploit post-close information and is illegal; market timing exploits stale prices but trades before the cutoff.
4. **C** — importing the (self-selected, favorable) pre-entry history is backfill bias; survivorship bias is the dropout of dead funds.
</details>
