# 20. Swaps

GARP tests two valuation views of an interest rate swap — a portfolio of two bonds and a portfolio of FRAs — plus the comparative-advantage story (and its flaw), currency swap mechanics, and where the credit risk sits. The bond-portfolio valuation with given zero rates is the workhorse calculation.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the mechanics of a plain vanilla interest rate swap and compute its cash flows; describe the role of confirmations and the ISDA Master Agreement.
- Explain the comparative advantage argument for swaps and evaluate its criticism.
- Value a plain vanilla interest rate swap as a portfolio of bonds and, equivalently, as a portfolio of FRAs.
- Explain the mechanics of a currency swap, compute its cash flows, and value it as two bonds linked by the spot exchange rate.
- Identify other swap types: basis, equity, amortizing, and swaptions.
- Describe the credit risk exposure in a swap position and how it differs between interest rate and currency swaps.

## Mechanics and documentation

In a plain vanilla interest rate swap, one party pays a **fixed** rate and receives a **floating** rate (historically LIBOR, now SOFR-based) on a **notional principal** that is never exchanged. Payments are netted each period; the floating rate applying to a period is set at the **start** of the period (or, for compounded SOFR, observed over it) and paid at the **end**. The economic terms of each trade are recorded in a **confirmation**, which sits under the **ISDA Master Agreement** governing netting, collateral, and default between the two parties. Dealers warehouse swaps and hedge residual exposure rather than waiting for matched offsetting counterparties.

A pay-fixed ("payer") swap gains when rates rise; a receive-fixed ("receiver") swap gains when rates fall. A swap converts a fixed-rate liability into floating (or vice versa), and likewise transforms asset income.

## Comparative advantage — and its criticism

Suppose A can borrow fixed at 4.0% or floating at SOFR − 0.1%, while B faces 5.2% fixed or SOFR + 0.6%. B pays 1.2% more fixed but only 0.7% more floating, so A has comparative advantage in **fixed**, B in **floating**. Total gain to be shared:

$$
\text{Gain} = 1.2\% - 0.7\% = 0.5\%
$$

Each borrows where it is relatively cheapest, then swaps into the exposure it wants (see worked example below).

{: .note }
The criticism: the fixed-rate spread (1.2%) is a **long-term, locked-in** credit spread, while the floating spread (0.7%) applies only until the **next refinancing/reset**. B's apparent "cheap" floating rate can be repriced upward if its credit deteriorates, so the 0.5% gain is partly illusory — it compensates for B bearing rollover/spread risk, not a free lunch from market inefficiency.

### Worked example: splitting the comparative-advantage gain

Using the rates above, A wants floating and B wants fixed; they share the 0.5% gain equally (no intermediary).

**Step 1 — borrow where each is relatively cheap.** A issues fixed at 4.0%; B issues floating at SOFR + 0.6%.

**Step 2 — swap.** B pays A 4.35% fixed; A pays B SOFR (on matching notionals).

**Step 3 — check net costs.** A: $$4.0\% + \text{SOFR} - 4.35\% = \text{SOFR} - 0.35\%$$ — saves 0.25% vs. its direct floating rate of SOFR − 0.1%. B: $$(\text{SOFR} + 0.6\%) + 4.35\% - \text{SOFR} = 4.95\%$$ — saves 0.25% vs. its direct fixed rate of 5.2%. With a bank intermediary taking 0.04%, each side would keep 0.23%.

## Valuing a swap as a bond portfolio

To the **receive-fixed** party, a swap is long a fixed-rate bond and short a floating-rate note:

$$
V_{\text{swap}} = B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}
$$

and to the **pay-fixed (payer)** party, $$V_{\text{swap}} = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$. Two facts make this fast:

- $$B_{\text{fixed}}$$ = PV of the remaining fixed coupons plus notional at maturity, discounted at current zero rates.
- $$B_{\text{float}}$$ **prices at par immediately after each reset**. Between resets, it equals the PV of (notional + the already-fixed next floating payment) discounted to the next payment date — all later floating cash flows are worth par at that date.

### Worked example: payer swap valued as a bond portfolio

A $100 million swap has 1.25 years remaining: the institution **pays 8% fixed** (semiannual) and receives 6-month floating. Continuously compounded zero rates are 10% (3 months), 10.5% (9 months), and 11% (15 months). The floating payment due in 3 months was fixed at 10.2% (semiannual) at the last reset.

**Step 1 — fixed bond.** Coupons of $4M at 0.25, 0.75, 1.25 years plus $100M notional at 1.25:

$$
B_{\text{fixed}} = 4e^{-0.10(0.25)} + 4e^{-0.105(0.75)} + 104e^{-0.11(1.25)}
$$

$$
B_{\text{fixed}} = 4(0.97531) + 4(0.92427) + 104(0.87153) = 3.9012 + 3.6971 + 90.6395 = \$98.238\text{M}
$$

**Step 2 — floating bond.** Worth (notional + known payment) discounted from the next reset: payment $$= 100 \times 0.102/2 = \$5.1$$M:

$$
B_{\text{float}} = 105.1\,e^{-0.10(0.25)} = 105.1(0.97531) = \$102.505\text{M}
$$

**Step 3 — payer swap value.**

$$
V = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}} = 102.505 - 98.238 = +\$4.27\text{M}
$$

The payer swap is in the money because rates (≈10–11%) sit well above the 8% fixed rate being paid.

## Valuing a swap as a portfolio of FRAs

Each exchange is an FRA: the floating payment is valued by **assuming forward rates are realized**. Compute the forward rate for each period from the zero curve, set each net cash flow as $$(\text{fixed} - \text{forward}) \times \tau \times L$$ (for the receiver), discount each at the matching zero rate, and sum. This gives **exactly the same value** as the bond approach. At initiation a swap is worth zero in total, but individual FRAs are not: with an upward-sloping curve, a receiver's early exchanges have positive value and later ones negative.

{: .warning }
When discounting the floating leg, do not project floating cash flows beyond the next payment — the next payment is **already fixed** at the last reset, and the FRN reverts to par right after it. Discounting "estimated" floating coupons to maturity, or valuing $$B_{\text{float}}$$ at par *between* resets (it is par only **at** reset), are the two classic errors. Also watch the sign: $$B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}$$ is the **receiver's** value; flipping the perspective flips the sign.

## Currency swaps

A fixed-for-fixed currency swap exchanges principal **and** interest in one currency for principal and interest in another. Unlike interest rate swaps, **principals are exchanged at initiation and at maturity** (at the same FX rate, set so each leg starts at par). Uses: transform a liability or investment from one currency to another; exploit comparative advantage across currency funding markets (the criticism here is weaker, since spreads partly reflect tax/market segmentation, but the swap creates FX-linked credit exposure).

Valuation as two bonds, for the party **receiving foreign** currency and paying dollars, with $$S_0$$ = spot (dollars per unit of foreign):

$$
V_{\text{swap}} = S_0 B_F - B_D
$$

where $$B_F$$ is the foreign-currency bond (in foreign units) and $$B_D$$ the dollar bond. A currency swap can also be valued as a portfolio of FX forwards, one per exchange date.

### Worked example: currency swap value

An institution receives 5% on ¥1,200M and pays 8% on $10M, with annual exchanges and 3 years remaining (principals exchanged at maturity). Flat continuously compounded rates: 4% in Japan, 9% in the US. Spot: ¥110 = $1.

**Step 1 — dollar bond.**

$$
B_D = 0.8e^{-0.09} + 0.8e^{-0.18} + 10.8e^{-0.27} = 0.7311 + 0.6682 + 8.2445 = \$9.644\text{M}
$$

**Step 2 — yen bond.**

$$
B_F = 60e^{-0.04} + 60e^{-0.08} + 1260e^{-0.12} = 57.647 + 55.387 + 1117.52 = ¥1230.55\text{M}
$$

**Step 3 — convert and net.** $$S_0 = 1/110$$ dollars per yen:

$$
V = \frac{1230.55}{110} - 9.644 = 11.187 - 9.644 = +\$1.543\text{M}
$$

## Other swaps and credit risk

- **Basis swap**: floating-for-floating on two different reference rates (e.g., SOFR vs. commercial paper) plus a spread.
- **Equity swap**: total return on an index exchanged for fixed or floating — equitizes cash without trading the stocks.
- **Amortizing/step-up swaps** vary the notional; **forward swaps** start later; **swaptions** are options to enter a swap (a payer swaption benefits from rising rates).
- **Credit risk**: a swap exposes a party only **when its value is positive** — exposure is the replacement cost, far smaller than notional, and mitigated by netting and collateral under the ISDA framework (and by CCP clearing for standardized swaps). Potential exposure on an interest rate swap peaks mid-life (value has had time to drift, payments remain); a **currency swap's exposure grows toward maturity** because the final principal exchange dominates — so currency swaps carry materially more credit risk than rate swaps of the same notional.

## Quick quiz

1. Immediately after a reset date (next floating rate just fixed and current payment made), the floating leg of a vanilla swap is worth:
   - A. Zero
   - B. The notional principal (par)
   - C. The PV of all projected floating coupons only
   - D. The fixed-leg value minus the swap value

2. For a counterparty that pays fixed in a $100M swap, current zero rates give $$B_{\text{fixed}} = \$102.0$$M and $$B_{\text{float}} = \$100.5$$M. The swap's value to this counterparty is:
   - A. +$1.5M
   - B. −$1.5M
   - C. +$2.0M
   - D. −$0.5M

3. Firm X borrows fixed at 6.0% or floating at SOFR + 0.2%; Firm Y at 7.0% fixed or SOFR + 0.6%. The total gain available from a swap is:
   - A. 1.0%
   - B. 0.4%
   - C. 0.6%
   - D. 1.4%

4. Compared with an interest rate swap of equal notional and maturity, a currency swap typically has:
   - A. Less credit risk, since principals are exchanged
   - B. Greater credit risk, concentrated late in the swap's life due to the principal exchange
   - C. Identical credit risk, since both are valued as bond portfolios
   - D. No credit risk if cleared bilaterally

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — just after reset, the floating-rate note reprices to par; between resets it is (notional + next known payment) discounted to the next payment date.
2. **B** — payer value $$= B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}} = 100.5 - 102.0 = -\$1.5$$M. (+$1.5M is the receiver's value — the sign-flip trap.)
3. **C** — fixed-rate difference 1.0% minus floating-rate difference 0.4% = 0.6% total gain to be shared.
4. **B** — the final exchange of principals means exposure builds toward maturity, unlike a rate swap whose exposure peaks mid-life and involves no principal.
