# 11. Commodity Forwards and Futures

GARP tests the cost-of-carry model extended for storage costs and convenience yield, contango vs backwardation (and the "normal" versions against *expected* spot), the lease rate for gold, and the qualitative characteristics of the major commodity markets.

## Learning objectives

- Explain how commodities differ from financial assets and how this changes forward pricing.
- Price forwards on investment commodities with storage costs (dollar and proportional).
- Explain why consumption commodities satisfy only an inequality, and define the convenience yield.
- Define the lease rate and price gold forwards using it.
- Define contango and backwardation; relate them to convenience yield; distinguish normal contango/backwardation relative to the expected future spot.
- Describe the characteristics of crude oil, natural gas, electricity, agricultural, and metals markets.
- Explain cross hedging and basis risk in commodity hedges.
- Describe weather derivatives (HDD/CDD contracts).

## How commodities differ from financial assets

Commodities incur **storage costs** (warehousing, insurance — effectively *negative income*), have **transport costs** that fragment prices regionally, exhibit **seasonality** in production or demand, often **cannot be shorted** easily (no lending market for soybeans), and are held mainly **for consumption**, not investment. The result: cost-of-carry holds with extra terms for investment commodities, and only as an upper bound for consumption commodities.

## Investment commodities with storage costs

Gold and silver are held by many investors purely as assets, so arbitrage works in both directions. With storage costs as a present value U (dollar costs), or as a proportional rate u (per year, like a negative dividend yield):

$$F_0 = (S_0 + U)e^{rT} \qquad \text{or} \qquad F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u)T}$$

## Consumption commodities and convenience yield

Holders of oil or copper inventory derive value from physically having it — keeping refineries running, avoiding stockouts. They will not sell inventory to exploit a "too low" futures price, so reverse cash-and-carry fails and only the upper bound binds:

$$F_0 \le S_0 e^{(r+u)T}$$

The **convenience yield** y is defined as whatever benefit-of-holding restores equality:

$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u-y)T}$$

A high convenience yield signals tight inventories and a market that fears shortage; it pushes futures *below* full carry and, if large enough, below spot.

{: .warning }
Sign trap: storage costs enter the exponent with a **plus** sign (they are negative income, raising the carry), while convenience yield enters with a **minus** (it is income-like, lowering the carry). Flipping either sign is the standard distractor — sanity-check that more storage cost means a *higher* forward and more convenience means a *lower* forward.

## The lease rate (gold)

Gold can be lent to borrowers (e.g., miners hedging) who pay the **lease rate** δ — the commodity's analog of a dividend yield. A holder who lends gold earns δ, so

$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-\delta)T}$$

Equivalently, the lease rate is the convenience yield net of storage costs implied by market prices. A cash-and-carry arbitrageur who cannot earn the lease rate faces a forward that looks "cheap" relative to own-storage carry.

## Contango, backwardation, and the "normal" versions

| Term | Comparison | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Contango** | Futures > current spot (upward-sloping term structure) | Full or positive net carry; ample inventories |
| **Backwardation** | Futures < current spot (downward-sloping) | Convenience yield exceeds r + u; tight inventories |
| **Normal contango** | Futures > **expected** future spot | Speculators net short earn a premium |
| **Normal backwardation** | Futures < **expected** future spot | Keynes: hedgers (producers) are net short, so long speculators demand a risk premium; futures drift *up* toward expected spot |

{: .important }
Contango/backwardation compare futures with the **current spot** (observable term-structure shape). Normal contango/backwardation compare futures with the **expected future spot** (unobservable, about risk premia). A market can be in contango and normal backwardation simultaneously.

## Commodity market characteristics

- **Crude oil**: storable, globally traded benchmark grades (WTI, Brent); deep futures markets; term structure driven by inventories and OPEC supply.
- **Natural gas**: storable but expensive to store; strongly **seasonal demand** (winter heating, summer cooling), so futures show seasonal peaks; delivery is a steady flow over a month.
- **Electricity**: essentially **non-storable**, so arbitrage pricing fails entirely; spot prices show extreme spikes (heat waves); day-ahead and real-time markets, peak vs off-peak contracts.
- **Agriculturals**: production is **harvest-seasonal**; prices peak pre-harvest and fall post-harvest; weather is the dominant risk; storability varies (grains store well, livestock doesn't).
- **Metals**: industrial metals (copper, aluminum) driven by global growth and inventories; precious metals (gold, silver) behave as investment assets with low storage cost relative to value.

## Cross hedging and basis risk

When no futures contract exists on the exposure itself, hedge with a correlated contract — the classic example is hedging **jet fuel** with **heating oil** (or crude) futures. The hedge leaves **basis risk**: the spread between jet fuel and heating oil can move. Commodity basis risk is larger than in financial futures because of grade, location, and timing mismatches; the optimal hedge ratio is set by the regression beta of spot exposure changes on futures price changes.

Sources of commodity basis risk:

- **Grade/quality**: WTI futures vs the heavier crude you actually buy.
- **Location**: futures deliver at a hub (Cushing, Henry Hub); your exposure is elsewhere, so transport spreads move the basis.
- **Timing**: hedge horizon rarely matches a delivery month, forcing rollovers at uncertain calendar spreads.

## Weather derivatives

Contracts pay off on temperature indices rather than prices: a month's **HDD** (heating degree days) accumulates max(0, 65°F − daily average temp); **CDD** (cooling degree days) accumulates max(0, daily average temp − 65°F). Energy companies use HDD/CDD futures and options (CME) to hedge volume risk — mild winters cut gas demand even if prices don't move. Payoff = tick size (e.g., $20) × index value.

{: .tip }
Memory anchor for the carry exponent: forward price grows with everything that **costs** the holder (financing r, storage u) and shrinks with everything that **benefits** the holder (income q, lease rate δ, convenience yield y). Every formula in this chapter and the last is the same skeleton $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(\text{costs} - \text{benefits})T}$$.

### Worked example: gold forward with storage costs

Gold spot is $2,400/oz; storage costs with a present value of $8/oz are paid for the year; r = 5% (continuous), T = 1. The no-arbitrage forward is

$$F_0 = (2400 + 8)e^{0.05\times1} = 2408 \times 1.0513 = 2531.53$$

Suppose the market futures price is **$2,560** — too high. Cash-and-carry:

1. Borrow $2,408 at 5%; buy 1 oz at $2,400 and prepay $8 storage.
2. Short the futures at $2,560.
3. At T: deliver the gold for $2,560; repay 2,408 × $$e^{0.05}$$ = 2,408 × 1.0513 = $2,531.53.

Riskless profit = 2,560.00 − 2,531.53 = **$28.47/oz**. If instead the futures were below $2,531.53, reverse cash-and-carry works for gold (an investment commodity, holders will lend/sell it) — but would *not* be enforceable for a consumption commodity.

### Worked example: implied convenience yield

Crude oil spot is $80.00; the 9-month futures price is $77.50; r = 4% and proportional storage cost u = 2% (both continuous), T = 0.75. From $$F_0 = S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}$$, solve

$$y = r + u - \frac{1}{T}\ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{S_0}\right) = 0.04 + 0.02 - \frac{1}{0.75}\ln\!\left(\frac{77.50}{80.00}\right)$$

Compute: 77.50/80.00 = 0.96875, and ln(0.96875) = −0.0317, so

$$y = 0.06 - \frac{-0.0317}{0.75} = 0.06 + 0.0423 = 0.1023$$

The implied convenience yield is **10.23%** — well above r + u, which is exactly why the market is in backwardation (futures $77.50 below spot $80). Inventories are valuable to hold.

## Quick quiz

1. Silver spot is $30.00/oz, storage costs with PV $0.45/oz, r = 6% (continuous), T = 1. The no-arbitrage forward is closest to:
   - A. $31.40
   - B. $31.86
   - C. $32.33
   - D. $32.79

2. A consumption commodity's futures price is well below $$S_0e^{(r+u)T}$$ yet no arbitrage occurs. The best explanation is:
   - A. Markets are inefficient
   - B. Inventory holders earn a convenience yield and will not sell stock to short the carry
   - C. Storage costs are negative
   - D. The risk-free rate is mismeasured

3. Normal backwardation means:
   - A. The futures price is below the current spot price
   - B. The futures curve slopes downward
   - C. The futures price is below the expected future spot price
   - D. The convenience yield is zero

4. Which commodity's spot market shows the most extreme price spikes, and why?
   - A. Gold, because of investment demand
   - B. Corn, because of harvest cycles
   - C. Crude oil, because of OPEC
   - D. Electricity, because it cannot be stored

1. **C** — $$F_0 = (30 + 0.45)e^{0.06} = 30.45 \times 1.0618 = 32.33$$.
2. **B** — convenience yield converts the pricing equality into the inequality $$F_0 \le S_0e^{(r+u)T}$$ for consumption commodities.
3. **C** — "normal" terms compare futures with the *expected* future spot (Keynes: net-short hedgers pay long speculators a risk premium).
4. **D** — non-storability removes the inventory buffer, so demand shocks hit prices directly.
