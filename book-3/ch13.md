# 13. Properties of Options

GARP tests the six pricing factors (and their signs for each option type), upper and lower bounds, put-call parity (European only!) and the American inequality, plus the early-exercise logic for American calls and puts. Expect at least one arbitrage-from-a-bound or parity question.

## Learning objectives

- Identify the six factors that affect an option's price and indicate the direction of each effect for European and American calls and puts.
- Identify, interpret, and compute upper and lower bounds for option prices on non-dividend-paying and dividend-paying stocks.
- Explain put-call parity and apply it to value European stock options, with and without dividends.
- Explain the put-call inequality for American options.
- Explain and assess potential rationales for early exercise of American calls and puts.

## The six factors

| Factor (increase) | European call $$c$$ | European put $$p$$ | American call $$C$$ | American put $$P$$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stock price $$S_0$$ | + | − | + | − |
| Strike price $$K$$ | − | + | − | + |
| Time to expiration $$T$$ | ? | ? | + | + |
| Volatility $$\sigma$$ | + | + | + | + |
| Risk-free rate $$r$$ | + | − | + | − |
| Dividends $$D$$ | − | + | − | + |

Key intuitions:

- **Time (European)**: usually more time helps, but the effect is *ambiguous*. A large dividend between two maturities can make a shorter-dated European call worth more; a deep-ITM European put may prefer a shorter wait for the payoff. **American options never decrease in value with $$T$$** — a longer option contains every right of the shorter one.
- **Rates**: higher $$r$$ raises the expected growth rate of the stock and lowers the PV of the strike — both help calls, both hurt puts.
- **Dividends** transfer value out of the stock on the ex-date: stock drops, so calls fall and puts rise.

## Upper bounds

A call gives at most the stock; a put gives at most $$K$$ (at $$T$$ for the European version):

$$
c \le S_0, \qquad C \le S_0, \qquad p \le Ke^{-rT}, \qquad P \le K
$$

## Lower bounds (European, non-dividend stock)

$$
c \ge \max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},\, 0), \qquad p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\, 0)
$$

With dividends of present value $$D$$:

$$
c \ge \max(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT},\, 0), \qquad p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} + D - S_0,\, 0)
$$

Dividends tighten the put bound and loosen the call bound — consistent with the six-factor table. If a market price violates a bound, an arbitrage exists (see Worked example 2).

## Put-call parity

For **European** options on a non-dividend-paying stock, a call plus cash equals a put plus the stock (both portfolios pay $$\max(S_T, K)$$ at $$T$$):

$$
c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0
$$

With dividends (PV = $$D$$):

$$
c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0
$$

For **American** options on a dividend-paying stock, only an inequality holds:

$$
S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}
$$

(Set $$D = 0$$ for the non-dividend case.)

{: .warning }
Put-call parity holds for **European options only**. The classic trap supplies American option prices and asks you to plug them into the parity equation — for American options you may only bound $$C - P$$, not pin it down. Second trap: the sign of dividends. Dividends **lower** call values and **raise** put values; in the parity equation $$D$$ is added to the call side.

## Early exercise

**American call, no dividends: never exercise early**, so $$C = c$$. Three reasons:

1. Exercising pays $$K$$ now instead of later — you forfeit interest on $$K$$.
2. You throw away the **insurance**: holding the call protects you if $$S$$ falls below $$K$$ before $$T$$; once you own the stock, it does not.
3. Since $$C \ge c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} > S_0 - K$$, the call is always worth more *alive* (sell it) than *exercised*. If you no longer want exposure, sell the call rather than exercise it.

**American put: early exercise can be optimal**, so $$P > p$$ is possible. Deep ITM (extreme case $$S_0 \to 0$$: the payoff $$K$$ cannot get better), receiving $$K$$ early earns interest. Early exercise is more attractive when $$S_0$$ is low, $$r$$ is high, and $$\sigma$$ is low.

**Dividends** can make early exercise of an American call optimal — only **immediately before an ex-dividend date**, when the dividend captured exceeds the time value plus interest forgone.

### Worked example: put-call parity arbitrage

A 3-month European call on a non-dividend stock trades at $$c = \$3$$ and the 3-month European put (same strike) at $$p = \$2.25$$, with $$S_0 = \$31$$, $$K = \$30$$, $$r = 10\%$$ continuous, $$T = 0.25$$.

Check parity ($$e^{-0.025} = 0.9753$$):

$$
c + Ke^{-rT} = 3 + 30(0.9753) = 3 + 29.2593 = 32.2593
$$

$$
p + S_0 = 2.25 + 31 = 33.25
$$

The put-plus-stock side is overpriced. **Strategy: buy the cheap side, sell the rich side** — buy the call, short the put, short the stock.

Cash at $$t = 0$$: $$-3 + 2.25 + 31 = +\$30.25$$, invested at 10% for 3 months ($$e^{0.025} = 1.0253$$):

$$
30.25 \times 1.0253 = 31.0153
$$

At $$T$$:

- If $$S_T > 30$$: exercise the call — buy the stock for $30 to close the short; the put dies worthless.
- If $$S_T < 30$$: the put is exercised against you — you buy the stock for $30 to close the short; the call dies worthless.

Either way you pay exactly $30. Riskless profit:

$$
31.0153 - 30 = \$1.0153
$$

### Worked example: lower-bound violation arbitrage

A 1-year European call on a non-dividend stock trades at $$c = \$3$$ with $$S_0 = \$20$$, $$K = \$18$$, $$r = 10\%$$ continuous.

Lower bound ($$e^{-0.10} = 0.9048$$):

$$
S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 20 - 18(0.9048) = 20 - 16.2871 = 3.7129 > 3
$$

The call is too cheap. **Buy the call, short the stock**: inflow $$20 - 3 = \$17$$, invested at 10% for 1 year ($$e^{0.10} = 1.1052$$):

$$
17 \times 1.1052 = 18.7884
$$

At $$T$$:

- If $$S_T > 18$$: exercise — buy the stock for $18 to close the short. Profit $$= 18.7884 - 18 = \$0.7884$$.
- If $$S_T \le 18$$: let the call lapse, buy the stock at $$S_T \le 18$$. Profit $$= 18.7884 - S_T \ge \$0.7884$$.

A riskless profit of at least $0.79 confirms the violation.

{: .tip }
For bound/parity arbitrage questions, always "buy low, sell high" at $$t=0$$, park the net inflow at $$r$$, and check both terminal branches ($$S_T > K$$ and $$S_T < K$$) — the payout at $$T$$ should be identical or bounded in both.

## Quick quiz

1. A 6-month European call on a non-dividend stock trades at $4.00; $$S_0 = \$50$$, $$K = \$50$$, $$r = 5\%$$ continuous. By put-call parity, the European put price is closest to:
   - A. $2.16
   - B. $2.77
   - C. $4.00
   - D. $5.23

2. An American call on a non-dividend-paying stock is deep in the money with three months left. The best action for a holder who no longer wants exposure is to:
   - A. Exercise immediately to capture intrinsic value
   - B. Sell the call in the market
   - C. Exercise and short the stock
   - D. Hold to expiration regardless

3. Which change *decreases* the value of a European put, all else equal?
   - A. Higher strike price
   - B. Higher volatility
   - C. Higher risk-free rate
   - D. Higher expected dividends

4. With $$S_0 = \$37$$, $$K = \$40$$, $$r = 5\%$$, $$T = 0.5$$, the lower bound on a European put (non-dividend stock) is closest to:
   - A. $1.01
   - B. $2.01
   - C. $3.00
   - D. $0.00

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — $$p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 4 + 50(0.9753) - 50 = 4 + 48.7655 - 50 = 2.77$$.
2. **B** — alive, $$C \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} > S_0 - K$$; selling captures time value that exercise destroys.
3. **C** — higher $$r$$ lowers the PV of the strike received, hurting puts; A, B, D all raise put values.
4. **B** — $$Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 40(0.9753) - 37 = 39.0124 - 37 = 2.01$$.
</details>
