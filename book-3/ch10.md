# 10. Pricing Financial Forwards and Futures

This is a core computational chapter: GARP tests the cost-of-carry formulas (no income, known income, known yield), the arbitrage that enforces them, and the value of a forward position after inception. Expect direct calculation questions.

## Learning objectives

- Differentiate investment assets from consumption assets.
- Describe short-selling mechanics and the risk of a short squeeze.
- Calculate the forward price of an asset with no income, known dollar income, or a known yield.
- Construct the cash-and-carry (and reverse) arbitrage when the market forward deviates from the no-arbitrage price.
- Calculate the value of an existing forward contract during its life.
- Explain the relationship between forward and futures prices, including the effect of interest-rate correlation.
- Calculate stock index futures prices and explain index arbitrage.
- Price currency forwards and futures as an asset with a known yield.

## Investment vs consumption assets

- **Investment assets** are held purely for investment by a significant number of investors (stocks, bonds, gold, silver). Arbitrage pins their forward prices exactly.
- **Consumption assets** are held primarily to be used (oil, copper, corn). Holders won't sell inventory to exploit mispricing, so arbitrage gives only an upper bound on the forward price (Chapter 11).

## Short selling

A short seller borrows shares (via a broker), sells them, and later buys them back to return. The short seller must pay the lender any **dividends** and post margin; the broker can demand the shares back at any time. A **short squeeze** occurs when borrowable shares dry up and shorts are forced to buy back at rising prices. Short selling is what makes *reverse* cash-and-carry arbitrage feasible for investment assets.

## Assumptions

No transaction costs, a single riskless borrowing/lending rate r (continuously compounded), no taxes distorting strategies, and the ability to short. Market participants exploit any arbitrage as it appears.

## The cost-of-carry formulas

With continuous compounding, spot price $$S_0$$, maturity T:

| Underlying | Forward price |
|---|---|
| No income | $$F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$$ |
| Known dollar income with PV = I | $$F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}$$ |
| Known yield q | $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$$ |
| Currency (foreign rate $$r_f$$) | $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-r_f)T}$$ |

Income *reduces* the forward price because the cash-and-carry arbitrageur receives it while holding the asset. A foreign currency is simply an asset whose yield is the foreign risk-free rate $$r_f$$.

### Enforcement by arbitrage

If the market forward $$F_{mkt} > F_0$$ — **cash-and-carry**: borrow, buy spot, sell forward.

| Time | Action | Cash flow |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Borrow $$S_0$$, buy asset, short forward at $$F_{mkt}$$ | 0 |
| T | Deliver asset, receive $$F_{mkt}$$; repay $$S_0 e^{rT}$$ | $$F_{mkt} - S_0 e^{rT} > 0$$ |

If $$F_{mkt} < F_0$$ — **reverse cash-and-carry**: short the asset, invest the proceeds at r, buy forward; at T pay $$F_{mkt}$$, collect $$S_0 e^{rT}$$, return the shares, and pocket $$S_0 e^{rT} - F_{mkt} > 0$$.

## Value of an existing forward contract

At inception the forward's value is **zero** (the delivery price K is set equal to $$F_0$$). Later, with the current forward price F for the same maturity, the value of a **long** position is

$$f = (F - K)e^{-rT}$$

Equivalently via spot for an asset with income of PV I: $$f = S_0 - I - Ke^{-rT}$$. A short position is worth $$-f$$.

{: .warning }
Compounding trap: every formula above assumes **continuous** compounding. If the exam hands you a semiannual or annual rate, either convert it — $$r_c = m\ln(1 + r_m/m)$$ — or use the discrete formula $$F_0 = S_0(1+r)^T$$ consistently. Mixing conventions is the classic wrong-answer choice. Second trap: the futures **price** $$F_0$$ is not the **value** of the contract — value at inception is zero, and only K is locked in.

## Forward vs futures prices

- With **deterministic** (constant or known) interest rates, forward and futures prices are equal.
- When the asset price is **strongly positively correlated** with interest rates, **futures > forwards**: daily settlement gains arrive when rates are high (reinvested richly) and losses when rates are low (financed cheaply). Negative correlation reverses this.
- In practice the gap is small for short maturities; it matters for long-dated interest-rate contracts (and futures also differ through taxes, transaction costs, margin, and CCP default protection).

## Stock index futures and index arbitrage

A stock index is an asset paying dividend yield q:

$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$$

**Index arbitrage**: if the futures price is above $$S_0e^{(r-q)T}$$, buy the basket of index stocks and short futures; if below, short the stocks (or sell from an indexed portfolio) and buy futures. Executed by computers as program trading; it broke down on Black Monday 1987 when execution lagged.

### Worked example: forward on a dividend-paying stock

A stock trades at $50 and will pay $0.75 dividends in 3 months and in 9 months. The risk-free rate is 4% (continuous) for all maturities. Price a 1-year forward.

PV of dividends:

$$I = 0.75e^{-0.04\times0.25} + 0.75e^{-0.04\times0.75} = 0.75(0.9900) + 0.75(0.9704) = 0.7425 + 0.7278 = 1.4703$$

Forward price:

$$F_0 = (50 - 1.4703)e^{0.04\times1} = 48.5297 \times 1.0408 = 50.51$$

Suppose the market forward is **$51.20** — too high. Cash-and-carry: borrow $50, buy the stock, short the forward at 51.20, reinvest dividends at 4%.

- Loan repayment at T: 50 × $$e^{0.04}$$ = 50 × 1.0408 = $52.0400
- Dividends with reinvestment: 0.75 × $$e^{0.03}$$ + 0.75 × $$e^{0.01}$$ = 0.75(1.0305) + 0.75(1.0101) = 0.7729 + 0.7576 = $1.5305
- Inflows at T: 51.20 (delivery) + 1.5305 = $52.7305

Riskless profit = 52.7305 − 52.0400 = **$0.6905 per share**, matching $$F_{mkt} - F_0$$ = 51.20 − 50.51 = 0.69.

### Worked example: valuing a forward after inception

Six months ago you went **long** a 1-year forward on a non-dividend stock when $$S_0$$ = $40 and r = 5%, so the delivery price was

$$K = 40e^{0.05\times1} = 40 \times 1.0513 = 42.0520$$

Today the stock is at $45, r is still 5%, and 6 months remain. The current 6-month forward price is

$$F = 45e^{0.05\times0.5} = 45 \times 1.0253 = 46.1385$$

Value of the long position:

$$f = (F - K)e^{-rT} = (46.1385 - 42.0520)e^{-0.025} = 4.0865 \times 0.9753 = 3.99$$

Check via spot: $$f = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$$ = 45 − 42.0520 × 0.9753 = 45 − 41.0133 = $3.99. The position that was worth zero at inception is now worth **$3.99 per share** to the long.

## Quick quiz

1. An index is at 2,000 with a dividend yield of 2%; the risk-free rate is 5% (both continuous). The 6-month futures price is closest to:
   - A. 2,015.1
   - B. 2,030.2
   - C. 2,050.6
   - D. 2,071.2

2. If an asset's price is strongly positively correlated with interest rates, then in theory:
   - A. Futures and forward prices are equal
   - B. Futures prices exceed forward prices
   - C. Forward prices exceed futures prices
   - D. Both must equal the expected future spot price

3. You are short a forward with delivery price K = $102 on a non-dividend asset; the current forward price for the same maturity is F = $98, r = 4%, and 1 year remains. The value of your position is closest to:
   - A. −$3.84
   - B. −$4.00
   - C. +$3.84
   - D. +$4.00

4. A stock trades at $25 and will pay dividends with a present value of $1.00 before contract maturity; r = 6% (continuous), T = 0.5. The 6-month forward price is closest to:
   - A. $24.00
   - B. $24.73
   - C. $25.76
   - D. $26.55

1. **B** — $$F_0 = 2000e^{(0.05-0.02)\times0.5} = 2000 \times e^{0.015} = 2000 \times 1.0151 = 2030.2$$; C uses r only, D adds the yield instead of subtracting.
2. **B** — daily settlement gains are reinvested when rates are high and losses financed when rates are low, favoring futures.
3. **C** — short value = $$(K - F)e^{-rT} = (102 - 98)e^{-0.04} = 4 \times 0.9608 = 3.84$$, positive because prices fell after you sold.
