# 4. Introduction to Derivatives

GARP tests payoff calculations (forwards and the four option positions), the exchange-traded vs. OTC distinction, the three classes of market participants with simple numeric comparisons, and the ways derivatives get misused.

## Learning objectives

- Define derivatives and describe how they are used to transfer risk and meet investor needs.
- Distinguish linear from nonlinear derivatives.
- Describe exchange-traded and OTC derivatives markets and their relative advantages.
- Differentiate forwards, futures, options, and swaps.
- Identify and calculate the payoffs of forward and option positions.
- Differentiate hedgers, speculators, and arbitrageurs, and compare hedging with forwards vs. options and speculating with options vs. the underlying.
- Describe the risks arising from the misuse of derivatives.

## What is a derivative?

A **derivative** is a contract whose value depends on ("derives from") the value of an underlying variable — a stock price, interest rate, FX rate, commodity price, credit event, even weather. Derivatives transfer risk from those who do not want it to those who will bear it for a price.

- **Linear derivatives** have payoffs that are a straight-line function of the underlying: forwards, futures, swaps. A position's gain per $1 move in the underlying is constant.
- **Nonlinear derivatives** have kinked or curved payoffs: options. The sensitivity to the underlying changes with the level of the underlying — this is what makes option risk management (the Greeks) necessary.

## Exchange-traded vs. OTC markets

| | Exchange-traded | Over-the-counter (OTC) |
|---|---|---|
| Contracts | Standardized (size, maturity, terms set by the exchange) | Customized, bilaterally negotiated |
| Participants | Anyone with a brokerage account | Mainly banks, funds, corporations |
| Counterparty risk | Minimal — central counterparty (CCP) plus margin | Present, mitigated by collateral and (post-2008) mandatory clearing of standardized contracts |
| Transparency | Public prices | Historically opaque; trade reporting now required |
| Market size | Smaller | Much larger by notional — OTC notional outstanding is in the hundreds of trillions of dollars |

{: .note }
Notional amounts vastly overstate OTC risk: the market value of OTC contracts is a small percentage of notional, and netting reduces exposure further.

## The instruments

- **Forward**: OTC agreement to buy (long) or sell (short) an asset at a future date $$T$$ for a delivery price $$K$$ fixed today. Costs nothing to enter (K is set so initial value is zero).
- **Futures**: the exchange-traded version of a forward — standardized, marked to market daily through margin accounts, guaranteed by a CCP.
- **Option**: the **right, not the obligation**, to buy (**call**) or sell (**put**) at the strike price $$K$$. European options exercise only at maturity; American options any time up to maturity. The buyer pays a **premium** up front; the seller (writer) collects it and takes on the obligation.
- **Swap**: agreement to exchange cash flows on future dates by a set formula (e.g., fixed-for-floating interest); economically a portfolio of forwards.

### Payoffs at maturity

Forward payoffs (per unit of underlying):

$$
\text{Long forward: } S_T - K \qquad \text{Short forward: } K - S_T
$$

The four European option payoffs (gross of premium):

| Position | Payoff | Profit (net of premium $$c$$ or $$p$$) |
|---|---|---|
| Long call | $$\max(S_T - K,\, 0)$$ | $$\max(S_T - K, 0) - c$$ |
| Short call | $$-\max(S_T - K,\, 0)$$ | $$c - \max(S_T - K, 0)$$ |
| Long put | $$\max(K - S_T,\, 0)$$ | $$\max(K - S_T, 0) - p$$ |
| Short put | $$-\max(K - S_T,\, 0)$$ | $$p - \max(K - S_T, 0)$$ |

Entering a forward costs nothing; an option always costs a premium, because the option's payoff is never negative for the holder.

{: .warning }
Exam trap: forwards and futures carry an **obligation** for both sides — a long forward *must* buy at $$K$$ even when $$S_T < K$$, producing a loss; an option holder simply walks away. And every derivative is **zero-sum**: the long's payoff is exactly the short's loss, so "the contract made money" always means *someone else* lost it.

## Market participants

### Hedgers

Hedgers hold a pre-existing exposure and use derivatives to reduce it.

**Example — forwards vs. options.** A US exporter will receive €1,000,000 in 3 months. The 3-month forward rate is 1.10 USD/EUR; a 3-month put on euros with strike 1.10 costs $0.02 per euro ($20,000 total).

| Spot in 3 months | Unhedged | Sell forward at 1.10 | Buy put, strike 1.10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.05 | $1,050,000 | $1,100,000 | $1,100,000 − $20,000 = $1,080,000 |
| 1.20 | $1,200,000 | $1,100,000 | $1,200,000 − $20,000 = $1,180,000 |

The forward locks in $1.10M — certainty, but no upside. The put sets a floor of $1.08M while keeping upside, at the cost of the premium. Neither dominates: hedging is about reducing uncertainty, not maximizing the outcome.

### Speculators

Speculators take on exposure to bet on direction. Derivatives give them **leverage**.

**Example — options vs. spot.** A speculator with $2,000 is bullish on a stock at $20. Two-month calls with strike $22.50 cost $1.

- **Buy stock**: 100 shares.
- **Buy calls**: 2,000 options.

| $$S_T$$ | Stock profit | Stock return | Option profit | Option return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $27 | 100 × 7 = $700 | +35% | 2,000 × (27 − 22.50) − 2,000 = $7,000 | +350% |
| $18 | 100 × (−2) = −$200 | −10% | −$2,000 (calls expire worthless) | −100% |

Leverage amplifies both directions: a good outcome multiplies tenfold, a modest decline wipes out the entire stake.

### Arbitrageurs

Arbitrageurs lock in riskless profit from price discrepancies, and in doing so eliminate them.

**Example.** A stock trades at $152 in New York and £100 in London when the exchange rate is 1.50 USD/GBP. The London price in dollars is 100 × 1.50 = $150. Buy 100 shares in London ($15,000), simultaneously sell 100 in New York ($15,200): riskless profit ≈ **$200** before costs. Arbitrage demand raises the London price and lowers the New York price until the gap is within transaction costs — which is why pricing formulas built on "no arbitrage" work.

## Misuse of derivatives

The same leverage that makes derivatives cheap hedging tools makes them dangerous:

- **Hedgers turning speculators**: a treasury desk mandated to hedge starts taking views because hedged results look "boring" or because past punts paid off. Without position limits and independent risk monitoring, the mandate drifts.
- **Rogue traders**: Barings (1995) — Nick Leeson, supposedly arbitraging Nikkei futures, hid escalating directional losses (~$1.3B) that destroyed the bank; Société Générale (2008) — Jérôme Kerviel built concealed index-futures positions losing €4.9B. Common thread: one person controlling both trading and its back-office confirmation/settlement, defeating segregation of duties.
- **Hidden tail risk**: strategies that earn small premiums most of the time (e.g., writing options) can carry catastrophic losses in rare states — track records understate the risk until it hits.

Controls: separate front and back office, independent valuation, position and loss limits, and watching traders whose reported profits look too good for their mandate.

## Quick quiz

1. An investor is short a forward contract on gold with delivery price $50 (per unit, scaled). At maturity the spot price is $44. The payoff per unit is:
   - A. −$6
   - B. +$6
   - C. $0 — the short can walk away
   - D. +$44

2. A trader buys a European put with strike $40 for a premium of $3. At expiration the stock is at $35. The payoff (gross of premium) and profit are:
   - A. Payoff $5; profit $2
   - B. Payoff $2; profit $2
   - C. Payoff $5; profit $5
   - D. Payoff $0; profit −$3

3. A speculator with $1,000 can buy 40 shares of a $25 stock or 2,000 calls with strike $27 at $0.50 each. If the stock ends at $30, the return on the option strategy is:
   - A. +20%
   - B. +100%
   - C. +500%
   - D. +600%

4. Which statement correctly distinguishes forwards from options?
   - A. Both parties to a forward have an obligation; only the option writer has an obligation
   - B. Both require an upfront premium
   - C. The forward buyer can abandon the contract if prices move adversely
   - D. Options are linear derivatives; forwards are nonlinear

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — short forward payoff = K − S_T = 50 − 44 = +$6; the short profits when prices fall, and neither side can walk away.
2. **A** — payoff = max(40 − 35, 0) = $5; profit = 5 − 3 = $2.
3. **C** — option payoff = 2,000 × (30 − 27) = $6,000; profit = 6,000 − 1,000 = $5,000 on $1,000 invested = +500% (vs. +20% on the stock: 40 × $5 = $200).
4. **A** — forwards bind both sides; an option binds only the writer, which is why the holder pays a premium.
</details>
