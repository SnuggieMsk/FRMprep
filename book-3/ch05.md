# 5. Exchanges and OTC Markets

GARP tests this chapter conceptually with light computation: what exchanges provide, how clearing and margining remove counterparty risk, how OTC markets differ, the toolkit for mitigating bilateral counterparty risk (netting, collateral, SPVs, DPCs, monolines), and the post-crisis reforms.

## Learning objectives

- Describe how exchanges can be used to alleviate counterparty risk.
- Explain developments in clearing that reduce risk: direct clearing, clearing rings, and complete clearing through a central counterparty.
- Define netting and describe a netting process.
- Describe the implementation of a margining process and explain the determinants of, and calculate, initial and variation margin requirements.
- Compare exchange-traded and OTC markets and describe their uses.
- Identify the classes of derivative securities and explain the risks associated with them.
- Identify risks associated with OTC markets and explain how these risks can be mitigated.
- Describe the role of collateralization in the OTC market and compare it to the margining system.
- Explain the use of special purpose vehicles (SPVs) in the OTC derivatives market.

## What exchanges provide

Exchanges began as physical marketplaces for commodities (CBOT, 1848) and evolved into derivatives marketplaces. Their core contributions:

- **Standardization** — contract size, quality of the underlying, delivery dates and locations are fixed by the exchange, so only price is negotiated. Standardization concentrates trading in a few contracts, which creates **liquidity**.
- **Price transparency** — all trades occur at publicly visible prices; quotes and settlement prices are disseminated.
- **Liquidity** — many buyers and sellers in one venue; positions can be closed out easily before delivery.
- **Counterparty risk management** — via the clearing house (below).

**Trading mechanics** moved from **open outcry** (traders shouting and hand-signaling in pits) to **electronic trading** (orders matched by computer). Electronic trading is faster, cheaper, less error-prone, and enables algorithmic and high-frequency trading — but removes the human market-making judgment of the pit.

## Clearing on exchanges

Clearing developed in three stages:

1. **Direct clearing** — two counterparties settle bilaterally; offsetting trades with the *same* counterparty can be netted.
2. **Clearing rings** — groups of members net offsetting positions among themselves, so a trader can exit by ringing out against any ring member, not just the original counterparty.
3. **Complete clearing (central counterparty)** — the modern model. Through **novation**, the original contract is replaced by two contracts: the clearing house becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. Members face only the clearing house.

### Margining

- **Initial margin (IM)** — posted when the position is opened; sized to cover potential losses over the close-out period at a high confidence level (driven by the volatility of the underlying).
- **Variation margin (VM)** — daily (or intraday) settlement of mark-to-market gains and losses. Losers pay VM; winners receive it. This resets the value of each contract to zero every day, so exposure cannot accumulate.
- **Netting** — long and short positions in the same contract offset, reducing both margin requirements and exposure.

**Members vs. non-members.** Only **clearing members** deal directly with the clearing house and contribute to its default fund. Non-members (including clients) must clear through a member, who is responsible to the clearing house for the client's positions and margin.

## OTC markets

The over-the-counter market is a **network of dealers** (mostly banks) trading bilaterally by phone or electronic message, often via **interdealer brokers** who anonymously match dealers' interests. Key features:

- **Bilateral** — each party bears the other's credit risk for the life of the trade.
- **Customizable** — any size, maturity, underlying, or payoff; ideal for hedgers with non-standard exposures.
- **Less transparent** — historically no public price tape or central record of positions.
- Notional outstanding in OTC derivatives is several times larger than exchange-traded notional (dominated by interest rate swaps).

## Mitigating OTC counterparty risk

### Bilateral netting (ISDA master agreements)

An **ISDA master agreement** makes all transactions between two parties a single legal agreement. On default, all transactions are terminated and their values **netted** to one payment (**close-out netting**). Netting also applies to ordinary payments on the same day in the same currency (**payment netting**).

### Worked example: gross vs. net exposure

Bank X has three derivatives with dealer Y, with current values **to Bank X** of +$10 million, −$4 million, and +$6 million.

- **Without netting**, if Y defaults, X loses the positive-value trades but still owes the negative one: exposure = 10 + 6 = **$16 million**.
- **With netting**, exposure = max(10 − 4 + 6, 0) = **$12 million**.

Netting cut the exposure by $4 million — exactly the negative-value trade that can now be offset.

### Collateralization (CSA)

A **Credit Support Annex (CSA)** to the ISDA master agreement requires the party whose portfolio has negative value to post collateral, analogous to exchange variation margin. Key terms:

- **Threshold** — uncollateralized exposure allowed before collateral must be posted (a $5 million threshold against a $12 million exposure requires $7 million of collateral). A zero threshold means full collateralization.
- **Independent amount** — extra collateral posted regardless of mark-to-market, analogous to initial margin.
- **Haircuts** — non-cash collateral is valued at a discount.
- **Minimum transfer amount** — small collateral calls are not made.

Compared with exchange margining, traditional CSA collateral was often one-way, subject to thresholds, disputed, and transferred less frequently — so it mitigates but does not eliminate counterparty risk.

### Other mitigants

| Mechanism | How it works | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Special purpose vehicle (SPV)** | Bankruptcy-remote entity holds collateral/transactions so one counterparty gets priority over the sponsor's other creditors | Transforms counterparty risk into legal risk; courts may consolidate the SPV with the sponsor |
| **Derivative product company (DPC)** | Separately capitalized, AAA-rated subsidiary of a dealer through which it trades derivatives | Rating ultimately tied to the parent; wind-down triggered if the parent fails |
| **Monolines / CDPCs** | Highly rated insurers selling credit protection (e.g., guaranteeing CDS or wrapping bonds), typically posting little or no collateral because of their AAA ratings | Massively leveraged credit exposure; in 2008 losses on structured products triggered downgrades, downgrades triggered collateral calls they could not meet — a death spiral. Protection buyers found their "hedge" worthless exactly when needed |

{: .warning }
The monoline lesson is a classic exam trap: collateral agreements with **ratings-based triggers** create wrong-way, cliff-edge risk. A counterparty that posts no collateral while AAA must suddenly post huge amounts precisely when it is downgraded and least able to pay — so the protection fails exactly when it is most valuable.

## Post-crisis reform of OTC markets

The 2009 G20 commitments (Dodd-Frank in the US, EMIR in the EU) require:

1. **Mandatory central clearing** of standardized OTC derivatives through CCPs.
2. **Trade reporting** of all OTC derivatives to trade repositories (transparency for regulators).
3. Trading of standardized contracts on electronic platforms — **swap execution facilities (SEFs)** — where appropriate.
4. **Higher capital and bilateral margin requirements** for derivatives that remain non-centrally cleared.

The result: the OTC market now resembles the exchange-traded market much more closely for standardized products.

## Exchange-traded vs. OTC comparison

| Feature | Exchange-traded | OTC |
|---|---|---|
| Contract terms | Standardized by exchange | Fully customizable |
| Trading | Central venue (electronic/open outcry) | Bilateral dealer network, interdealer brokers, SEFs |
| Counterparty | Clearing house (after novation) | The dealer (unless centrally cleared) |
| Counterparty risk | Minimal — margining, netting, default fund | Present — mitigated by netting, collateral, CCPs post-crisis |
| Price transparency | High — public quotes and settlement prices | Lower — improved by trade reporting |
| Liquidity | High in standard contracts | Varies; can be low for bespoke trades |
| Typical users | Speculators, standardized hedgers | Hedgers with custom needs, dealers |

## Quick quiz

1. A bank has four trades with one counterparty worth (to the bank) +$8M, −$5M, +$3M, and −$2M. Its current credit exposure with and without close-out netting is closest to:
   - A. $4M with netting; $11M without
   - B. $11M with netting; $4M without
   - C. $4M with netting; $18M without
   - D. $11M with netting; $11M without

2. Under novation by an exchange clearing house:
   - A. The original counterparties remain liable to each other, with the clearing house as guarantor
   - B. The original contract is replaced by two contracts, each with the clearing house as counterparty
   - C. Contracts are netted multilaterally among ring members
   - D. The buyer's obligations are transferred to an SPV

3. The primary reason monoline insurers failed as counterparties in 2008 was:
   - A. They posted too much collateral, draining their liquidity
   - B. Their exposures were fully netted under ISDA agreements
   - C. Downgrades triggered collateral calls they could not meet, after years of posting little or no collateral
   - D. Central clearing mandates forced them out of the market

4. Which of the following was NOT a post-crisis G20 reform of OTC derivatives markets?
   - A. Mandatory central clearing of standardized OTC derivatives
   - B. Reporting of all OTC derivatives to trade repositories
   - C. A requirement that all OTC derivatives be converted to exchange-traded futures
   - D. Higher capital and margin requirements for non-centrally cleared derivatives

1. **A** — With netting: max(8 − 5 + 3 − 2, 0) = $4M. Without: only positive values count, 8 + 3 = $11M.
2. **B** — Novation tears up the original contract and substitutes the clearing house as buyer to the seller and seller to the buyer.
3. **C** — Ratings-based collateral triggers created a cliff-edge: downgrade → massive collateral calls → failure exactly when protection was needed.
4. **C** — Standardized contracts must be cleared and traded on platforms (SEFs) where appropriate, but bespoke OTC contracts still exist; nothing forced conversion to futures.
