# 18. Mortgages and Mortgage-Backed Securities

Two skills dominate exam questions here: level-payment mortgage arithmetic (payment, interest/principal split, amortization) and prepayment measurement (CPR ↔ SMM, PSA). Layer on the securitization vocabulary — agency vs. non-agency, pass-throughs vs. CMOs, dollar rolls — and the chapter is done.

## Learning objectives

- Describe the various types of residential mortgage products.
- Calculate the fixed monthly payment on a level-payment mortgage and decompose payments into interest and scheduled principal (amortization).
- Summarize the drivers of prepayment and the risk prepayment creates for investors.
- Calculate prepayment speeds: CPR, SMM, and the PSA benchmark, and convert between CPR and SMM.
- Differentiate agency from non-agency MBS, and pass-throughs from collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs).
- Describe the dollar roll transaction and evaluate its financing implications; explain weighted average life (WAL) and why it differs from duration.

## Mortgage types

- **Level-payment fixed-rate mortgage**: constant monthly payment fully amortizing the loan; the borrower holds a valuable **prepayment option** (effectively a call on the loan).
- **Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)**: rate resets periodically off a reference index plus a margin, often with initial teaser rates and caps; interest-rate risk shifts to the borrower.
- Other variants: balloon (large final payment), interest-only periods, hybrid ARMs (fixed then floating). Credit quality splits into **prime**, **subprime**, and government-insured (FHA/VA) loans.

## Level-payment mortgage math

For principal $$L$$, monthly rate $$r$$ (annual rate ÷ 12) and $$n$$ monthly payments, the level payment is

$$
X = \frac{L \, r}{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}
$$

Each month, **interest = monthly rate × beginning balance**; **scheduled principal = payment − interest**; the balance falls by the scheduled principal. Early in the loan almost all of the payment is interest; the principal share grows over time (amortization). The outstanding balance at any date equals the present value of the remaining payments at rate $$r$$.

### Worked example: monthly payment and the first-month split

A $300,000, 30-year level-payment mortgage carries a 6% annual rate ($$r = 0.5\%$$ monthly, $$n = 360$$).

**Step 1 — payment.** With $$(1.005)^{360} = 6.0226$$, so $$(1.005)^{-360} = 0.16604$$:

$$
X = \frac{300{,}000 \times 0.005}{1 - 0.16604} = \frac{1{,}500}{0.83396} = \$1{,}798.65
$$

**Step 2 — first-month interest.**

$$
\text{Interest}_1 = 0.005 \times 300{,}000 = \$1{,}500.00
$$

**Step 3 — first-month scheduled principal and new balance.**

$$
\text{Principal}_1 = 1{,}798.65 - 1{,}500.00 = \$298.65, \qquad \text{Balance}_1 = 300{,}000 - 298.65 = \$299{,}701.35
$$

Only 16.6% of the first payment reduces principal — a favorite conceptual follow-up.

## Prepayment: drivers and risk

Borrowers prepay because of **refinancing** (rates fall below the loan rate — the dominant, rate-driven component), **housing turnover** (moves, relocations), **curtailments** (partial extra payments), and **defaults** (foreclosure proceeds pass through as prepayment in agency pools). Refinancing incentive depends on the loan rate vs. current rates, and shows **burnout**: pools already heavily refinanced respond less to a given rate drop because remaining borrowers couldn't or wouldn't refinance.

Prepayment creates two-sided risk for the investor:

- **Contraction risk**: rates fall → prepayments accelerate → principal returns early, exactly when reinvestment rates are low. Price appreciation is capped — **negative convexity**.
- **Extension risk**: rates rise → prepayments slow → the security lengthens just when you want your money back.

## Measuring prepayment speed: SMM, CPR, PSA

**SMM (single monthly mortality)** is the fraction of the beginning-of-month balance, *net of scheduled principal*, that prepays that month:

$$
\text{SMM} = \frac{\text{Prepayment}}{\text{Beginning balance} - \text{Scheduled principal}}
$$

**CPR (conditional prepayment rate)** is the annualized equivalent. The conversion compounds survival, not the rate itself:

$$
\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR})^{1/12}, \qquad \text{CPR} = 1 - (1 - \text{SMM})^{12}
$$

The **PSA benchmark** (100 PSA) assumes CPR starts at 0.2% in month 1, rises by 0.2% per month until reaching 6% at month 30, and stays at 6% thereafter. Multiples scale the whole path: at 150 PSA, the month-$$t$$ CPR is 1.5 × the 100-PSA CPR for that month (capped path at 1.5 × 6% = 9% after month 30).

{: .warning }
Do **not** convert CPR to SMM by dividing by 12 — that ignores compounding and is always offered as a distractor. CPR of 6% gives $$\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.94^{1/12} = 0.5143\%$$, not 0.50%. And apply the SMM to the balance **after** subtracting that month's scheduled principal, not to the raw beginning balance.

### Worked example: SMM from CPR and the prepayment amount

A pool prepays at a constant 6% CPR. The beginning-of-month balance is $100,000,000 and scheduled principal for the month is $150,000.

**Step 1 — convert CPR to SMM.**

$$
\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - 0.06)^{1/12} = 1 - 0.94^{0.08333} = 1 - 0.994857 = 0.005143 = 0.5143\%
$$

**Step 2 — apply SMM to the balance net of scheduled principal.**

$$
\text{Prepayment} = 0.005143 \times (100{,}000{,}000 - 150{,}000) = 0.005143 \times 99{,}850{,}000 = \$513{,}529
$$

Total principal returned this month = $$150{,}000 + 513{,}529 = \$663{,}529$$.

## Agency vs. non-agency; pass-throughs vs. CMOs

- **Agency MBS** are issued/guaranteed by Ginnie Mae (full faith and credit of the US government) or by the GSEs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Investors bear essentially **no credit risk, only prepayment risk**. Loans must "conform" to size and underwriting limits.
- **Non-agency (private-label) MBS** securitize jumbo, alt-A, or subprime loans; investors bear credit risk, managed with credit enhancement (subordination, overcollateralization, excess spread).
- A **pass-through** sends every holder a pro-rata share of interest and principal (less servicing/guarantee fees). Pools trade **TBA (to-be-announced)**: price, coupon, issuer, and month are agreed, but the specific pools are delivered later — this makes the market deeply liquid; "specified pools" trade at a pay-up.
- A **CMO** carves pass-through cash flows into tranches with different prepayment exposure: **sequential-pay** tranches receive principal in order; **PAC** tranches get a stable schedule protected by **support (companion)** tranches that absorb prepayment variability. CMOs redistribute prepayment risk; they do not eliminate it.

## Dollar rolls, WAL, and duration

In a **dollar roll**, an MBS holder sells a TBA position for delivery this month and simultaneously buys it back for delivery next month. The price drop between the two settlement months (the "drop") plus the lost coupon/principal during the month determine the implied financing cost — when that implied rate is below the holder's funding rate, the roll is cheap financing ("roll trades special"). The buyer of the roll holds the bonds during the month and receives the cash flows.

**Weighted average life (WAL)** is the average time to receipt of **principal**, weighted by the principal amounts. It answers "when does my money come back?" but it is a cash-flow timing measure, **not** a price-sensitivity measure: **duration** weights the PV of *all* cash flows and, for MBS, must also account for prepayments changing with rates (effective duration). Because of negative convexity, an MBS's effective duration shortens as rates fall and extends as rates rise.
