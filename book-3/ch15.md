# 15. Exotic Options

GARP tests definitions and payoff formulas for the exotic "menagerie," which exotics are path-dependent, which are cheaper or dearer than vanilla, and a handful of identities (barrier in + out = vanilla, chooser decomposition, gap payoffs that can go negative).

## Learning objectives

- Define and contrast exotic derivatives with plain vanilla derivatives, and describe factors driving the development of exotics.
- Explain how any derivative can be converted into a zero-cost product, and how standard American options become nonstandard American options.
- Identify the characteristics and payoff structures of: packages, gap, forward start, cliquet, compound, chooser, barrier, binary, lookback, shout, Asian, exchange, basket, and rainbow options.
- Describe and contrast volatility and variance swaps.
- Explain the basic premise of static option replication and its application to hedging exotic options.

## Why exotics exist

Exotics arise from genuine hedging needs (matching a corporate exposure exactly), tax/regulatory/accounting motives, and dealers' ability to charge wider margins on tailored products. Any derivative can be made **zero-cost** by deferring the premium: the buyer pays the future value of the premium at maturity instead of up front (a zero-cost collar/range forward is the classic example — buy a put, sell a call with strikes chosen so premiums net to zero).

## The menagerie

- **Packages**: portfolios of vanilla options, forwards, cash, and the asset — e.g., bull spreads, straddles, **range forwards** (zero-cost collars). Not exotic individually, but priced as a bundle.
- **Nonstandard American**: early exercise restricted — **Bermudan** (exercise only on specified dates), **lock-out** (no exercise during an initial period), or a strike that steps up/down over the option's life. Typical of warrants and employee stock options.
- **Gap options**: a gap call pays $$S_T - K_1$$ if $$S_T > K_2$$ and zero otherwise. The trigger $$K_2$$ and the payoff strike $$K_1$$ differ, so **the payoff can be negative** (when $$K_1 > K_2$$ and $$K_2 < S_T < K_1$$). A gap put pays $$K_1 - S_T$$ if $$S_T < K_2$$.
- **Forward start options**: paid for now, start at a future date $$T_1$$, usually struck **at the money at $$T_1$$**. Employee stock option grants promised today for future award dates are forward start options.
- **Cliquet (ratchet)**: a series of forward start options, each resetting at the money when the previous one expires — locks in gains period by period.
- **Compound options**: options on options — call-on-call, call-on-put, put-on-call, put-on-put. **Two strikes and two maturities**: at $$T_1$$ pay $$K_1$$ to acquire (or sell) the underlying option with strike $$K_2$$ expiring at $$T_2$$. Cheap up-front exposure; very sensitive to volatility.
- **Chooser options**: at $$T_1$$ the holder chooses whether the option is a call or a put (both with strike $$K$$, expiry $$T_2$$). Value at $$T_1$$ is $$\max(c, p)$$; using put-call parity this decomposes into a $$T_2$$-maturity call plus $$e^{-q(T_2-T_1)}$$ put options with adjusted strike maturing at $$T_1$$ — i.e., a package of a call and a put, which is why a chooser resembles (and costs less than) a straddle.
- **Barrier options**: payoff depends on whether $$S$$ hits a barrier $$H$$. Four basic types: **up-and-in, up-and-out, down-and-in, down-and-out** (knock-ins come alive at the barrier; knock-outs die). For the same $$K, T, H$$:

$$
c_{\text{in}} + c_{\text{out}} = c_{\text{vanilla}}, \qquad p_{\text{in}} + p_{\text{out}} = p_{\text{vanilla}}
$$

  Barriers are **cheaper than vanilla** (you can lose the option). A **reverse knock-out** has its barrier in the money (e.g., an up-and-out call with $$H > K$$), so it dies precisely when it is most valuable — large negative vega/odd Greeks near the barrier.
- **Binary (digital) options**: **cash-or-nothing** call pays a fixed $$Q$$ if $$S_T > K$$ (value $$Qe^{-rT}N(d_2)$$); **asset-or-nothing** call pays $$S_T$$ if $$S_T > K$$. A vanilla call = asset-or-nothing call − cash-or-nothing call with $$Q = K$$.
- **Lookback options**: payoff depends on the extreme price over the life. **Floating** lookback call pays $$S_T - S_{\min}$$ (buy at the low); floating lookback put pays $$S_{\max} - S_T$$ (sell at the high). **Fixed** lookback call pays $$\max(S_{\max} - K, 0)$$; fixed lookback put pays $$\max(K - S_{\min}, 0)$$. Path-dependent and **expensive** relative to vanilla.
- **Shout options**: the holder may "shout" once, locking in the intrinsic value at the shout date as a floor; final payoff is $$\max(S_T - K, S_\tau - K)$$ for a call shouted at $$\tau$$. Cheaper than a lookback, similar idea.
- **Asian options**: payoff based on the average price. **Average price call**: $$\max(S_{ave} - K, 0)$$; average price put: $$\max(K - S_{ave}, 0)$$. **Average strike call**: $$\max(S_T - S_{ave}, 0)$$; average strike put: $$\max(S_{ave} - S_T, 0)$$. Averaging lowers effective volatility, so average price options are **cheaper than vanilla**, and the average is **harder to manipulate** than a single closing price — ideal for hedging a stream of FX conversions.
- **Exchange options**: the right to exchange one asset for another; payoff $$\max(S_T^{(1)} - S_T^{(2)}, 0)$$ — e.g., exchange shares in a stock-for-stock takeover bid.
- **Basket options**: payoff on a **portfolio** of assets; cheaper than a basket of options because asset correlations < 1 reduce basket volatility.
- **Rainbow options**: payoff depends on several assets, e.g., the best-of or worst-of two or more assets (the CTD choice in a bond futures contract is rainbow-like).

## Volatility and variance swaps

A **volatility swap** exchanges realized volatility over the period for a fixed (strike) volatility, with payoff $$L(\sigma_{realized} - \sigma_K)$$ — pure volatility exposure with no price-direction exposure. A **variance swap** pays on realized **variance**, $$L(\sigma^2_{realized} - \sigma^2_K)$$, and is easier to value and hedge because variance can be replicated from a static strip of vanilla calls and puts across strikes; dealers therefore prefer variance swaps.

## Static option replication

Instead of dynamic delta hedging (costly, breaks down near barriers), **static replication** finds a portfolio of vanilla options that matches the exotic's value on a chosen boundary (e.g., along the barrier and at maturity). If two portfolios agree on the whole boundary, they agree inside it; the vanilla portfolio is bought once and unwound only if the boundary is reached.

## Comparison table

| Option | Payoff | Path-dependent? | vs. vanilla cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gap call | $$S_T - K_1$$ if $$S_T > K_2$$ | No | Either (can be negative payoff) |
| Forward start | Vanilla from $$T_1$$, ATM at $$T_1$$ | No | Comparable (PV of future ATM option) |
| Compound | Option on an option | No | Cheaper up front, high leverage |
| Chooser | $$\max(c, p)$$ at $$T_1$$ | No | Cheaper than straddle |
| Barrier (in or out) | Vanilla conditional on hitting $$H$$ | Yes | **Cheaper** |
| Binary cash-or-nothing | $$Q$$ if ITM | No | — |
| Floating lookback call | $$S_T - S_{\min}$$ | Yes | **Dearer** |
| Asian average price call | $$\max(S_{ave} - K, 0)$$ | Yes | **Cheaper** |
| Exchange | $$\max(S_T^{(1)} - S_T^{(2)}, 0)$$ | No | — |
| Basket | Option on portfolio value | No | Cheaper than sum of options |

{: .warning }
Two classic traps. (1) A **gap option's payoff can be negative**: the trigger $$K_2$$ and the payoff strike $$K_1$$ are different numbers, and exercise is automatic when $$S_T > K_2$$ — do not floor the payoff at zero. (2) **Barrier parity**: knock-in + knock-out with the same strike, maturity, and barrier = the vanilla option. If a question gives you two of the three prices, the third is free marks.

### Worked example: gap call with a negative payoff

A gap call has payoff strike $$K_1 = \$50$$ and trigger $$K_2 = \$45$$.

- $$S_T = 60$$: trigger met ($$60 > 45$$), payoff $$= 60 - 50 = \$10$$.
- $$S_T = 47$$: trigger met ($$47 > 45$$), payoff $$= 47 - 50 = -\$3$$ — the holder **loses** $3 at expiry.
- $$S_T = 44$$: trigger not met, payoff $$= 0$$.

A vanilla 50-strike call would pay 10, 0, 0 in the three cases; the gap call is cheaper than the vanilla 45-strike call precisely because of the negative-payoff region between 45 and 50.

### Worked example: lookbacks on a price path

A stock observed monthly over an option's life trades at 50, 45, 62, 58, and finishes at $$S_T = 55$$. So $$S_{\min} = 45$$, $$S_{\max} = 62$$.

- Floating lookback call: $$S_T - S_{\min} = 55 - 45 = \$10$$ (you bought at the low).
- Floating lookback put: $$S_{\max} - S_T = 62 - 55 = \$7$$ (you sold at the high).
- Fixed lookback call, $$K = 50$$: $$\max(S_{\max} - K, 0) = 62 - 50 = \$12$$.
- Asian average price call, $$K = 50$$: $$S_{ave} = (50 + 45 + 62 + 58 + 55)/5 = 270/5 = 54$$, payoff $$= 54 - 50 = \$4$$ — versus $$55 - 50 = \$5$$ for the vanilla call on this path.

## Quick quiz

1. A gap call has $$K_1 = 55$$ and $$K_2 = 50$$. At expiry $$S_T = 53$$. The payoff is:
   - A. $0
   - B. $3
   - C. −$2
   - D. −$5

2. A down-and-out call is worth $4.20 and the vanilla call (same strike, maturity) is worth $6.50. The down-and-in call with the same barrier is worth:
   - A. $2.30
   - B. $4.20
   - C. $6.50
   - D. $10.70

3. Compared with an otherwise identical vanilla call, an average price (Asian) call is:
   - A. Dearer, because it is path-dependent
   - B. Cheaper, because averaging reduces effective volatility
   - C. Identical in value by no-arbitrage
   - D. Dearer, because it is harder to manipulate

4. A floating lookback call pays:
   - A. $$\max(S_{\max} - K, 0)$$
   - B. $$S_{\max} - S_T$$
   - C. $$S_T - S_{\min}$$
   - D. $$\max(K - S_{\min}, 0)$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **C** — trigger met ($$53 > 50$$), so payoff $$= S_T - K_1 = 53 - 55 = -\$2$$; gap payoffs are not floored at zero.
2. **A** — in + out = vanilla: $$6.50 - 4.20 = \$2.30$$.
3. **B** — the average is less volatile than the terminal price, so the option is cheaper (manipulation-resistance is a feature, not a cost driver upward).
4. **C** — floating lookback call buys at the observed minimum; A is the fixed lookback call.
</details>
