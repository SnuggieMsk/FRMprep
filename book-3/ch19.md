# 19. Interest Rate Futures

A computation-dense chapter: day counts, accrued interest, Treasury bond futures with conversion factors and the cheapest-to-deliver rule, Eurodollar/SOFR futures arithmetic ($25 per basis point), the futures-to-forward convexity adjustment, and duration-based hedging. Every formula here is directly testable.

## Learning objectives

- Identify the most commonly used day-count conventions and calculate accrued interest under each.
- Calculate the price of a T-bill from its quoted discount rate, and convert between clean (quoted) and dirty (cash) bond prices.
- Describe Treasury bond futures: conversion factors, cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) selection, and the delivery options (including the wild card play).
- Calculate the final contract price of a Eurodollar/three-month SOFR futures contract and the gain/loss per basis point.
- Apply the convexity adjustment to convert a futures rate to a forward rate.
- Calculate the number of futures contracts in a duration-based hedge.

## Day-count conventions

| Instrument | Convention | Accrual fraction |
|---|---|---|
| US Treasury bonds/notes | **Actual/Actual** (in period) | actual days elapsed ÷ actual days in coupon period |
| US corporate and municipal bonds | **30/360** | each month counts 30 days, year counts 360 |
| US money market (T-bills, SOFR) | **Actual/360** | actual days ÷ 360 |

Accrued interest = coupon for the period × accrual fraction. Note Actual/360 means a 365-day year accrues 365/360 of the quoted rate — money-market rates understate the true annual rate.

**T-bill quotes** use a **discount rate** on face value: if the quoted discount is $$Q$$ (% per annum) for $$n$$ days, the cash price per $100 face is

$$
P = 100 - \frac{n}{360}\,Q
$$

The discount rate understates the true yield because it divides by face (100), not price, and uses a 360-day year.

## Clean vs. dirty prices

Bonds are **quoted clean**; you **pay dirty**:

$$
\text{Cash (dirty) price} = \text{Quoted (clean) price} + \text{Accrued interest}
$$

### Worked example: accrued interest and dirty price

A US Treasury bond with an 8% coupon (paid semiannually March 1 and September 1) is quoted at 98-16 for settlement on July 3.

**Step 1 — day count (Actual/Actual).** March 1 → July 3 = 124 actual days; March 1 → September 1 = 184 actual days.

**Step 2 — accrued interest.** The semiannual coupon is 4 per 100 face:

$$
\text{AI} = 4 \times \frac{124}{184} = 2.6957
$$

**Step 3 — dirty price.** The quote 98-16 means $$98\tfrac{16}{32} = 98.50$$:

$$
\text{Cash price} = 98.50 + 2.6957 = 101.1957
$$

If this were a **corporate** bond (30/360), days would be $$4 \times 30 + 2 = 122$$ over a 180-day period: $$\text{AI} = 4 \times 122/180 = 2.7111$$ — a small but testable difference.

## Treasury bond futures

The short can deliver any eligible long-term bond; the invoice price adjusts by a **conversion factor (CF)**, the approximate price of the delivered bond per $1 face assuming a 6% flat yield:

$$
\text{Cash received by short} = (\text{Futures settlement} \times \text{CF}) + \text{Accrued interest}
$$

The short delivers the **cheapest-to-deliver** bond — the one minimizing

$$
\text{Cost} = \text{Quoted bond price} - (\text{Futures price} \times \text{CF})
$$

When yields exceed 6%, low-coupon long-maturity bonds tend to be CTD; below 6%, high-coupon short-maturity bonds. The short also holds **delivery timing options** and the **wild card play**: futures settle at 2:00 p.m., but the short can issue a notice of intention to deliver until later that evening at the 2:00 p.m. price — if cash prices fall after the close, delivering cheap bonds at the fixed invoice price is profitable. These embedded options *reduce* the futures price relative to a no-options contract.

### Worked example: cheapest-to-deliver selection

The futures settlement price is 93.25. Three deliverable bonds:

| Bond | Quoted price | CF | Cost = Price − Futures × CF |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 99.50 | 1.0382 | $$99.50 - 93.25 \times 1.0382 = 99.50 - 96.8122 = 2.6878$$ |
| 2 | 143.50 | 1.5188 | $$143.50 - 93.25 \times 1.5188 = 143.50 - 141.6281 = 1.8719$$ |
| 3 | 119.75 | 1.2615 | $$119.75 - 93.25 \times 1.2615 = 119.75 - 117.6349 = 2.1151$$ |

Bond 2 has the lowest delivery cost and is **cheapest to deliver**.

## Eurodollar and three-month SOFR futures

Quote = $$100 - R$$, where $$R$$ is the (annualized) three-month rate in percent. Contract size $1 million, three-month accrual ⇒ one basis point change in the rate moves the contract value by

$$
1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 \times \frac{90}{360} = \$25
$$

Final contract price $$= 10{,}000 \times [100 - 0.25(100 - Q)]$$. A **long** position gains when the quote rises, i.e., when rates **fall**. Three-month SOFR futures work identically but settle to compounded realized SOFR over the reference quarter.

## Convexity adjustment: futures rate → forward rate

Daily settlement makes the futures rate exceed the corresponding forward rate (gains arrive when rates are high and fund cheaply when rates are low — an advantage to the short rate position priced into futures). For a futures contract on the rate between $$t_1$$ and $$t_2$$ (rates continuously compounded, $$\sigma$$ = annual standard deviation of the short rate):

$$
\text{forward} = \text{futures} - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t_1 t_2
$$

Example: futures rate 6.038%, $$\sigma = 1.2\%$$, $$t_1 = 8$$, $$t_2 = 8.25$$: adjustment $$= \tfrac{1}{2}(0.012)^2(8)(8.25) = 0.00475$$, so the forward rate is $$6.038\% - 0.475\% = 5.563\%$$. The adjustment grows with the **square** of both volatility and (roughly) maturity — negligible for short maturities, large beyond a few years.

## Duration-based hedging

To hedge a portfolio of value $$P$$ and duration $$D_P$$ with futures of contract price $$F_C$$ (price per contract) and underlying duration $$D_F$$:

$$
N^* = -\frac{P\,D_P}{F_C\,D_F}
$$

A negative $$N^*$$ means **short** futures (the usual case for hedging a long bond portfolio against rate rises).

{: .warning }
Duration hedging assumes a small **parallel** shift in the yield curve — it fails for twists and large moves (no convexity). For T-bond futures, use the duration of the **cheapest-to-deliver bond at the futures expiry**, not the portfolio's duration, for $$D_F$$. And remember the CTD cost formula subtracts $$\text{futures} \times \text{CF}$$ from the **quoted** price — adding accrued interest to both sides cancels, so dirty prices are a distractor.

### Worked example: duration-based hedge

A manager holds a $10 million bond portfolio with duration 6.8 years and fears rising rates over the next three months. T-bond futures trade at 93-02 (i.e., $$93\tfrac{2}{32} = 93.0625$$, contract value $$\$93{,}062.50$$) and the CTD bond will have duration 9.2 years at expiry.

$$
N^* = -\frac{10{,}000{,}000 \times 6.8}{93{,}062.50 \times 9.2} = -\frac{68{,}000{,}000}{856{,}175} = -79.4
$$

**Short 79 contracts.** If rates fall instead, the hedge gives up the portfolio's gains — duration hedging locks in value both ways.

## Quick quiz

1. A 90-day T-bill is quoted at a discount rate of 8.00%. Its cash price per $100 of face value is:
   - A. $92.00
   - B. $98.00
   - C. $98.04
   - D. $96.00

2. One basis point move in the rate underlying a Eurodollar futures contract changes the contract value by:
   - A. $100
   - B. $50
   - C. $25
   - D. $10

3. The futures settlement price is 95.00. Deliverable bonds: A quoted 98.00 (CF 1.0300), B quoted 105.00 (CF 1.1000), C quoted 112.50 (CF 1.1800). The cheapest to deliver is:
   - A. Bond A
   - B. Bond B
   - C. Bond C
   - D. Cannot be determined without accrued interest

4. Relative to the forward rate for the same period, the Eurodollar futures rate is:
   - A. Lower, because of the convexity adjustment
   - B. Higher, because daily settlement favors the position when rates rise
   - C. Identical, by no-arbitrage
   - D. Higher only when the yield curve is inverted

<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — $$P = 100 - (90/360)(8) = 100 - 2 = 98.00$$. (C, $$100/1.02$$, wrongly treats the discount quote as a yield.)
2. **C** — $$\$1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 \times 0.25 = \$25$$.
3. **A** — costs: A: $$98 - 95(1.03) = 98 - 97.85 = 0.15$$; B: $$105 - 104.50 = 0.50$$; C: $$112.50 - 112.10 = 0.40$$. Lowest cost wins; accrued interest cancels from both legs.
4. **B** — futures rate = forward rate **plus** $$\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t_1 t_2$$; daily margining is advantageous to the rate position, so the futures rate is biased upward, and the adjustment subtracts it.
