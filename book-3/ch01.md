# 1. Banks

GARP tests this chapter conceptually: the taxonomy of bank risks, the economic-vs-regulatory capital distinction, moral hazard from deposit insurance, conflicts of interest, originate-to-distribute, and the mechanics (and risks) of securities underwriting.

## Learning objectives

- Identify the major risks banks face and explain how each arises.
- Distinguish economic capital from regulatory capital, and explain the Basel Committee's motivation for capital regulation.
- Explain how deposit insurance creates a moral hazard problem.
- Describe investment banking financing arrangements: private placement, public offering, best efforts, firm commitment, and Dutch auction IPOs.
- Describe potential conflicts of interest among commercial banking, securities services, and investment banking, and how they are managed.
- Distinguish the banking book from the trading book.
- Explain the originate-to-distribute model, its benefits, and its drawbacks.

## The major risks banks face

| Risk | Definition | How it arises |
|---|---|---|
| **Credit risk** | Losses from borrowers or counterparties failing to pay | Loan defaults, bond issuer defaults, derivative counterparty failures |
| **Market risk** | Losses from movements in market prices | Trading-book positions in equities, rates, FX, commodities, and derivatives |
| **Operational risk** | Losses from failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events | Rogue traders, cyberattacks, fraud, legal/compliance failures, system outages |

Credit risk is typically the largest risk for a commercial bank; operational risk is the hardest to quantify because losses are infrequent but can be enormous.

## Economic capital vs. regulatory capital

- **Expected loss (EL)** is the average loss a bank anticipates. It is a *cost of doing business* and is covered by **pricing** — the interest spread charged on loans.
- **Unexpected loss (UL)** is the loss beyond the average in bad years. It is absorbed by **capital** (equity), which exists so depositors and senior creditors survive a bad year.
- **Economic capital** is the bank's *own* estimate of the capital needed to keep its default probability below a target (e.g., losses exceeded only 0.1% of the time, consistent with an AA rating).
- **Regulatory capital** is the *minimum* set by supervisors (the Basel Committee framework), motivated by systemic stability and the protection of depositors and the deposit-insurance fund. A bank holds the greater of the two in practice.

### Numeric illustration

A bank has a $10 billion loan portfolio. Expected loss is 0.8% per year; the 99.9th-percentile one-year loss is 3.0%.

- EL = 0.8% × $10B = **$80 million** — built into loan spreads (pricing), not capital.
- 99.9% loss = 3.0% × $10B = $300 million.
- Economic capital = UL at 99.9% = $300M − $80M = **$220 million** of equity.

{: .note }
Capital covers *unexpected* loss only. If a question asks how much capital is needed against a loss distribution, subtract the expected loss first — EL is covered by pricing/provisions.

## Deposit insurance and moral hazard

Deposit insurance (FDIC in the US, up to $250,000 per depositor per bank) prevents bank runs: insured depositors have no reason to flee a troubled bank. But it creates **moral hazard**: because insured depositors do not monitor risk or demand higher rates from risky banks, a bank can fund aggressive risk-taking with cheap insured deposits — heads the shareholders win, tails the insurance fund pays. The 1980s US savings-and-loan crisis is the classic example. Mitigants: capital regulation, supervision, and risk-based deposit-insurance premiums.

## Commercial vs. investment banking

- **Commercial banking**: taking deposits and making loans (retail and wholesale). Income is primarily net interest margin.
- **Investment banking**: raising capital for issuers (debt and equity underwriting), advising on M&A, brokerage, market making, and trading. Income is primarily fees and trading profits.

Many large banks do both ("universal banks"); the US Glass-Steagall separation (1933) was effectively repealed in 1999.

## Conflicts of interest and Chinese walls

- **Research vs. underwriting**: analysts may be pressured to publish favorable research on companies the bank is underwriting to win or keep the mandate.
- **Commercial banking vs. securities business**: a bank with private knowledge that a borrower is in trouble could underwrite that borrower's bonds and have its loan repaid from the proceeds — dumping credit risk onto bond investors. It could also push trust/asset-management clients into those securities.
- **Banking vs. trading**: confidential client information (e.g., a pending takeover) could leak to the trading desk.

The standard remedy is **Chinese walls** (information barriers): internal rules, physical and IT separation, and compliance monitoring preventing information flow between departments, reinforced by regulation.

## Originate-to-distribute

Under **originate-to-distribute (OTD)**, a bank originates loans (e.g., mortgages) and then sells them — usually via **securitization** — rather than holding them to maturity.

**Benefits**: frees up capital and balance-sheet capacity, transfers credit risk to investors willing to bear it, increases credit supply, generates fee income, and gives investors access to loan exposures.

**Drawbacks (the 2007–08 lesson)**: when the originator keeps no exposure, its incentive to screen and monitor borrowers collapses. Lending standards on US subprime mortgages deteriorated (NINJA loans, teaser rates), the loans were repackaged into MBS and CDOs, and when house prices fell the losses propagated through the financial system. Post-crisis rules require originators to retain "skin in the game" (risk retention).

## Banking book vs. trading book

| | Banking book | Trading book |
|---|---|---|
| Contents | Loans and instruments held to maturity / not traded | Instruments held for trading or market making |
| Accounting | Historical cost / amortized cost; loss provisions when impairment is likely | **Marked to market** daily; gains and losses flow through P&L |
| Capital charge | Credit risk rules | Market risk rules |

{: .warning }
Exam trap: moving assets between books changes the accounting and the capital charge. During 2007–08, banks held securitized credit exposures in the trading book (lower capital, mark-to-market) and were accused of regulatory arbitrage; illiquid positions also made "market" values unreliable. Regulators now restrict reclassification between books.

## Raising capital for issuers

- **Private placement**: securities sold directly to a small number of sophisticated institutions; faster and cheaper, no public registration, but less liquid. Bank earns a fee.
- **Public offering**: securities sold broadly to the public; requires registration/prospectus.
  - **Best efforts**: the bank sells what it can at the offer price for a fee — the *issuer* bears the risk of a shortfall.
  - **Firm commitment**: the bank *buys the entire issue* at one price and resells at a higher offer price — the *bank* bears the placement risk.
- **IPO**: first public sale of equity. Pricing is hard (no market history); IPOs tend to be underpriced on average (large first-day pops), which costs the issuer money.
- **Dutch auction IPO**: investors submit bids (number of shares, price); the clearing price is the highest price at which *all* shares can be sold, and every successful bidder pays that single price. Used by Google (2004); designed to let the market set the price and reduce underwriter underpricing.

### Numeric example: firm-commitment risk

A bank agrees on a firm-commitment basis to buy 50 million shares from an issuer at $30 and offer them at $32.

- If the full issue sells at $32: profit = 50M × ($32 − $30) = **+$100 million**.
- If demand is weak and shares can only be sold at $29: loss = 50M × ($30 − $29) = **−$50 million**.

Under best efforts with a fee of, say, $0.30 per share actually sold, the bank's revenue is modest but never negative — the price risk stays with the issuer.

## Quick quiz

1. A bank's loan portfolio has an expected one-year loss of $40M and a 99.9th-percentile one-year loss of $190M. The economic capital it should hold against this portfolio is closest to:
   - A. $40M
   - B. $150M
   - C. $190M
   - D. $230M

2. Deposit insurance most directly creates moral hazard because:
   - A. Depositors demand higher rates from riskier banks
   - B. Insured depositors stop monitoring bank risk, allowing banks to take risk with cheap funding
   - C. It forces banks to hold more capital than economically necessary
   - D. It eliminates the bank's credit risk on its loans

3. In a firm-commitment underwriting, a bank buys 20 million shares at $15 and offers them at $16, but can only sell the issue at $14.50. The bank's profit is:
   - A. +$20 million
   - B. −$10 million
   - C. −$20 million
   - D. $0 — the issuer bears the shortfall

4. Which statement about the originate-to-distribute model is most accurate?
   - A. It increases the originating bank's required capital
   - B. It eliminated credit risk from the financial system before 2007
   - C. It weakened originators' incentives to screen borrowers, contributing to the subprime crisis
   - D. It requires loans to be held in the trading book

1. **B** — economic capital covers unexpected loss: $190M − $40M = $150M; expected loss is covered by pricing.
2. **B** — insured depositors have no incentive to monitor or price risk, so risky banks fund cheaply.
3. **B** — loss = 20M × ($14.50 − $15.00) = −$10M; under firm commitment the *bank* bears the risk.
4. **C** — with no retained exposure, screening and monitoring incentives collapse; lending standards fell.
