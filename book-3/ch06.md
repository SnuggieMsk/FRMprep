# 6. Central Clearing

GARP tests CCP mechanics (novation, multilateral netting), the margin stack, the exact order of the default waterfall, and the balanced view of central clearing's advantages and risks.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the mechanics of a central counterparty (CCP): novation, multilateral netting, and the CCP as buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer.
- Describe the regulatory initiatives requiring central clearing of standardized OTC derivatives and their impact.
- Describe advantages and disadvantages of central clearing of OTC derivatives.
- Compare margin requirements in centrally cleared and bilateral markets, and explain how margin mitigates risk.
- Describe the CCP loss waterfall and the order in which resources are consumed after a member default.
- Explain how a CCP manages a clearing member default, including porting and auctioning.
- Identify and explain the types of risks faced by CCPs and the risks to clearing members and non-members.
- Compare principal-to-principal and agency client clearing models.

## CCP mechanics: novation

When a trade is accepted for clearing, **novation** replaces the bilateral contract with two contracts: the **CCP becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer**. Members no longer care about each other's creditworthiness — only the CCP's. The CCP runs a **matched book** (every long offset by a short), so it has no market risk, only **counterparty risk**: if a member defaults, the book is no longer matched.

## Multilateral netting

Bilateral netting compresses exposures between *pairs*; a CCP nets across *all* members.

### Numeric example

Three dealers have bilateral obligations: A owes B $20M, B owes C $30M, and C owes A $40M.

**Bilaterally**, total amounts owed = 20 + 30 + 40 = **$90M** of gross flows, and each dealer carries exposure to a specific counterparty (e.g., A is owed $40M by C).

**Through a CCP**, each dealer nets to a single position against the CCP:

| Dealer | Pays | Receives | Net vs. CCP |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 20 | 40 | receives $20M |
| B | 30 | 20 | pays $10M |
| C | 40 | 30 | pays $10M |

Total flows fall from $90M to $40M (20 + 10 + 10), and no dealer depends on any other specific dealer. Note multilateral netting helps most when exposures are well distributed; it can *reduce* netting benefits if it splits products across multiple CCPs that were previously netted bilaterally under one ISDA agreement.

## The margin stack

| Resource | Who pays | How it is set | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Variation margin (VM)** | Loser each day (or intraday) | Daily mark-to-market change | Settles gains/losses so exposure never accumulates |
| **Initial margin (IM)** | Every member, per portfolio | Covers potential close-out losses over a margin period of risk (e.g., 5 days) at a high confidence level (e.g., 99%+), via SPAN/VaR-type models; rises with volatility and concentration | Buffer against losses between last VM payment and close-out of a defaulter |
| **Default fund contribution** | Every clearing member | Sized to extreme but plausible scenarios — typically the default of the largest one or two members ("Cover 1"/"Cover 2") | Mutualized layer against losses exceeding a defaulter's IM |

Compared with bilateral markets: CCP margining is two-way, zero-threshold, marked daily, and includes IM — historically stricter than typical CSA collateral. Post-crisis rules now also impose IM and VM on non-cleared bilateral trades, narrowing the gap.

Regulatory context: the post-2009 G20/Dodd-Frank/EMIR mandates pushed standardized OTC interest rate swaps and index CDS into CCPs, making CCP risk management — and the waterfall below — central to systemic stability.

## The default waterfall

When a clearing member defaults, losses are absorbed in this exact order:

1. **Defaulting member's initial margin**
2. **Defaulting member's default fund contribution**
3. **CCP's own capital ("skin in the game")** — a pre-funded tranche of CCP equity
4. **Surviving members' default fund contributions** (loss mutualization)
5. **Further assessments / recovery tools** — rights of assessment (additional cash calls on survivors, often capped), variation margin gains haircutting, partial tear-up of contracts; failing all, CCP resolution or failure

{: .warning }
Exam trap: a **non-defaulting** member's initial margin is NOT mutualized — it is returned if that member never defaults and cannot be used to cover another member's losses. Only the **default fund contributions** of survivors (plus assessment rights) are mutualized. IM sits at the top of the waterfall only for the **defaulter**.

## Managing a member default

- **Declare default** and stop the member's trading.
- **Port client positions**: clients of the defaulted member are transferred, with their collateral, to a surviving clearing member (porting works best with segregated client margin and a willing backup member).
- **Hedge the book**: the CCP hedges the defaulter's house positions to neutralize market risk.
- **Auction the book** to surviving members. Incentives to bid: members' default fund contributions can be "juniorized" against poor bids.
- Losses, if any, flow through the waterfall above. This is **loss mutualization**: survivors collectively absorb tail losses, in exchange for the system-wide benefits of clearing.

## Advantages and disadvantages of central clearing

**Advantages**

- **Counterparty risk reduction**: novation, daily two-way margining, multilateral netting, and mutualized default resources.
- **Transparency**: the CCP (and regulators) see positions across members; bilateral webs of exposure are replaced by a hub-and-spoke structure.
- **Liquidity and standardization**: easier position offset; orderly default management replaces chaotic bilateral close-outs (contrast Lehman's cleared book, wound down without loss to the CCP, with its bilateral book).
- Removes the loss-spiral contagion channel of bilateral markets.

**Disadvantages / risks**

- **Concentration of risk**: the CCP is itself a systemically critical single point of failure — "too big to fail" by design.
- **Procyclical margins**: IM models raise requirements when volatility spikes, draining liquidity from members exactly in stressed markets.
- **Moral hazard**: members may monitor counterparties less because the CCP bears the risk; the CCP may underprice risk to win business (race to the bottom among competing CCPs).
- **Model risk**: IM and default fund adequacy depend on margin models that can be wrong in the tails.
- **CCP risks**: default (counterparty) risk, investment risk on posted collateral, liquidity risk (must pay VM on time even while a defaulter's assets are being liquidated), operational and legal risk, sovereign/FMI interdependencies. Historical CCP failures: Paris (1974), Kuala Lumpur (1983), Hong Kong (1987).
- Standardization required for clearing means bespoke trades stay bilateral.

## Bilateral vs. central clearing

| Feature | Bilateral (ISDA/CSA) | Central clearing (CCP) |
|---|---|---|
| Counterparty | Original dealer | CCP after novation |
| Netting | Bilateral, per counterparty | Multilateral, across all members |
| Margin | Negotiated; thresholds possible (now IM/VM required on non-cleared) | Mandatory two-way VM + IM, zero threshold |
| Loss sharing | None — each party bears own losses | Mutualized via default fund and assessments |
| Transparency | Low (improved by trade repositories) | High — CCP sees all cleared positions |
| Products | Anything, including bespoke | Standardized, liquid products only |

## Client clearing models

- **Principal-to-principal (European model)**: the client faces the clearing member, and the member faces the CCP with a back-to-back mirror trade. The member is principal to both contracts.
- **Agency (US FCM model)**: the client's contract is with the CCP; the member acts as agent and **guarantees** the client's performance to the CCP.

In both, client margin segregation (omnibus vs. individual segregation) determines how safely positions can be ported on member default.

{: .tip }
Memorize the waterfall as "defaulter pays first, twice; then the CCP; then everyone else": defaulter's IM, defaulter's default fund, CCP skin in the game, survivors' default fund, assessments. Exam questions hinge on the exact ordering and on which resources belong to whom.

## Quick quiz

1. After a clearing member defaults, its initial margin and default fund contribution are exhausted. The next resource in the default waterfall is:
   - A. Surviving members' initial margin
   - B. The CCP's own capital (skin in the game)
   - C. Surviving members' default fund contributions
   - D. Variation margin gains haircutting

2. A owes B $50M, B owes C $70M, and C owes A $60M. If all positions move to a CCP, B's net position is:
   - A. Pays $20M to the CCP
   - B. Receives $20M from the CCP
   - C. Pays $50M to the CCP
   - D. Receives $10M from the CCP

3. Which statement about loss mutualization at a CCP is correct?
   - A. Non-defaulting members' initial margin can be used to absorb a defaulter's losses
   - B. Only the defaulter's resources can ever be used, so survivors bear no losses
   - C. Survivors' default fund contributions, and possibly further assessments, can absorb a defaulter's losses
   - D. The CCP's skin in the game is the last resource used

4. Procyclicality of CCP initial margin refers to the risk that:
   - A. Margin requirements fall in volatile markets, leaving the CCP underprotected
   - B. Margin requirements rise in volatile markets, draining members' liquidity exactly when funding is scarce
   - C. Margin is only collected once per cycle
   - D. The default fund grows automatically with trading volume

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — Waterfall order: defaulter's IM → defaulter's default fund → CCP skin in the game → survivors' default fund → assessments/recovery tools.
2. **A** — B receives $50M (from A) and pays $70M (to C): net pays $20M.
3. **C** — Mutualization operates through survivors' default fund contributions and assessment rights, never through their initial margin.
4. **B** — Volatility-driven margin models demand more collateral in stress, amplifying liquidity strains (procyclicality).
</details>
