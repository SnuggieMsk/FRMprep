# 7. Futures Markets

GARP tests contract specifications, the mechanics of daily settlement and margin calls (a near-certain computation), open interest vs. volume, order types, and the forwards-vs-futures contrast.

## Learning objectives

- Define and describe the key features and specifications of a futures contract: the underlying asset, contract size, delivery months and locations, price quotes, and price/position limits.
- Explain how positions are opened and closed, and contrast physical delivery with cash settlement, including the mechanics of the delivery process.
- Describe marking to market; compute initial, maintenance, and variation margin and the margin call mechanics.
- Define and interpret trading volume and open interest, and explain how trades affect each.
- Explain the convergence of futures and spot prices, and distinguish normal from inverted futures markets.
- Evaluate the impact of different order types: market, limit, stop, stop-limit, market-if-touched, discretionary, time-of-day, good-till-canceled, fill-or-kill.
- Describe the role of the exchange clearing house and compare margin requirements for hedgers and speculators.
- Compare and contrast forward and futures contracts.

## Contract specifications

The exchange standardizes everything except price:

- **Underlying asset and quality**: for commodities, the deliverable grade(s) — alternatives may be deliverable at premiums/discounts set by the exchange. For financials, the asset is precisely defined (e.g., a specific index, currency amount).
- **Contract size**: the quantity per contract (e.g., 5,000 bushels of corn; 1,000 barrels of oil; $250 × index for the classic S&P 500 contract; 62,500 GBP).
- **Delivery location and months**: where and when delivery can occur; the exchange specifies the delivery months traded and when trading ceases.
- **Price quotes**: units and tick size (minimum price move).
- **Price limits**: daily limit up/down moves trigger trading halts — intended to curb panic moves, but they can delay price discovery.
- **Position limits**: maximum contracts a speculator may hold, to prevent market corners. Bona fide hedgers are typically exempt.

## Opening, closing, and delivery

Most futures positions are **closed out** before maturity by entering the offsetting trade (a long sells the same contract). Fewer than ~2% of contracts end in delivery.

- **Physical delivery**: the **short chooses** when in the delivery period to deliver, what grade, and where (within contract terms). The short files a *notice of intention to deliver* with the exchange, which assigns it to a long (typically the oldest outstanding long).
- **Cash settlement**: contracts on undeliverable underlyings (stock indices, Eurodollar-style rates) settle in cash against the final settlement price; positions are marked to the spot value of the underlying at expiration.
- **Convergence**: as expiration approaches, the futures price converges to the spot price — otherwise arbitrage (buy spot/sell futures and deliver, or the reverse) would be available.

## Marking to market and margin

Every day, the contract is settled at the **settlement price** (the price at or near the close, set by the exchange). Gains are credited and losses debited to the margin account — daily **variation margin** flows mean a futures contract is rewritten at the new price each day, with value reset to zero.

- **Initial margin (IM)**: deposit required to open the position.
- **Maintenance margin (MM)**: minimum balance, typically ~75% of IM.
- If the balance falls **below MM**, a **margin call** requires topping up **to the initial margin** (the shortfall vs. IM is the variation margin deposit). Failure to meet a call means the broker closes the position.
- Balances above IM can be withdrawn; margin accounts often earn interest, and IM (not VM) can often be posted in securities at a haircut.

{: .warning }
Two traps in one: the margin call is triggered when the balance falls **below the maintenance margin** (touching MM exactly is not below it), and the account must be restored **to the INITIAL margin, not to maintenance**. The call amount is initial margin minus current balance.

**Hedgers vs. speculators**: exchanges set **lower margin requirements for bona fide hedgers** (and for day trades and spread positions) because their futures losses are offset by gains on the underlying exposure, so total risk is lower.

**Clearing house role**: through novation it becomes counterparty to every trade, collects margin from clearing members (who collect from clients), nets positions, and guarantees performance — virtually eliminating counterparty risk.

### Worked example: multi-day margin account

An investor goes **long 2 gold futures** (100 oz each, 200 oz total) at $1,800/oz. Initial margin is $12,000; maintenance margin is $9,000.

| Day | Settlement price | Daily gain/(loss) | Margin balance | Margin call |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,800 | — | 12,000 | — |
| 1 | 1,791 | (9) × 200 = (1,800) | 10,200 | — |
| 2 | 1,778 | (13) × 200 = (2,600) | 7,600 → 12,000 | 4,400 |
| 3 | 1,785 | 7 × 200 = 1,400 | 13,400 | — |
| 4 | 1,770 | (15) × 200 = (3,000) | 10,400 | — |
| 5 | 1,775 | 5 × 200 = 1,000 | 11,400 | — |

- Day 2: balance $7,600 < $9,000 MM → call = 12,000 − 7,600 = **$4,400**, restoring the balance to the *initial* margin.
- Day 4: $10,400 is above MM, so no call even though the day's loss was large.
- Check the total: price fell 1,800 → 1,775, so cumulative loss = 25 × 200 = $5,000. Total deposited = 12,000 + 4,400 = 16,400; final balance 11,400; 16,400 − 11,400 = $5,000. Consistent.

### Worked example: volume vs. open interest

Open interest (OI) starts the day at 1,000 contracts. Three trades occur:

1. A (opening a new long) buys 50 from B (opening a new short): OI rises by 50; volume 50.
2. C (closing an existing long) sells 30 to D (opening a new long): OI unchanged; volume 30.
3. E (closing an existing long) sells 20 to F (buying to close an existing short): OI falls by 20; volume 20.

End-of-day OI = 1,000 + 50 + 0 − 20 = **1,030**. Total volume = 50 + 30 + 20 = **100**.

| Buyer | Seller | Open interest | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| New position | New position | +1 per contract | +1 |
| Closing | New position | Unchanged | +1 |
| New position | Closing | Unchanged | +1 |
| Closing | Closing | −1 per contract | +1 |

**Volume** counts contracts traded during a period; **open interest** counts contracts outstanding (total longs = total shorts). Volume can exceed OI on heavy days (e.g., near expiration as positions roll).

## Normal vs. inverted markets

- **Normal market**: futures prices **increase** with maturity (typical when carrying costs exceed benefits of holding the asset).
- **Inverted market**: futures prices **decrease** with maturity (high convenience yield or expected price declines, e.g., tight oil supply).

The settlement price drives daily marking to market; quotes also show open, high, low, change, volume, and open interest by delivery month.

## Order types

| Order | Instruction |
|---|---|
| **Market** | Execute immediately at best available price |
| **Limit** | Execute only at the stated price or better (buy at or below; sell at or above); may never fill |
| **Stop (stop-loss)** | Becomes a **market** order once the stop price is reached; used to cap losses; execution price not guaranteed |
| **Stop-limit** | Becomes a **limit** order once the stop price is reached; may not fill in a fast market |
| **Market-if-touched (MIT)** | Becomes a market order when the price is touched — used to enter at a *favorable* price (opposite trigger side from a stop) |
| **Discretionary (market-not-held)** | Broker may delay execution seeking a better price |
| **Time-of-day** | Execute only during a specified period |
| **Good-till-canceled (open)** | Remains live until executed or canceled (vs. day orders, which expire at the close) |
| **Fill-or-kill** | Execute immediately in full or cancel |

## Forwards vs. futures

| Feature | Forward | Futures |
|---|---|---|
| Trading venue | OTC, bilateral | Exchange |
| Terms | Customized | Standardized |
| Counterparty risk | Yes (dealer credit) | Minimal — clearing house, margining |
| Settlement | Once, at maturity | Daily marking to market |
| Delivery | Usually delivered or cash-settled at maturity | Usually closed out before delivery |
| Liquidity/closeout | Harder — negotiate with counterparty | Easy — offsetting trade |
| Regulation | Lighter (post-crisis: reporting, margin) | Exchange and government regulated |
| Gains/losses | Realized at maturity | Realized daily |

## Quick quiz

1. A trader posts $5,000 initial margin; maintenance margin is $3,750. After losses the balance is $3,200. The margin call is:
   - A. $550
   - B. $1,250
   - C. $1,800
   - D. $3,200

2. A trade in which the buyer is opening a new long and the seller is closing an existing short causes open interest to:
   - A. Increase by the trade size
   - B. Decrease by the trade size
   - C. Remain unchanged
   - D. Increase by twice the trade size

3. An investor long futures at 250 wants to limit losses and is willing to accept execution uncertainty in a fast market but demands the order fire automatically if the price falls to 240. The appropriate order is a:
   - A. Limit sell at 240
   - B. Stop (stop-loss) sell at 240
   - C. Market-if-touched sell at 260
   - D. Fill-or-kill sell at 240

4. In an inverted futures market:
   - A. Futures prices increase with time to maturity
   - B. Futures prices decrease with time to maturity
   - C. The futures price equals the spot price at all maturities
   - D. Daily settlement is suspended

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **C** — Balance ($3,200) is below maintenance ($3,750), so top up to the **initial** margin: 5,000 − 3,200 = $1,800.
2. **C** — One new position and one closing position leave open interest unchanged (volume still rises).
3. **B** — A stop sell becomes a market order at 240, guaranteeing the trigger but not the price; a limit sell at 240 might not execute as the market falls through it.
4. **B** — Inverted (backwardated-style) market: futures prices decline with maturity; a normal market shows prices rising with maturity.
</details>
