# 17. Corporate Bonds

This chapter is mostly definitional — indenture mechanics, bond types, retirement provisions, and the ratings/default/recovery toolkit — with one heavily tested calculation: the **expected return on a defaultable bond**, which is always below the promised yield. Know the vocabulary cold and be able to separate promised, current, and expected measures of return.

## Learning objectives

- Describe a bond indenture and the role of the corporate trustee.
- Define high-yield bonds and describe the high-yield market and types of high-yield issuers and structures.
- Differentiate among types of corporate bonds by collateral (mortgage bonds, collateral trust bonds, equipment trust certificates, debentures, guaranteed bonds) and by maturity (notes vs. bonds).
- Describe the mechanisms by which bonds are retired before maturity: call provisions, sinking funds, tender offers, and defeasance.
- Define credit ratings, describe rating transitions, and explain how default rates and recovery rates are measured.
- Differentiate between credit default risk and credit spread risk, and between a bond's promised yield, current yield, and expected return.

## The indenture and the trustee

The **indenture** is the legal contract between the issuer and bondholders. It specifies the coupon, maturity, collateral, covenants (restrictions on dividends, additional debt, asset sales), and retirement provisions. Because thousands of dispersed bondholders cannot monitor the issuer individually, a **corporate trustee** (typically a bank's trust department) is appointed as a third party to act in the bondholders' interest: it certifies compliance with covenants and takes action on their behalf in default. The trustee acts only on the powers granted in the indenture — it is a watchdog for the contract, not a guarantor of payment.

## Classifying corporate bonds

By **maturity at issue**: **notes** are medium-term (commonly up to 10 years); **bonds** are longer. Medium-term notes (MTNs) are sold continuously through agents rather than underwritten in one block.

By **security (collateral)**:

| Type | Backing |
|---|---|
| **Mortgage bonds** | Lien on specific real assets (property, plant); first-mortgage holders are paid from pledged assets before others |
| **Collateral trust bonds** | Secured by financial assets (stocks, bonds of subsidiaries) deposited with the trustee |
| **Equipment trust certificates** | Title to equipment (aircraft, rolling stock) held by a trustee until the debt is fully paid; very high recovery |
| **Debentures** | **Unsecured** — backed only by the issuer's general credit; subordinated debentures rank below other unsecured debt |
| **Guaranteed bonds** | A third party (often a parent) guarantees payment — credit quality depends on the guarantor, but a guarantee does not eliminate default risk |

{: .note }
"Debenture" means *unsecured* in US usage. A first-mortgage bond of a weak company can still be riskier than a debenture of a strong one — collateral type ranks claims *within* an issuer, not across issuers.

## Retiring bonds before maturity

- **Call provision**: the issuer may redeem at a stated **call price** (typically starting above par and declining toward par at maturity). A deferred call gives investors **call protection** for the first years. A **make-whole call** sets the call price as the present value of remaining cash flows discounted at the Treasury rate plus a small spread — so it is rarely exercised for refunding gain. Calls benefit the **issuer** when rates fall; investors are compensated with a higher yield.
- **Sinking fund**: the issuer must retire a portion of the issue periodically, either by delivering bonds bought in the market or by calling them (usually at par) by lottery. An **accelerated sinking fund** (doubling option) lets the issuer retire more than the scheduled amount — again valuable to the issuer when rates are low. Sinking funds reduce default risk at maturity but expose holders to early redemption at par when the bond trades above par.
- **Tender offers and open-market purchases**: the issuer offers to buy bonds back, often to remove covenants or restructure.
- **Defeasance**: the issuer deposits a portfolio of Treasury securities in trust that replicates the remaining bond cash flows. The bond is then considered economically (and often legally) extinguished and removed from the balance sheet — bondholders are effectively left holding Treasury-collateralized cash flows.

## Credit ratings, default rates, and recovery rates

Ratings agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) grade issues from Aaa/AAA down. The **investment-grade boundary** is Baa3/BBB−; anything below is **high yield (junk)**.

| Band | Moody's | S&P / Fitch |
|---|---|---|
| Investment grade | Aaa, Aa, A, Baa | AAA, AA, A, BBB |
| High yield | Ba, B, Caa, Ca, C | BB, B, CCC, CC, C |
| In default | — | D |

Key empirical regularities:

- **Cumulative default rates** rise with horizon for all ratings, and rise sharply as the rating falls — investment-grade defaults are rare over short horizons; single-B and below default frequently.
- For investment-grade bonds, the *marginal* (hazard) default rate tends to **increase** with time since issue (the issuer can drift down); for low-rated bonds it tends to **decrease** (survivors prove themselves).
- **Rating transitions** matter even without default: a downgrade widens the credit spread and produces a mark-to-market loss — this is **credit spread risk** as opposed to **credit default risk**.
- **Recovery rates** depend on seniority and security: first-mortgage/senior secured debt recovers most, subordinated debt least. Recovery rates are **negatively correlated with default rates** — in recessions defaults are high *and* recoveries are low, compounding losses.
- Default rates can be measured by **issuer count** (number defaulting ÷ number outstanding) or by **dollar amount**; the **default loss rate** ≈ default rate × (1 − recovery rate).

## The high-yield market

High-yield issuers are either **original-issue** junk borrowers (often LBO-related) or **fallen angels** (downgraded from investment grade). Because cash flow is tight early in a leveraged deal, structures often defer cash coupons:

- **Deferred-interest (zero-coupon) bonds** — no coupons for an initial period, sold at deep discount;
- **Step-up bonds** — coupon starts low and increases on a schedule;
- **Payment-in-kind (PIK) bonds** — issuer may pay coupons in additional bonds instead of cash.

## Promised yield, current yield, and expected return

- **Promised yield (YTM)**: the discount rate equating price to *promised* cash flows — it assumes no default and reinvestment at the same rate, so it is the *maximum* you can earn holding to maturity.
- **Current yield**: annual coupon ÷ price. Ignores principal gain/loss and time value entirely.
- **Expected return**: weights each payoff scenario by its probability, including default with recovery. For any defaultable bond the expected return is **below** the promised yield; the gap widens with default probability and with lower recovery.

{: .warning }
The classic trap: a B-rated bond "yielding 9%" does **not** offer an expected return of 9%. The 9% is promised, conditional on full payment. Expected return = probability-weighted payoff ÷ price − 1, and part of the remaining spread over Treasuries compensates for systematic (non-diversifiable) credit risk, not just expected loss. A second trap: recovery is usually quoted as a percentage of **face value**, not of price or of face plus coupon — read the question stem carefully.

### Worked example: expected return on a defaultable bond

A 1-year corporate bond (face $100) pays an 8% annual coupon and trades at par ($100). The probability of default over the year is 4%; in default the bond recovers 40% of face value and pays no coupon.

**Step 1 — payoff in each state.** No default: $$100 + 8 = 108$$. Default: $$0.40 \times 100 = 40$$.

**Step 2 — expected payoff.**

$$
E[\text{payoff}] = 0.96 \times 108 + 0.04 \times 40 = 103.68 + 1.60 = 105.28
$$

**Step 3 — expected return.**

$$
E[r] = \frac{105.28}{100} - 1 = 5.28\%
$$

The promised yield is 8.00%; default risk knocks the expected return down to 5.28%. The 2.72% gap is the expected loss rate, $$0.04 \times (108 - 40)/100 = 2.72\%$$.

### Worked example: current yield vs. yield to maturity

A 10-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon (face $100) and trades at $90.

**Current yield:**

$$
\text{CY} = \frac{6}{90} = 6.67\%
$$

**YTM** solves $$90 = \sum_{t=1}^{10} \frac{6}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{100}{(1+y)^{10}}$$. A quick approximation:

$$
y \approx \frac{C + (F - P)/n}{(F + P)/2} = \frac{6 + 10/10}{95} = \frac{7}{95} = 7.37\%
$$

Trial and error refines this: at $$y = 7.4\%$$ the price is 90.35; at $$y = 7.5\%$$ it is 89.70, so $$y \approx 7.45\%$$. Note the ordering for a **discount** bond: coupon rate (6%) < current yield (6.67%) < YTM (7.45%). For a premium bond the order reverses.

## Quick quiz

1. A 1-year zero-coupon corporate bond (face $100) trades at $92. The probability of default is 5% and recovery in default is 40% of face. The expected return is closest to:
   - A. 8.70%
   - B. 5.43%
   - C. 4.00%
   - D. 2.17%

2. The lowest rating that still qualifies as investment grade is:
   - A. A3 / A−
   - B. Ba1 / BB+
   - C. Baa3 / BBB−
   - D. B1 / B+

3. A company deposits a portfolio of Treasury securities with a trustee that exactly replicates the remaining cash flows of its outstanding bond issue, removing the debt from its balance sheet. This is:
   - A. A sinking fund provision
   - B. A make-whole call
   - C. A tender offer
   - D. Defeasance

4. Which statement about recovery rates is correct?
   - A. They are highest for subordinated debentures because of their higher coupons
   - B. They tend to be negatively correlated with default rates over the credit cycle
   - C. They are fixed at 40% by market convention
   - D. They are independent of seniority within the capital structure

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **B** — expected payoff $$= 0.95 \times 100 + 0.05 \times 40 = 95 + 2 = 97$$; expected return $$= 97/92 - 1 = 5.43\%$$. (A is the *promised* yield, $$100/92 - 1 = 8.70\%$$.)
2. **C** — Baa3 (Moody's) / BBB− (S&P, Fitch) is the investment-grade floor; Ba1/BB+ and below are high yield.
3. **D** — defeasance extinguishes the obligation by trust-funding it with Treasuries; a sinking fund retires bonds gradually with cash, and a make-whole call is a redemption at a PV-based price.
4. **B** — when defaults spike in recessions, asset values are depressed and recoveries fall. Recovery rises with seniority and security (first-mortgage highest, subordinated lowest).
</details>
