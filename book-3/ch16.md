# 16. Properties of Interest Rates

GARP tests rate definitions (and which rate is "risk-free" for derivatives), compounding conversions, bootstrapping zero curves, forward rates, FRA valuation, and the three term-structure theories. Nearly every question here is computational — master the continuous-compounding mechanics.

## Learning objectives

- Describe Treasury rates, LIBOR and its replacement by SOFR and other overnight risk-free rates, repo rates, and OIS rates, and explain what "risk-free rate" means for derivatives pricing.
- Calculate the value of an investment under different compounding frequencies and convert rates between frequencies, including continuous compounding.
- Define spot (zero) rates, calculate bond prices from zeros, and define bond yield and par yield.
- Derive zero rates from coupon bond prices by bootstrapping.
- Derive forward rates from spot rates.
- Calculate the cash flows and value of a forward rate agreement (FRA).
- Compare the theories of the term structure of interest rates.

## Types of rates

| Rate | Description |
|---|---|
| **Treasury** | Yield on government borrowing; conventionally "risk-free" but **too low** for derivatives work because of tax advantages and regulatory/liquidity demand for Treasuries |
| **LIBOR (legacy)** | Unsecured interbank borrowing estimate; embedded bank credit risk; manipulated during the crisis era and **phased out**, replaced by overnight risk-free reference rates |
| **SOFR / overnight RFRs** | Secured Overnight Financing Rate (US), based on actual Treasury repo transactions; SONIA (UK), ESTR (EU), etc. Term rates are built by compounding overnight rates |
| **Repo** | Rate on a sale-and-repurchase: secured borrowing collateralized by securities; very low credit risk |
| **OIS** | Fixed rate in an overnight indexed swap, exchanged for the geometric average of the overnight rate; the standard proxy for the term risk-free rate |

For **derivatives pricing**, the risk-free rate is proxied by **OIS/SOFR-based rates, not Treasury yields** — Treasury yields are artificially depressed by tax and regulatory factors.

## Compounding frequencies and conversions

$100 invested for one year at rate $$R$$ compounded $$m$$ times per year grows to $$100(1 + R/m)^m$$; with continuous compounding it grows to $$100e^{R}$$. Conversions:

$$
r_c = m \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right), \qquad r_m = m\left(e^{r_c/m} - 1\right)
$$

Between two discrete frequencies, equate effective annual growth:

$$
\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right)^m = \left(1 + \frac{r_n}{n}\right)^n
$$

More frequent compounding always means a **lower** quoted rate for the same effective return.

### Worked example: compounding conversions

(a) Convert 10% per annum with semiannual compounding to continuous compounding:

$$
r_c = 2\ln(1 + 0.10/2) = 2\ln(1.05) = 2(0.048790) = 0.097580 = 9.7580\%
$$

(b) Convert 8% continuous to quarterly compounding ($$e^{0.02} = 1.0202$$):

$$
r_m = 4\left(e^{0.08/4} - 1\right) = 4(1.0202 - 1) = 4(0.0202) = 0.0808 = 8.08\%
$$

(c) Convert 12% with monthly compounding to an effective annual rate:

$$
(1.01)^{12} - 1 = 1.126825 - 1 = 12.6825\%
$$

## Zero rates, bond pricing, yield, and par yield

The **$$n$$-year zero (spot) rate** is the rate on an investment with a single cash flow at $$n$$ — no intermediate coupons. A coupon bond is a strip of cash flows, each discounted at its own zero rate. With 1- and 2-year continuous zeros of 4% and 4.5%, a 2-year 6% annual-coupon bond is worth

$$
P = 6e^{-0.04} + 106e^{-0.09} = 6(0.9608) + 106(0.9139) = 5.7648 + 96.8734 = 102.6382
$$

- **Bond yield**: the single discount rate that reproduces the market price when applied to all cash flows.
- **Par yield**: the coupon rate that prices the bond at par. With $$m$$ coupons per year, annuity factor $$A$$ (= sum of discount factors for all coupon dates) and $$d$$ = discount factor for the final maturity:

$$
c = \frac{(100 - 100d)\,m}{A}
$$

Using the zeros above ($$m = 1$$, $$d = e^{-0.09} = 0.9139$$, $$A = 0.9608 + 0.9139 = 1.8747$$): $$c = (100 - 91.39)/1.8747 = 8.61/1.8747 = 4.59$$, i.e., a 2-year par yield of 4.59%.

## Bootstrapping the zero curve

Work from the shortest maturity outward: each new bond adds exactly one unknown zero rate.

### Worked example: bootstrapping two zeros

Bond A is a 1-year zero priced at 96.08 (face 100). Bond B is a 2-year bond with a 5% annual coupon priced at 100.77.

**Step 1 — 1-year zero rate.**

$$
96.08 = 100e^{-R_1 \times 1} \;\Rightarrow\; R_1 = \ln(100/96.08) = \ln(1.0408) = 0.0400 = 4.00\%
$$

**Step 2 — 2-year zero rate.** Bond B pays 5 at year 1 and 105 at year 2 ($$e^{-0.04} = 0.9608$$):

$$
100.77 = 5(0.9608) + 105e^{-2R_2} = 4.8039 + 105e^{-2R_2}
$$

$$
e^{-2R_2} = \frac{100.77 - 4.8039}{105} = \frac{95.9661}{105} = 0.9140
$$

$$
R_2 = -\frac{\ln(0.9140)}{2} = \frac{0.0899}{2} = 0.0450 = 4.50\%
$$

The curve is upward-sloping: $$R_1 = 4.00\%$$, $$R_2 = 4.50\%$$.

## Forward rates

With **continuous compounding**, the forward rate for the period $$[T_1, T_2]$$ implied by spot rates $$R_1, R_2$$ is

$$
R_F = \frac{R_2 T_2 - R_1 T_1}{T_2 - T_1}
$$

If the zero curve is upward-sloping, forward rates lie **above** zero rates (and vice versa).

{: .warning }
This formula requires **continuous compounding**. With annual compounding you must instead solve $$(1+R_2)^{T_2} = (1+R_1)^{T_1}(1+R_F)^{T_2-T_1}$$. Mixing conventions is a deliberate distractor. Second trap: an **FRA settles at $$T_1$$, in advance** — the payment is the interest differential **discounted from $$T_2$$ back to $$T_1$$**; the undiscounted differential is always among the wrong answers.

## Forward rate agreements

An FRA locks in rate $$R_K$$ on principal $$L$$ for the future period $$[T_1, T_2]$$. If $$R_F$$ is today's forward rate for that period (with $$R_K, R_F$$ expressed with compounding matching the period length), the value to the party **receiving fixed** $$R_K$$ is

$$
V = L\,(R_K - R_F)(T_2 - T_1)\,e^{-R_2 T_2}
$$

The pay-fixed (receive-floating) side has the opposite sign: $$V = L(R_F - R_K)(T_2 - T_1)e^{-R_2 T_2}$$. At expiry the realized payment is based on the observed rate $$R_M$$ and settles at $$T_1$$ as $$L(R_K - R_M)(T_2 - T_1)/[1 + R_M(T_2 - T_1)]$$.

### Worked example: forward rate and FRA value

The 1-year and 2-year continuously compounded zero rates are 4% and 5%.

**Step 1 — forward rate for year 2 (continuous):**

$$
R_F = \frac{0.05 \times 2 - 0.04 \times 1}{2 - 1} = \frac{0.10 - 0.04}{1} = 0.06 = 6.00\%
$$

**Step 2 — convert to annual compounding** (the FRA period is one year; $$e^{0.06} = 1.0618$$):

$$
R_F^{annual} = e^{0.06} - 1 = 0.0618 = 6.18\%
$$

**Step 3 — value an FRA to receive 6.5% (annually compounded) on $100 million for year 2** ($$e^{-0.10} = 0.9048$$):

$$
V = 100{,}000{,}000 \times (0.065 - 0.0618) \times 1 \times e^{-0.05 \times 2}
$$

$$
V = 100{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0032 \times 0.9048 = 320{,}000 \times 0.9048 = \$289{,}536
$$

The FRA has positive value because the locked-in receive rate (6.5%) exceeds the forward rate (6.18%).

## Theories of the term structure

| Theory | Claim | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Pure expectations** | Forward rates = expected future spot rates | Curve shape reflects only rate expectations |
| **Market segmentation** | Each maturity bucket has its own supply/demand; rates are set independently | No necessary link between short and long rates |
| **Liquidity preference** | Lenders prefer short maturities, borrowers prefer long; long rates carry a premium | Forward rates are **biased upward** relative to expected future spot rates — explains why curves are usually upward-sloping |

Banks' instinct to lend short and borrow long is reinforced by liquidity preference; matching maturities of assets and liabilities (or pricing long lending higher) manages the resulting interest-rate risk. Sensitivity measures (duration, convexity) quantify that risk but are covered in depth in Book 4.

   - A. 3.25%
   - B. 3.50%
   - C. 4.00%
   - D. 7.00%

3. For pricing derivatives, the best proxy for the risk-free rate is:
   - A. The Treasury yield, since governments cannot default in their own currency
   - B. OIS/SOFR-based rates, since Treasury yields are depressed by tax and regulatory factors
   - C. The prime lending rate
   - D. Legacy LIBOR, since it reflects bank funding costs

4. Under the liquidity preference theory, forward rates:
   - A. Equal expected future spot rates
   - B. Understate expected future spot rates
   - C. Overstate expected future spot rates
   - D. Are unrelated to expected future spot rates
