# 8. Using Futures for Hedging

A perennial multi-question chapter: expect computations on the minimum-variance hedge ratio, the optimal number of contracts, adjusting portfolio beta with index futures, and basis-risk reasoning (who gains when the basis strengthens).

## Learning objectives

- Define and differentiate short and long hedges and identify when each is appropriate.
- Describe the arguments for and against hedging and hedging's potential impact on firm profitability.
- Define the basis and basis risk; explain how basis risk arises when hedging with futures, and calculate the effective price locked in by a hedge.
- Define cross hedging; compute and interpret the minimum-variance hedge ratio and hedge effectiveness.
- Compute the optimal number of futures contracts for a hedge, with and without "tailing the hedge."
- Explain how to use stock index futures to hedge an equity portfolio or to change its beta, and compute the required number of contracts.
- Explain how to create long-term hedges with stack-and-roll strategies and describe the resulting rollover/basis risks.

## Short vs. long hedges

- **Short hedge** (sell futures): appropriate when you **own the asset or will receive/sell it later** — a farmer with a harvest coming, an oil producer, an exporter awaiting foreign currency. Futures gains offset spot price declines.
- **Long hedge** (buy futures): appropriate when you **will need to buy the asset later** — an airline buying jet fuel, a manufacturer buying copper. Futures gains offset spot price increases.

## Arguments for and against hedging

**For**: stabilizes cash flows and margins, lets management focus on the core business, avoids financial distress costs, and prices/budgets can be set with certainty.

**Against**:

- **Shareholders can hedge themselves** (and diversify), often more cheaply — and they may have *chosen* the firm for its commodity exposure.
- **Competitive pressures**: if rivals do not hedge, input costs and output prices move together across the industry and margins are naturally stable; the *hedger's* margins then fluctuate. Hedging when competitors don't can create risk.
- **Explaining futures losses**: if the underlying moves favorably, the hedge loses money. A treasurer can be blamed for a large, visible futures loss even though the combined position performed exactly as designed.

## Basis and basis risk

$$b = S - F$$

The **basis** is spot minus futures (for consumption assets this convention is standard; beware some texts flip it for financial assets). At expiration the basis converges to zero (or to grade/location differentials). Because hedges are usually closed before expiration, the basis at close, $$b_2 = S_2 - F_2$$, is uncertain — this is **basis risk**.

- **Strengthening basis** (basis increases): improves the **short hedger's** position (sells at effectively higher price), hurts the long hedger.
- **Weakening basis**: helps the **long hedger**, hurts the short hedger.

**Effective price locked in** = futures price at initiation + basis at close:

$$\text{Effective price} = F_1 + b_2 = S_2 + (F_1 - F_2)$$

Hedging replaces *price risk* with the (much smaller) *basis risk*. Sources of basis risk: asset mismatch (cross hedging), timing mismatch (hedge horizon vs. delivery month — choose a delivery month shortly *after* the hedge ends), and location/grade differences.

## Cross hedging and the minimum-variance hedge ratio

When the hedged asset differs from the futures underlying (jet fuel hedged with heating oil futures), the hedge ratio that minimizes the variance of the hedged position is

$$h^* = \rho \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}$$

where $$\sigma_S$$ and $$\sigma_F$$ are the standard deviations of *changes* in spot and futures prices over the hedge period, and $$\rho$$ is their correlation. (Equivalently, h* is the slope of a regression of ΔS on ΔF.)

**Optimal number of contracts** for a position of size $$Q_A$$ units with futures contract size $$Q_F$$:

$$N^* = \frac{h^* Q_A}{Q_F}$$

**Hedge effectiveness** is the proportion of variance eliminated:

$$R^2 = \rho^2$$

**Tailing the hedge**: because futures are settled daily, the hedge should be based on **values, not quantities**:

$$N^* = \frac{h^* V_A}{V_F}$$

where $$V_A$$ is the value of the position being hedged and $$V_F$$ = futures price × contract size. Tailing slightly reduces the contract count when interest/daily-settlement effects are recognized.

### Worked example: airline cross hedge

An airline will buy 2,000,000 gallons of jet fuel in one month and hedges with heating oil futures (42,000 gallons per contract). From historical data: $$\sigma_S = 0.0263$$, $$\sigma_F = 0.0313$$ (standard deviations of monthly price changes), $$\rho = 0.928$$.

Step 1 — hedge ratio:

$$h^* = 0.928 \times \frac{0.0263}{0.0313} = 0.928 \times 0.8403 = 0.7798$$

Step 2 — number of contracts:

$$N^* = \frac{0.7798 \times 2{,}000{,}000}{42{,}000} = 0.7798 \times 47.62 = 37.13 \approx 37 \text{ contracts}$$

The airline needs fuel later, so it takes a **long** position in 37 heating oil contracts.

Step 3 — hedge effectiveness: $$R^2 = 0.928^2 = 0.861$$ — about 86.1% of the variance of the fuel cost is eliminated; 13.9% remains as basis risk.

*Tailing check*: with jet fuel spot at $2.00/gal and heating oil futures at $2.10/gal, $$V_A$$ = 2,000,000 × 2.00 = $4,000,000 and $$V_F$$ = 42,000 × 2.10 = $88,200, so $$N^* = 0.7798 \times 4{,}000{,}000 / 88{,}200 = 35.4 \approx 35$$ contracts — slightly fewer than the quantity-based answer.

## Hedging equity portfolios with index futures

To **fully hedge** a portfolio of value P with index futures priced at F (= futures quote × multiplier), **short**:

$$N^* = \beta \frac{P}{F}$$

To **change beta** from $$\beta$$ to a target $$\beta^*$$:

$$N = (\beta^* - \beta)\frac{P}{F}$$

Positive N = **buy (long)** contracts (raising beta); negative N = **sell (short)** contracts (lowering beta). Setting $$\beta^* = 0$$ recovers the full hedge.

**Why hedge rather than sell the portfolio?** Avoids transaction costs and capital gains taxes of liquidation, keeps the carefully selected stocks (the manager wants to keep the alpha but shed market risk temporarily), and is quickly reversible. A fully hedged portfolio earns roughly the risk-free rate (plus any alpha).

### Worked example: adjusting portfolio beta

A manager runs a $24,000,000 portfolio with $$\beta = 1.2$$. Index futures trade at 4,000 with a $250 multiplier, so F = 4,000 × 250 = $1,000,000.

**(a) Full hedge**: $$N^* = 1.2 \times 24{,}000{,}000 / 1{,}000{,}000 = 28.8$$ → **short 29 contracts** (round to nearest whole contract).

**(b) Reduce beta to 0.6**:

$$N = (0.6 - 1.2)\times\frac{24{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} = -0.6 \times 24 = -14.4$$

→ **short 14 contracts**.

**(c) Raise beta to 1.8**: $$N = (1.8 - 1.2) \times 24 = +14.4$$ → **long 14 contracts**.

### Worked example: effective price via the basis

In May, a producer expecting to sell a commodity in August shorts September futures at $$F_1 = 2.50$$. In August, spot is $$S_2 = 2.30$$ and the September futures price is $$F_2 = 2.20$$, so the basis at close is $$b_2 = 2.30 - 2.20 = 0.10$$.

- Futures gain = 2.50 − 2.20 = 0.30 per unit.
- Effective sale price = spot + futures gain = 2.30 + 0.30 = **2.60**, which equals $$F_1 + b_2 = 2.50 + 0.10$$. ✔

The producer locked in $$F_1$$ plus whatever the basis turns out to be — only $$b_2$$ was uncertain.

{: .warning }
Direction and ratio traps: (1) **Reducing** beta (or fully hedging a long portfolio) means **shorting** contracts — a positive count in the formula output means *buy*, negative means *sell*; read the sign. (2) The hedge ratio is $$h^* = \rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F$$ — spot volatility on **top**. Answer choices built from $$\rho\,\sigma_F/\sigma_S$$ are standard distractors. (3) A **strengthening** basis benefits the **short** hedger, not the long.

## Stack and roll

A long-dated exposure can be hedged with liquid **short-dated** futures: take the full position ("stack") in a near contract, then close it just before delivery and re-establish it in the next contract ("roll"). Each roll exposes the hedger to **rollover basis risk** — the price difference between the expiring and new contracts is unknown in advance.

A second danger is **cash-flow timing**: futures losses trigger immediate margin calls, while offsetting gains on the underlying long-term position are unrealized.

{: .note }
**Metallgesellschaft (1993)**: hedged long-term fixed-price oil supply contracts by stacking and rolling short-dated long futures; when oil prices fell, massive margin calls on the futures created a funding crisis (the offsetting gains on the supply contracts were distant and unrealized), and unwinding the hedge locked in roughly $1.3 billion of losses.

## Quick quiz

1. Monthly changes: $$\sigma_S = 0.04$$, $$\sigma_F = 0.05$$, $$\rho = 0.85$$. The minimum-variance hedge ratio is closest to:
   - A. 0.85
   - B. 1.06
   - C. 0.68
   - D. 0.43

2. A $50 million portfolio has beta 1.4. Index futures trade at 5,000 with a $250 multiplier. To cut beta to 0.8 the manager should:
   - A. Buy 24 contracts
   - B. Sell 24 contracts
   - C. Sell 56 contracts
   - D. Buy 40 contracts

3. A short hedger benefits when:
   - A. The basis weakens unexpectedly
   - B. The basis strengthens unexpectedly
   - C. The futures price rises more than spot
   - D. Basis risk is eliminated by daily settlement

4. A short hedge is initiated at a futures price of 60. At close-out, spot is 53 and the futures price is 55. The effective sale price is:
   - A. 53
   - B. 55
   - C. 58
   - D. 60

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **C** — h* = 0.85 × (0.04/0.05) = 0.85 × 0.8 = 0.68. (1.06 uses the inverted ratio — the classic distractor.)
2. **B** — F = 5,000 × 250 = $1,250,000; N = (0.8 − 1.4) × 50,000,000/1,250,000 = −0.6 × 40 = −24 → sell 24 contracts.
3. **B** — Effective price for a short hedger = F1 + b2; a stronger (higher) basis at close raises the effective sale price.
4. **C** — b2 = 53 − 55 = −2; effective price = F1 + b2 = 60 − 2 = 58 (equivalently 53 + futures gain of 5).
</details>
