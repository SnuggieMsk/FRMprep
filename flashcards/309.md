**Q1.** In the quote EUR/USD = 1.1000, which is the base currency and what does the number mean?
> **A:** EUR (listed first) is the base currency; 1 EUR costs 1.1000 USD. The price is always expressed in the quote currency (USD).

**Q2.** Distinguish direct from indirect quotes.
> **A:** Direct quote: domestic currency per unit of foreign currency (US investor quoting USD per EUR). Indirect quote: foreign currency per unit of domestic currency (the reciprocal).

**Q3.** What is a pip?
> **A:** The smallest standard price increment — usually the 4th decimal (0.0001) for most pairs, the 2nd decimal for JPY pairs. A move from 1.1000 to 1.1025 is 25 pips.

**Q4.** What is an FX swap?
> **A:** A single contract with two legs: a spot exchange combined with a reverse forward exchange. It is the most heavily traded FX instrument, economically a collateralized borrowing of one currency against another; used for rolling hedges and short-term funding.

**Q5.** Compute the implied cross rate: USD/GBP = 1.2500 and USD/EUR = 1.1000.
> **A:** EUR per GBP = $$\frac{1.2500}{1.1000} = 1.1364$$. If a dealer quotes a different cross, triangular arbitrage (converting through three currencies and back) locks in a riskless profit.

**Q6.** Distinguish transaction, translation, and economic FX risk.
> **A:** Transaction: known future foreign-currency cash flows change domestic value. Translation: accounting exposure from consolidating foreign assets/liabilities. Economic: long-run competitive exposure of future revenues and costs to FX moves.

**Q7.** How is each FX risk hedged?
> **A:** Transaction risk: forwards, futures, options, money-market hedges. Translation risk: finance foreign assets with liabilities in the same currency so gains/losses offset. Economic risk: operational diversification of production, sourcing, and sales across currencies.

**Q8.** Trap: why is hedging translation risk with derivatives often speculative?
> **A:** If the foreign assets are not actually going to be sold, the derivative produces real cash gains/losses against a paper accounting exposure. The sound hedge is matched-currency financing.

**Q9.** State purchasing power parity and the real exchange rate.
> **A:** Real rate: $$E_{real} = E_{nominal} \times P_{foreign}/P_{domestic}$$. PPP says nominal rates adjust over time to offset inflation differentials, keeping the real rate constant — a good long-run anchor but a poor short-run predictor.

**Q10.** State covered interest rate parity (CIP).
> **A:** With S, F as domestic per unit of foreign: $$F = S e^{(r - r_f)T}$$ (continuous) or $$F = S\frac{(1+r)^T}{(1+r_f)^T}$$ (discrete). CIP is enforced by arbitrage — a riskless round trip exists if it is violated.

**Q11.** Under CIP, does the higher-interest-rate currency trade at a forward premium or discount?
> **A:** A forward discount — it depreciates forward. Its rate advantage must be given back through the forward, or arbitrage exists.

**Q12.** Spot USD/GBP = 1.3000, USD rate 3%, GBP rate 5% (continuous). No-arbitrage 1-year forward?
> **A:** $$F = 1.3000\,e^{(0.03-0.05)\times1} = 1.3000 \times 0.9802 = 1.2743$$. GBP (higher rate) is at a forward discount. A market forward of 1.2900 is too high → sell GBP forward and carry it for riskless profit.

**Q13.** State uncovered interest rate parity (UIP) and why it is not enforced.
> **A:** $$E[S_T] = S e^{(r - r_f)T}$$ — it replaces the forward with the expected future spot. Nothing enforces it (no arbitrage in an expectation), and empirically high-rate currencies don't depreciate as much as UIP predicts.

**Q14.** What is the carry trade and its risk profile?
> **A:** Borrow the low-rate currency, invest in the high-rate currency, earn the differential — exploiting UIP's empirical failure. It earns steadily in calm markets but suffers severe losses when the funding currency appreciates sharply: "up the stairs, down the elevator."
