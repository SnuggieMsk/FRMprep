**Q1.** The six factors affecting option price and their direction for a European call
> **A:** Increase in: stock price $$S_0$$ (+), strike $$K$$ (−), time $$T$$ (ambiguous), volatility $$\sigma$$ (+), risk-free rate $$r$$ (+), dividends $$D$$ (−).

**Q2.** Direction of the six factors for a European put
> **A:** Increase in: stock price $$S_0$$ (−), strike $$K$$ (+), time $$T$$ (ambiguous), volatility $$\sigma$$ (+), risk-free rate $$r$$ (−), dividends $$D$$ (+).

**Q3.** Why is the effect of time ambiguous for European options but never negative for American?
> **A:** For European options a large dividend before the longer maturity can make a shorter-dated call worth more, and a deep-ITM European put may prefer a shorter wait. American options never decrease in value with $$T$$ — a longer option contains every right of the shorter one.

**Q4.** Why does a higher risk-free rate help calls and hurt puts?
> **A:** Higher $$r$$ raises the expected growth rate of the stock and lowers the PV of the strike — both help calls and both hurt puts. Dividends do the opposite (stock drops on ex-date): calls fall, puts rise.

**Q5.** Upper bounds on option prices
> **A:** $$c \le S_0$$, $$C \le S_0$$ (a call gives at most the stock); $$p \le Ke^{-rT}$$, $$P \le K$$ (a put gives at most $$K$$).

**Q6.** Lower bounds for European options on a non-dividend stock
> **A:** $$c \ge \max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},\, 0)$$ and $$p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\, 0)$$.

**Q7.** Lower bounds with dividends (PV = $$D$$)
> **A:** $$c \ge \max(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT},\, 0)$$ and $$p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} + D - S_0,\, 0)$$. Dividends loosen the call bound and tighten the put bound.

**Q8.** Put-call parity (European, non-dividend and with dividends)
> **A:** Non-dividend: $$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$. With dividends PV $$D$$: $$c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$. Both portfolios pay $$\max(S_T, K)$$ at $$T$$.

**Q9.** Put-call inequality for American options
> **A:** $$S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$$ (set $$D=0$$ for the non-dividend case). For American options you can only bound $$C-P$$, not pin it down.

**Q10.** Trap: applying put-call parity to American options
> **A:** Put-call parity holds for EUROPEAN options only. The classic trap supplies American prices and asks you to plug into the parity equation — you may only use the inequality. Second trap: dividends LOWER calls and RAISE puts, and $$D$$ is added to the CALL side of parity.

**Q11.** Why is an American call on a non-dividend stock never exercised early ($$C = c$$)?
> **A:** (1) Exercising pays $$K$$ now, forfeiting interest on $$K$$. (2) You throw away downside insurance the call provides. (3) $$C \ge c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} > S_0 - K$$, so the call is worth more alive (sell it) than exercised.

**Q12.** When can early exercise of an American put or call be optimal?
> **A:** American put: early exercise can be optimal ($$P > p$$ possible), more attractive when $$S_0$$ is low, $$r$$ is high, and $$\sigma$$ is low (receiving $$K$$ early earns interest). American call: only immediately before an ex-dividend date, when the dividend captured exceeds time value plus interest forgone.

**Q13.** Worked: put-call parity arbitrage strategy
> **A:** If $$p + S_0 > c + Ke^{-rT}$$, the put-plus-stock side is overpriced: buy the cheap side (buy the call), sell the rich side (short the put, short the stock). Park the net inflow at $$r$$; at $$T$$ you pay exactly $$K$$ for the stock either way, locking a riskless profit.

**Q14.** Worked: lower-bound violation arbitrage
> **A:** If $$c < S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$$ the call is too cheap: buy the call, short the stock, invest the inflow $$S_0 - c$$ at $$r$$. At $$T$$, exercise if $$S_T > K$$ (buy at $$K$$) or buy in the market if $$S_T \le K$$ — riskless profit at least $$(S_0-c)e^{rT} - K$$.
