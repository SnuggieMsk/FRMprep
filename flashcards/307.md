**Q1.** What does the exchange standardize in a futures contract, and what is left to the market?
> **A:** The exchange standardizes everything — underlying asset/quality, contract size, delivery location and months, price quotes/tick size, price limits, and position limits — except the price, which is set by trading.

**Q2.** In physical delivery, who chooses the timing, grade, and location?
> **A:** The short. The short files a notice of intention to deliver with the exchange, which assigns it to a long (typically the oldest outstanding long).

**Q3.** What triggers a margin call and how much must be deposited?
> **A:** A call is triggered when the account balance falls below the maintenance margin (MM). The account must then be topped up to the initial margin (IM), not to MM. Call amount = IM − current balance.

**Q4.** Trap: balance touches MM exactly, or a large loss leaves the balance above MM. Is there a call?
> **A:** No call. The call fires only when the balance falls below MM; touching MM exactly is not below it, and a big daily loss that still leaves the balance above MM triggers nothing.

**Q5.** Long 2 gold futures (200 oz) at $1,800; IM $12,000, MM $9,000. Day 2 settles at $1,778. Margin call?
> **A:** Loss from prior day ($1,791) = (1,778−1,791)×200 = −$2,600 → balance $7,600 < $9,000 MM. Call = 12,000 − 7,600 = $4,400, restoring to the initial margin.

**Q6.** Why do exchanges set lower margins for bona fide hedgers?
> **A:** Their futures losses are offset by gains on the underlying exposure, so total risk is lower. Day trades and spread positions also get lower requirements.

**Q7.** Define trading volume vs open interest.
> **A:** Volume counts contracts traded during a period; open interest counts contracts outstanding (total longs = total shorts). Volume can exceed OI on heavy days such as expiration rolls.

**Q8.** How does each trade type affect open interest?
> **A:** New buyer + new seller: OI +1. Closing + new (either side): OI unchanged. Closing buyer + closing seller: OI −1. Volume rises by the trade size in every case.

**Q9.** What is the convergence property of futures prices?
> **A:** As expiration approaches, the futures price converges to the spot price; otherwise buy-spot/sell-futures (or the reverse) arbitrage would be available.

**Q10.** Distinguish a normal from an inverted futures market.
> **A:** Normal: futures prices increase with maturity (carrying costs exceed holding benefits). Inverted: futures prices decrease with maturity (high convenience yield or expected price declines, e.g., tight oil supply).

**Q11.** Contrast a stop order with a stop-limit order.
> **A:** A stop (stop-loss) becomes a market order once the stop price is reached — the trigger is guaranteed but not the execution price. A stop-limit becomes a limit order at the stop price and may not fill in a fast market.

**Q12.** What is a market-if-touched (MIT) order?
> **A:** It becomes a market order when the price is touched, used to enter at a favorable price — the opposite trigger side from a stop order.

**Q13.** Define fill-or-kill and good-till-canceled orders.
> **A:** Fill-or-kill: execute immediately in full or cancel. Good-till-canceled (open): stays live until executed or canceled, unlike day orders that expire at the close.

**Q14.** Key differences between forwards and futures.
> **A:** Forwards: OTC, customized, dealer credit risk, settle once at maturity, usually delivered, harder to close out, lighter regulation. Futures: exchange-traded, standardized, minimal counterparty risk via clearing house and margining, daily marking to market, usually closed out before delivery, easy offset, heavily regulated.
