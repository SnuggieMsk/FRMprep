**Q1.** How does stress testing differ from VaR/ES?
> **A:** VaR/ES ask "how bad over a typical period at probability α?" (probabilistic, short horizon, sample-based). Stress testing asks "what happens in this specific extreme scenario?" (usually no probability, long horizon, captures unprecedented events).

**Q2.** Are VaR and stress testing substitutes or complements?
> **A:** Complements. VaR backtests well but is blind beyond the sample; stress tests probe the tail but carry no likelihood. Backward-looking VaR failed in 2007 because the prior sample was calm.

**Q3.** What is stressed VaR/ES?
> **A:** VaR/ES computed with parameters (vols, correlations) calibrated to a crisis window (e.g., 2008). Basel 2.5 added stressed VaR; FRTB's ES is calibrated to the worst observed period — blending the two frameworks.

**Q4.** Contrast historical and hypothetical scenarios.
> **A:** Historical: replay actual crises (1987, 1998, 2008, 2020) — coherent and objective but the next crisis is never the last. Hypothetical: constructed shocks — forward-looking and tailored, but subjective and hard to keep coherent.

**Q5.** What does scenario "coherence" require?
> **A:** All variables move in an internally consistent way — you can't shock equities −40% while leaving credit spreads and volatility unchanged. Set non-core variables via conditional relationships or macro models.

**Q6.** Explain the severity-vs-plausibility trade-off.
> **A:** Scenarios must be severe but plausible. Too mild → false comfort; too wild → dismissed by management and distorts capital allocation. "A more severe scenario is always better" is false.

**Q7.** Define reverse stress testing.
> **A:** Start from the failure outcome ("what would breach capital minima / make us fail?") and search backward for scenarios that produce it. Forces discovery of hidden vulnerabilities; required by UK/EU regulators for recovery/resolution planning.

**Q8.** What governance is expected for stress testing?
> **A:** Board/senior management own the program (approve framework and severity, act on results — the "use test"). Independent validation challenges models and coherence; internal audit reviews the process end-to-end.

**Q9.** What is CCAR/DFAST and what does it constrain?
> **A:** Annual Fed supervisory stress tests of large US banks (baseline/severely adverse) projecting capital ratios over 9 quarters. Results gate capital distributions (dividends/buybacks) via the stress capital buffer.

**Q10.** Distinguish capital and liquidity stress tests.
> **A:** Capital tests ask whether equity survives losses; liquidity tests (LCR-style survival horizons) ask whether cash outflows can be met under funding runs. 2008 and 2023 (SVB) showed liquidity can kill before solvency.

**Q11.** Why include knock-on/second-round effects?
> **A:** Fire sales, funding runs, and counterparty failures made 2008 worse than first-round losses. Good scenarios capture these system-wide feedback effects.

**Q12.** Is there a fixed ordering between a 99% ES and a stress loss?
> **A:** No — the stress loss can exceed or fall short of ES; they answer different questions. Common trap statements: "stress tests assign probabilities" (false) and "stress testing replaces VaR" (false).

**Q13.** What is the disclosure debate around regulatory stress tests?
> **A:** Publishing results aids market discipline (the 2009 SCAP restored confidence) but may encourage teaching-to-the-test and herding into identically-measured risks.
