**Q1.** Corporate governance vs. risk governance?
> **A:** Corporate governance is the system by which a firm is directed and controlled for stakeholders. Risk governance is the subset dealing with how risk taking is directed, constrained, and overseen.

**Q2.** What is the board's role in risk — oversight or management?
> **A:** Oversight, not management. The board sets appetite, approves strategy and key policies, ensures capable management and sound incentives, and monitors — it does not run the risk function day to day.

**Q3.** Name key post-crisis (2007–09) governance/regulation changes.
> **A:** Dodd-Frank (incl. Volcker rule), Basel III (more capital, leverage ratio, LCR/NSFR), mandatory stress testing (CCAR), board risk committees, CRO elevation, and compensation reform (deferral, clawback/malus).

**Q4.** Why must risk appetite and business strategy be set jointly?
> **A:** A growth strategy implies risk consequences, and an appetite constraint shapes which strategies are feasible. A disconnect (conservative appetite, aggressive targets) gets resolved on the trading floor toward growth.

**Q5.** Why are incentives the "transmission belt" of risk governance?
> **A:** If bonuses pay on revenue or short-term P&L with no risk adjustment, employees push the risk profile beyond appetite regardless of policy. Fix with risk-adjusted measures (RAROC), deferral, clawbacks.

**Q6.** What are the three lines of defense?
> **A:** 1st: business lines own and manage the risk they generate. 2nd: risk management & compliance set the framework, measure independently, challenge. 3rd: internal audit independently assures lines 1 and 2.

**Q7.** Exam trap: who "owns" the risk in the three-lines model?
> **A:** The business line (first line) owns its risks. Risk management (second line) measures, monitors, and challenges but does not own the risk. Internal audit (third line) audits the framework and stays independent.

**Q8.** What is the CRO's appropriate reporting line?
> **A:** Independent, with direct access/reporting to the board or its risk committee — not buried under the business or the CFO.

**Q9.** What is the primary responsibility of the audit committee?
> **A:** Overseeing the integrity of financial reporting, the control environment, and the internal/external audit relationship. It oversees internal audit but does not run its day-to-day testing.

**Q10.** Risk committee vs. audit committee?
> **A:** The risk committee owns risk-appetite oversight and forward-looking risk; the audit committee owns financial-reporting integrity, controls assurance, and internal/external audit.

**Q11.** Which committee oversees ALCO-type concerns, and what does ALCO cover?
> **A:** ALCO is a management-level committee covering balance-sheet, interest-rate, and liquidity risk; it sits within the governance transmission of appetite, alongside credit and operational risk committees.

**Q12.** Why does a risk number depend on functional interdependence?
> **A:** A risk number is only as good as the trade capture, valuation, and data plumbing beneath it — depending on front office data, operations/IT, finance/treasury valuations, and compliance.

**Q13.** What is the purpose of clawback and deferral provisions?
> **A:** To align pay with long-run, risk-adjusted outcomes rather than short-term, risk-blind returns.

**Q14.** Which back-office failure enabled Barings, and what governance lesson follows?
> **A:** Absence of an independent back office to confirm and settle trades let a trader control both trading and settlement. Lesson: segregate front and back office so positions are independently verified.
