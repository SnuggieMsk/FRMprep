**Q1.** Name the traditional credit risk mitigants.
> **A:** Purchasing insurance/guarantees/letters of credit, netting, marking to market, collateralization, and termination/put options (e.g., downgrade triggers).

**Q2.** How does a credit default swap (CDS) work?
> **A:** The protection buyer pays a periodic spread; on a defined credit event (bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring) of the reference entity, the seller pays par minus recovery. Economically: insurance on a bond without owning it.

**Q3.** Single-name CDS vs. index CDS vs. first-to-default basket?
> **A:** Single-name: one reference entity. Index (CDX, iTraxx): standardized baskets. First-to-default: seller covers the first default in a small basket — cheaper than buying CDS on every name.

**Q4.** What does a total return swap (TRS) transfer?
> **A:** Both credit and market risk. One party pays the total return (coupons + price change) of a reference asset and receives floating + spread; the receiver gets the asset's full economics without funding it.

**Q5.** Why does a credit-linked note (CLN) leave the protection buyer with no counterparty risk?
> **A:** It is funded — the investor pays cash upfront for a note whose principal is written down on default, so the protection buyer already holds the money.

**Q6.** Which instruments are funded, and what does the protection buyer's counterparty risk look like for each (CDS, TRS, CLN)?
> **A:** CDS: unfunded, counterparty risk yes (seller may fail). TRS: unfunded, counterparty risk yes. CLN: funded, counterparty risk no.

**Q7.** Exam trap: what risk does a CDS create, and what is wrong-way risk?
> **A:** A CDS transfers credit risk but creates counterparty risk on the seller. Wrong-way risk: the seller's health is correlated with the reference credit (AIG/monolines insuring mortgage CDOs), so the hedge fails when needed.

**Q8.** What is a special purpose vehicle (SPV) in securitization?
> **A:** A bankruptcy-remote entity that buys the asset pool in a true sale, isolating the assets from the originator's insolvency, and issues tranches against the pool's cash flows.

**Q9.** How do losses and cash flows flow through securitization tranches?
> **A:** Losses flow bottom-up (equity/first-loss, then mezzanine, then senior); cash flows top-down — the waterfall. Senior has first claim, lowest yield, highest rating.

**Q10.** Name forms of credit enhancement in a securitization.
> **A:** Subordination, overcollateralization, excess spread, and reserve accounts.

**Q11.** Originate-to-distribute vs. buy-and-hold — incentive difference?
> **A:** Buy-and-hold gives a strong incentive to screen/monitor borrowers (bank eats its losses). OTD weakens it — the "skin in the game" problem and volume-driven underwriting; risk passes to investors.

**Q12.** What is pipeline/warehouse risk in the OTD model?
> **A:** The risk that loans get stuck on the originator's balance sheet when securitization markets close, plus moral hazard and reputational/legal risk from bad originations.

**Q13.** What post-crisis reform targeted the OTD incentive problem?
> **A:** Risk retention ("skin in the game") rules requiring originators/sponsors to keep a slice (e.g., 5%) of securitized deals.

**Q14.** Name the key post-crisis changes to the credit derivatives market.
> **A:** Central clearing of standardized CDS via CCPs, trade reporting, standardization (Big Bang protocol: fixed coupons, auction settlement, determinations committees), margin for non-cleared trades, and CVA capital charges.
