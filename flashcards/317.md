**Q1.** Bond indenture and the role of the corporate trustee
> **A:** The indenture is the legal contract specifying coupon, maturity, collateral, covenants, and retirement provisions. The trustee (usually a bank trust department) monitors covenant compliance and acts for bondholders in default — a watchdog for the contract, not a guarantor of payment.

**Q2.** Notes vs. bonds (by maturity at issue)
> **A:** Notes are medium-term (commonly up to 10 years); bonds are longer. Medium-term notes (MTNs) are sold continuously through agents rather than underwritten in one block.

**Q3.** Corporate bond types by collateral
> **A:** Mortgage bonds (lien on real assets), collateral trust bonds (financial assets with trustee), equipment trust certificates (title to equipment, very high recovery), debentures (unsecured), guaranteed bonds (third-party guarantee).

**Q4.** What "debenture" means — classic trap
> **A:** In US usage a debenture is unsecured, backed only by general credit; subordinated debentures rank below other unsecured debt. Collateral type ranks claims within an issuer, not across issuers — a first-mortgage bond of a weak firm can be riskier than a debenture of a strong one.

**Q5.** Call provision and make-whole call
> **A:** Issuer may redeem at a stated call price (declining toward par). A make-whole call sets the price as PV of remaining cash flows at the Treasury rate plus a small spread, so it is rarely exercised for refunding. Calls benefit the issuer when rates fall; investors get a higher yield.

**Q6.** Sinking fund
> **A:** Issuer must retire part of the issue periodically (buy in market or call at par by lottery). An accelerated/doubling option lets it retire extra — valuable to the issuer when rates are low. Reduces maturity default risk but exposes holders to early redemption at par.

**Q7.** Defeasance
> **A:** Issuer deposits a portfolio of Treasuries in trust replicating the remaining bond cash flows; the bond is economically/legally extinguished and removed from the balance sheet. Holders effectively hold Treasury-collateralized cash flows.

**Q8.** Investment-grade boundary
> **A:** Baa3 (Moody's) / BBB− (S&P, Fitch). Anything below is high yield (junk): Ba/BB and lower.

**Q9.** Credit default risk vs. credit spread risk
> **A:** Default risk = loss from the issuer failing to pay. Spread risk = mark-to-market loss when a downgrade or spread widening occurs without default. A rating transition causes spread risk even absent default.

**Q10.** Recovery rates — key empirical facts
> **A:** Rise with seniority/security (first-mortgage highest, subordinated lowest) and are negatively correlated with default rates: in recessions defaults are high and recoveries low, compounding losses. Default loss rate ≈ default rate × (1 − recovery rate).

**Q11.** High-yield structures that defer cash coupons
> **A:** Deferred-interest (zero-coupon) bonds, step-up bonds (coupon rises on schedule), and payment-in-kind (PIK) bonds (coupons paid in additional bonds). Issuers are original-issue junk borrowers or fallen angels.

**Q12.** Promised yield vs. current yield vs. expected return
> **A:** Promised yield (YTM) discounts promised cash flows assuming no default — the maximum if held to maturity. Current yield = coupon ÷ price. Expected return weights payoffs by probability including default/recovery and is always below the promised yield for a defaultable bond.

**Q13.** Expected return on a defaultable bond
> **A:** $$E[r] = \frac{E[\text{payoff}]}{\text{Price}} - 1$$, where $$E[\text{payoff}]$$ weights the no-default payoff (face + coupon) and the default payoff (recovery × face) by their probabilities. Recovery is usually quoted as a percent of face value.

**Q14.** Yield ordering for a discount bond
> **A:** Coupon rate < current yield < YTM. For a premium bond the order reverses. Quick YTM approximation: $$y \approx \frac{C + (F-P)/n}{(F+P)/2}$$.
