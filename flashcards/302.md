**Q1.** Term life vs. whole life insurance
> **A:** Term: pays only if death occurs within a fixed term; pure mortality protection, no investment component, cheapest. Whole life: lifetime coverage with level premiums that overpay early, building a cash surrender value plus a tax-advantaged investment component.

**Q2.** What are annuities and what risk do they expose the insurer to?
> **A:** The reverse of life insurance — a lump sum converted into income for life. The insurer loses if the annuitant lives longer than expected (longevity risk).

**Q3.** Mortality risk vs. longevity risk for a life insurer
> **A:** Mortality risk: policyholders die earlier than expected (hurts life policies). Longevity risk: they live longer (hurts annuities). The two partially offset — a natural hedge.

**Q4.** How do conditional probabilities chain in a mortality table?
> **A:** The probability a person aged $$x$$ dies in the second year is $$(1-q_x)\,q_{x+1}$$ — survive year one, then die. E.g., $$q_{70}=0.02, q_{71}=0.022$$: $$(0.98)(0.022)=0.0216$$.

**Q5.** Breakeven term-life premium — setup
> **A:** Set PV(premiums) = PV(expected payouts). Payouts discounted at death probabilities (deaths paid end of year); the second premium is contingent on year-1 survival, so multiply it by $$(1-q_x)$$. Actual premiums exceed breakeven to cover expenses and profit.

**Q6.** Adverse selection vs. moral hazard
> **A:** Adverse selection occurs before the contract — high-risk individuals are most eager to buy, worsening the pool. Moral hazard occurs after — being insured changes behavior toward less care/more risk. Mitigate adverse selection with underwriting (exams, risk-based pricing); mitigate moral hazard with deductibles, coinsurance, limits, no-claims discounts.

**Q7.** P&C combined ratio
> **A:** $$\text{Combined ratio} = \text{loss ratio} + \text{expense ratio}$$ (both as % of premiums earned). Combined after dividends = combined ratio + policyholder dividends.

**Q8.** P&C operating ratio
> **A:** $$\text{Operating ratio} = \text{combined ratio after dividends} - \text{investment income ratio}$$. Sign trap: dividends are added, investment income is subtracted.

**Q9.** Exam trap: does a combined ratio above 100% mean the insurer is unprofitable?
> **A:** No. Subtract investment income (earned on the float — premiums held before claims are paid) to get the operating ratio. E.g., 102% combined after dividends − 8% investment income = 94% operating, profitable.

**Q10.** What is a CAT bond?
> **A:** A catastrophe bond lets a P&C insurer transfer extreme tail risk to capital markets: it pays a high coupon, but interest and/or principal is forgiven if a defined catastrophe loss exceeds a trigger. Investors gain diversification (CAT losses uncorrelated with markets); for the insurer it is an alternative to reinsurance.

**Q11.** How does insurer regulation differ from bank regulation in the US, and the guaranty system from deposit insurance?
> **A:** US insurers are regulated by states (coordinated by NAIC), not federally. On failure, state guaranty associations cover policyholders via post-insolvency assessments on surviving insurers. The FDIC, by contrast, is a permanent, pre-funded fund from ongoing bank premiums. Insurance failures are slower (no runs), so after-the-fact levies work.

**Q12.** Mutual vs. stock insurance company
> **A:** Mutual: owned by policyholders, no shareholders, profits accrue to policyholders, no owner-policyholder conflict but limited capital access. Stock: owned by shareholders, easier capital raising but a potential owner-policyholder conflict. Many mutuals have demutualized.

**Q13.** Defined benefit vs. defined contribution pensions — who bears the risk?
> **A:** DB: formula-based income; the employer bears investment and longevity risk and must fund deficits. DC: payout = contributions + investment performance; the employee bears investment and longevity risk. DC is more portable; the trend is decisively toward DC.

**Q14.** What is Solvency II's two-level capital structure?
> **A:** The European insurer framework sets a minimum capital requirement (MCR) and a higher solvency capital requirement (SCR), with graduated supervisory intervention between the two levels.
