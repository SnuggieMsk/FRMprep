**Q1.** Call vs put; American vs European
> **A:** A call gives the right to buy at strike $$K$$; a put the right to sell at $$K$$. American options can be exercised any time up to expiration; European only at expiration. Most exchange-traded stock options are American. The buyer pays a premium; the writer receives it and bears the obligation.

**Q2.** Payoff and profit of the four basic positions
> **A:** Long call payoff $$\max(S_T-K,0)$$, profit minus $$c$$. Short call payoff $$-\max(S_T-K,0)$$, profit plus $$c$$. Long put payoff $$\max(K-S_T,0)$$, profit minus $$p$$. Short put payoff $$-\max(K-S_T,0)$$, profit plus $$p$$.

**Q3.** Moneyness for calls and puts
> **A:** ITM: call $$S>K$$, put $$S<K$$. ATM: $$S=K$$ for both. OTM: call $$S<K$$, put $$S>K$$.

**Q4.** Intrinsic value vs time value
> **A:** Intrinsic value = exercise value now: $$\max(S-K,0)$$ for a call, $$\max(K-S,0)$$ for a put. Time value = premium − intrinsic value; it reflects remaining optionality and is largest at the money.

**Q5.** Contract specifications: size, expirations, strikes, limits
> **A:** One contract = options on 100 shares. Standardized monthly cycles plus current/next month; LEAPS ~3 years; weeklys expire each Friday. Strike spacing ~$2.50/$5/$10 by price level. Position limits cap contracts held on one side; exercise limits cap exercises over a window — both deter manipulation.

**Q6.** Corporate-action adjustments: splits and stock dividends
> **A:** For an n-for-m split, strike × m/n and shares per contract × n/m (economics preserved). Example: 3-for-1 split, $$K=\$90$$ on 100 shares → $$K=\$30$$ on 300 shares. A 25% stock dividend = 5-for-4 split → $$K=\$72$$ on 125 shares.

**Q7.** Trap: are options adjusted for cash dividends?
> **A:** No. Exchange-traded options are NOT adjusted for ordinary cash dividends — the stock drops on the ex-date and holders bear it (the dividend is priced into the premium). They ARE adjusted for splits and stock dividends. Only special/large distributions trigger discretionary OCC adjustment.

**Q8.** Role of the OCC and assignment mechanics
> **A:** The Options Clearing Corporation is the central counterparty — guarantees performance, becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, and nets positions. On exercise it selects a short member firm, which assigns a customer (random or FIFO). Most ITM options exercise automatically at expiration.

**Q9.** Why sell an ITM American option rather than exercise it early?
> **A:** Exercise captures only intrinsic value, while a sale also captures remaining time value. Therefore early exercise of a call on a non-dividend stock is never optimal — sell it instead.

**Q10.** Option buyer and naked writer margin rules
> **A:** Buyers pay the premium in full (options already embed leverage); only options with maturity over 9 months may be bought on 50% margin. Naked writers post the greater of: (1) premium + 20% of stock price − OTM amount; (2) premium + 10% of stock price (calls) or 10% of strike (puts). Covered call writers need no extra margin.

**Q11.** Worked: naked call writer margin
> **A:** Write 5 naked calls, $$K=55$$, $$S=51$$, premium $2.00. Formula 1: $$2.00 + 0.20(51) - (55-51) = \$8.20$$. Formula 2: $$2.00 + 0.10(51) = \$7.10$$. Margin = $$\max(8.20, 7.10) \times 100 \times 5 = \$4,100$$.

**Q12.** Nonstandard option products
> **A:** FLEX: exchange-traded with negotiable terms, OCC-cleared. Weeklys: standard options expiring weekly. Binary (digital): pay a fixed amount if ITM, else nothing. DOOM options / CEBOs: deep-OTM puts and Credit Event Binary Options that pay on collapse/credit event — exchange-traded, CDS-like.

**Q13.** Warrants, ESOs, and convertibles vs exchange-traded options
> **A:** They are issued by the company itself (not investors); exercise creates NEW shares causing dilution; terms are custom (ESOs nontransferable, with vesting); number outstanding is fixed at issuance. Because exercise brings cash to the company and dilutes holders, valuation differs from ordinary options.

**Q14.** Trap: premium quoted per share vs cash per contract
> **A:** Premiums are quoted per share, but cash changes hands per contract (100 shares). A $3.50 quote costs $$3.50 \times 100 = \$350$$ per contract. Read whether the question asks for the per-share or per-contract figure.
