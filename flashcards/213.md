**Q1.** List the basic steps of a Monte Carlo simulation.
> **A:** (1) Specify the data-generating process; (2) generate pseudo-random draws; (3) compute the statistic/payoff per draw; (4) repeat $$N$$ times; (5) aggregate — average for the estimate, empirical distribution for quantiles.

**Q2.** Give the Monte Carlo standard error and its convergence rate.
> **A:** $$SE = \dfrac{s}{\sqrt{N}}$$ ($$s$$ = across-trial SD). Precision improves at rate $$\sqrt{N}$$: 10× more accuracy needs 100× more trials.

**Q3.** How do antithetic variates reduce variance?
> **A:** Pair each draw $$u$$ with its mirror ($$1-u$$ or $$-z$$); the negatively correlated estimates give $$\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{\hat\theta_1+\hat\theta_2}{2}\right) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1+\rho)$$, below independent sampling when $$\rho < 0$$ — and reuses the random numbers.

**Q4.** How do control variates work?
> **A:** Simulate a related quantity $$X$$ with known $$E[X]$$ alongside the target: $$\hat\theta_{CV} = \hat\theta + \beta(E[X] - \hat X)$$. The control's simulation error corrects the target's; works best when highly correlated.

**Q5.** What are pseudo-random numbers and the role of the seed?
> **A:** Deterministic sequences that pass randomness tests. The seed sets the starting state — the same seed reproduces the identical sequence, enabling reproducibility and fair comparison of models on identical shocks.

**Q6.** Describe the bootstrap procedure.
> **A:** Draw $$n$$ observations WITH replacement from the observed sample, compute the statistic, repeat $$B$$ times; the spread of the $$B$$ statistics estimates the sampling distribution (SEs, CIs, VaR).

**Q7.** Distinguish iid bootstrap from circular block bootstrap.
> **A:** iid bootstrap draws single observations (fine for independent data); circular block bootstrap draws consecutive blocks (wrapping around) to preserve time-series dependence like volatility clustering.

**Q8.** What is the bootstrap's key advantage over Monte Carlo?
> **A:** No distributional assumption — it samples the empirical distribution directly, capturing fat tails and skew that a normal-based MC would miss (no model risk from a misspecified DGP).

**Q9.** When does the bootstrap fail?
> **A:** Structural breaks / non-stationarity; strong dependence handled with naive iid resampling (use block bootstrap); and tails beyond the data — it can never generate a loss worse than the worst observation.

**Q10.** Contrast what Monte Carlo and bootstrap draw from, and their respective weaknesses.
> **A:** MC draws from an assumed model (weakness: model misspecification); bootstrap draws from observed data (weakness: short/unrepresentative samples, cannot extrapolate past observed extremes).

**Q11.** A MC estimate has SE $50,000 from 2,500 trials. How many trials to reach SE $10,000?
> **A:** SE must fall 5×, so trials rise $$5^2 = 25$$×: $$2{,}500 \times 25 = 62{,}500$$.

**Q12.** Per-pair variance $$\sigma^2 = 400$$; antithetic pairs with $$\rho = -0.6$$. Find the variance of the paired average.
> **A:** $$\mathrm{Var} = \dfrac{400}{2}(1 - 0.6) = 200 \times 0.4 = 80$$ (a 60% reduction vs. 200 for independent pairs).

**Q13.** What are the general disadvantages of simulation?
> **A:** Sampling error bought slowly ($$\sqrt{N}$$); MC answers only as good as the assumed DGP (model risk); high computational cost; answers are parameter-specific and give less general insight than analytic solutions.
