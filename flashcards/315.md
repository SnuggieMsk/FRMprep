**Q1.** Why do exotic options exist, and how is any derivative made zero-cost?
> **A:** Exotics arise from precise hedging needs, tax/regulatory/accounting motives, and dealers' wider margins on tailored products. Any derivative becomes zero-cost by deferring the premium: the buyer pays the future value of the premium at maturity instead of up front (e.g., a zero-cost collar/range forward — buy a put, sell a call so premiums net to zero).

**Q2.** Nonstandard American options
> **A:** Early exercise is restricted: Bermudan (exercise only on specified dates), lock-out (no exercise during an initial period), or a strike that steps up/down over the option's life. Typical of warrants and employee stock options.

**Q3.** Gap option payoff and the negative-payoff trap
> **A:** A gap call pays $$S_T - K_1$$ if $$S_T > K_2$$ (trigger), else zero; a gap put pays $$K_1 - S_T$$ if $$S_T < K_2$$. Because the trigger $$K_2$$ and payoff strike $$K_1$$ differ, the payoff can be NEGATIVE (when $$K_1 > K_2$$ and $$K_2 < S_T < K_1$$) — do not floor it at zero.

**Q4.** Forward start and cliquet (ratchet) options
> **A:** Forward start: paid for now, starts at future date $$T_1$$, usually struck at the money at $$T_1$$ (e.g., promised ESO grants). Cliquet: a series of forward start options, each resetting at the money when the previous expires — locks in gains period by period.

**Q5.** Compound options
> **A:** Options on options (call-on-call, call-on-put, put-on-call, put-on-put). Two strikes and two maturities: at $$T_1$$ pay $$K_1$$ to acquire/sell the underlying option with strike $$K_2$$ expiring at $$T_2$$. Cheap up-front exposure, high leverage, very sensitive to volatility.

**Q6.** Chooser options
> **A:** At $$T_1$$ the holder chooses whether the option is a call or a put (same strike $$K$$, expiry $$T_2$$); value at $$T_1$$ is $$\max(c, p)$$. By put-call parity it decomposes into a package of a call and a put, which is why a chooser resembles — and costs less than — a straddle.

**Q7.** Barrier options: the four types and the in/out parity identity
> **A:** Up-and-in, up-and-out, down-and-in, down-and-out (knock-ins come alive at the barrier $$H$$; knock-outs die). For the same $$K, T, H$$: $$c_{in}+c_{out}=c_{vanilla}$$ and $$p_{in}+p_{out}=p_{vanilla}$$. Barriers are CHEAPER than vanilla. A reverse knock-out has its barrier in the money, so it dies when most valuable.

**Q8.** Binary (digital) options
> **A:** Cash-or-nothing call pays a fixed $$Q$$ if $$S_T > K$$ (value $$Qe^{-rT}N(d_2)$$); asset-or-nothing call pays $$S_T$$ if $$S_T > K$$. A vanilla call = asset-or-nothing call − cash-or-nothing call with $$Q = K$$.

**Q9.** Lookback options (floating vs fixed)
> **A:** Floating lookback call pays $$S_T - S_{\min}$$ (buy at the low); floating put pays $$S_{\max} - S_T$$ (sell at the high). Fixed lookback call pays $$\max(S_{\max}-K,0)$$; fixed put pays $$\max(K-S_{\min},0)$$. Path-dependent and EXPENSIVE relative to vanilla.

**Q10.** Shout options
> **A:** The holder may "shout" once, locking in the intrinsic value at the shout date $$\tau$$ as a floor; the final call payoff is $$\max(S_T - K,\, S_\tau - K)$$. Cheaper than a lookback, similar idea.

**Q11.** Asian options and why average-price options are cheaper
> **A:** Average price call $$\max(S_{ave}-K,0)$$; average strike call $$\max(S_T - S_{ave},0)$$ (and puts analogously). Averaging lowers effective volatility, so average price options are CHEAPER than vanilla; the average is also harder to manipulate — ideal for hedging a stream of FX conversions.

**Q12.** Exchange, basket, and rainbow options
> **A:** Exchange: right to exchange one asset for another, payoff $$\max(S_T^{(1)} - S_T^{(2)}, 0)$$ (e.g., stock-for-stock takeover). Basket: payoff on a portfolio, cheaper than a basket of options because correlations < 1 reduce basket volatility. Rainbow: payoff on several assets, e.g., best-of/worst-of (CTD choice is rainbow-like).

**Q13.** Volatility swap vs variance swap
> **A:** Volatility swap pays $$L(\sigma_{realized} - \sigma_K)$$; variance swap pays $$L(\sigma^2_{realized} - \sigma^2_K)$$. Dealers prefer variance swaps because variance can be replicated from a static strip of vanilla calls and puts across strikes, making them easier to value and hedge.

**Q14.** Static option replication
> **A:** Instead of costly dynamic delta hedging (which breaks down near barriers), find a vanilla-option portfolio that matches the exotic's value along a chosen boundary (the barrier and maturity). If two portfolios agree on the whole boundary, they agree inside it; the portfolio is bought once and unwound only if the boundary is reached.
