**Q1.** When is a short hedge versus a long hedge appropriate?
> **A:** Short hedge (sell futures): when you own the asset or will sell/receive it later (farmer, oil producer, exporter) — gains offset spot price declines. Long hedge (buy futures): when you will need to buy the asset later (airline buying fuel, manufacturer buying copper) — gains offset spot price increases.

**Q2.** Give arguments against hedging.
> **A:** Shareholders can hedge/diversify themselves more cheaply and may have chosen the firm for its exposure; competitive pressures mean hedging when rivals don't can destabilize margins; and a visible futures loss can be hard to explain even when the combined position performed as designed.

**Q3.** Define the basis and basis risk.
> **A:** Basis $$b = S - F$$ (spot minus futures). At expiration it converges to zero (or grade/location differentials). Since hedges usually close before expiration, the basis at close $$b_2 = S_2 - F_2$$ is uncertain — that uncertainty is basis risk.

**Q4.** Who benefits from a strengthening versus a weakening basis?
> **A:** A strengthening (increasing) basis helps the short hedger (sells at an effectively higher price) and hurts the long hedger. A weakening basis helps the long hedger and hurts the short hedger.

**Q5.** What is the effective price locked in by a hedge?
> **A:** $$\text{Effective price} = F_1 + b_2 = S_2 + (F_1 - F_2)$$ — the initial futures price plus the basis at close. Hedging replaces price risk with the smaller basis risk.

**Q6.** State the minimum-variance hedge ratio.
> **A:** $$h^* = \rho \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}$$ where $$\sigma_S$$ and $$\sigma_F$$ are standard deviations of price changes in spot and futures and $$\rho$$ their correlation. It equals the slope of regressing ΔS on ΔF. Trap: spot volatility goes on top.

**Q7.** Compute the optimal number of contracts (quantity-based).
> **A:** $$N^* = \frac{h^* Q_A}{Q_F}$$ where $$Q_A$$ is the position size in units and $$Q_F$$ the futures contract size.

**Q8.** How is hedge effectiveness measured?
> **A:** $$R^2 = \rho^2$$ — the proportion of the hedged position's variance eliminated. Example: $$\rho = 0.928$$ gives $$R^2 = 0.861$$, so ~86% of variance is removed and ~14% remains as basis risk.

**Q9.** What is tailing the hedge?
> **A:** Because futures settle daily, base the hedge on values not quantities: $$N^* = \frac{h^* V_A}{V_F}$$ where $$V_A$$ is the position value and $$V_F$$ = futures price × contract size. Tailing slightly reduces the contract count.

**Q10.** How many contracts to fully hedge an equity portfolio with index futures?
> **A:** Short $$N^* = \beta \frac{P}{F}$$ where P is portfolio value and F = futures quote × multiplier. Example: β=1.2, P=$24M, F=$1M → 28.8 → short 29 contracts.

**Q11.** How do you change a portfolio's beta from β to a target β* with index futures?
> **A:** $$N = (\beta^* - \beta)\frac{P}{F}$$. Positive N = buy/long (raise beta); negative N = sell/short (lower beta). Setting β*=0 recovers the full hedge.

**Q12.** Why hedge an equity portfolio with futures rather than sell it?
> **A:** Avoids transaction costs and capital gains taxes of liquidation, keeps the carefully selected stocks (retain alpha while temporarily shedding market risk), and is quickly reversible. A fully hedged portfolio earns roughly the risk-free rate plus any alpha.

**Q13.** What is the stack-and-roll strategy and its risks?
> **A:** Hedge a long-dated exposure with liquid short-dated futures: take the full position in a near contract, then close and re-establish in the next contract before delivery. Risks: rollover basis risk (price gap between expiring and new contracts) and cash-flow timing — futures losses trigger immediate margin calls while offsetting gains are unrealized.

**Q14.** What lesson does Metallgesellschaft (1993) teach?
> **A:** It hedged long-term fixed-price oil supply contracts by stacking and rolling short-dated long futures. When oil prices fell, massive margin calls on the futures caused a funding crisis (offsetting supply-contract gains were distant and unrealized); unwinding locked in roughly $1.3 billion of losses.
