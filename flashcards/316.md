**Q1.** Best proxy for the risk-free rate when pricing derivatives
> **A:** OIS/SOFR-based rates, not Treasury yields. Treasury yields are artificially depressed by tax and regulatory/liquidity demand for Treasuries.

**Q2.** SOFR vs. legacy LIBOR
> **A:** SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) is based on actual Treasury repo transactions — secured, near risk-free. Legacy LIBOR was an unsecured interbank estimate carrying bank credit risk and was phased out after manipulation.

**Q3.** Convert a rate compounded $$m$$ times/year to continuous compounding (and back)
> **A:** $$r_c = m\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right)$$ and $$r_m = m\left(e^{r_c/m} - 1\right)$$. More frequent compounding means a lower quoted rate for the same effective return.

**Q4.** Future value of $100 under $$m$$-times and continuous compounding at rate $$R$$ for one year
> **A:** Discrete: $$100(1 + R/m)^m$$. Continuous: $$100e^{R}$$.

**Q5.** Equate two discrete compounding frequencies
> **A:** $$\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right)^m = \left(1 + \frac{r_n}{n}\right)^n$$ — match effective annual growth.

**Q6.** Definition of the $$n$$-year zero (spot) rate
> **A:** The rate on an investment with a single cash flow at time $$n$$ and no intermediate coupons. A coupon bond is a strip of cash flows each discounted at its own zero rate.

**Q7.** Bond yield vs. par yield
> **A:** Bond yield = the single discount rate reproducing the market price across all cash flows. Par yield = the coupon rate that prices the bond at par: $$c = \frac{(100 - 100d)\,m}{A}$$, with $$A$$ = sum of discount factors, $$d$$ = final-maturity discount factor.

**Q8.** Bootstrapping the zero curve — method
> **A:** Work from shortest maturity outward; each new bond adds exactly one unknown zero rate. Solve the 1-year zero from a 1-year instrument, then use it to back out the 2-year zero, and so on.

**Q9.** Forward rate for $$[T_1,T_2]$$ with continuous compounding
> **A:** $$R_F = \frac{R_2 T_2 - R_1 T_1}{T_2 - T_1}$$. With an upward-sloping curve, forward rates lie above zero rates.

**Q10.** Trap: forward rate with annual (not continuous) compounding
> **A:** The simple difference formula needs continuous compounding. With annual compounding solve $$(1+R_2)^{T_2} = (1+R_1)^{T_1}(1+R_F)^{T_2-T_1}$$. Mixing conventions is a deliberate distractor.

**Q11.** Value of an FRA to the party receiving fixed $$R_K$$
> **A:** $$V = L\,(R_K - R_F)(T_2 - T_1)\,e^{-R_2 T_2}$$. Pay-fixed side has the opposite sign. Positive when the locked-in receive rate exceeds the current forward rate.

**Q12.** FRA settlement trap
> **A:** An FRA settles at $$T_1$$ (in advance). The realized payment $$L(R_K - R_M)(T_2-T_1)/[1+R_M(T_2-T_1)]$$ is the interest differential discounted from $$T_2$$ back to $$T_1$$; the undiscounted differential is a wrong answer.

**Q13.** Three theories of the term structure
> **A:** Pure expectations: forwards = expected future spot rates. Market segmentation: each maturity bucket priced independently by its own supply/demand. Liquidity preference: long rates carry a premium, biasing forwards upward.

**Q14.** Why curves are usually upward-sloping (liquidity preference)
> **A:** Lenders prefer short maturities and borrowers prefer long, so long rates carry a liquidity premium and forward rates overstate expected future spot rates.
