**Q1.** Open-end mutual fund vs. closed-end fund vs. ETF
> **A:** Open-end: variable shares, trades with the fund once daily at 4 p.m. NAV. Closed-end: fixed shares after IPO, trades intraday on an exchange, can trade at a premium or (usually) discount to NAV. ETF: variable shares (block creation/redemption), trades intraday, stays near NAV via creation/redemption arbitrage.

**Q2.** NAV calculation
> **A:** $$\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}$$. E.g., ($250M − $10M)/12M = $20.00; all purchases/redemptions that day transact at $20.00.

**Q3.** What is tracking error and what causes it?
> **A:** The deviation of a fund's return from its index return (often the standard deviation of the return difference). Sources: fees/expenses, sampling vs. full replication, cash drag, transaction costs at rebalancing, dividend-timing differences.

**Q4.** Front-end load, back-end load, expense ratio
> **A:** Front-end: charged on purchase (a 5% load on $10,000 invests only $9,500 — needs 5.26% to break even). Back-end: charged on redemption, often declining with holding period. Expense ratio: annual management/operating/12b-1 costs as % of assets — compounds yearly, usually most damaging long term.

**Q5.** Late trading vs. market timing
> **A:** Late trading: placing orders after the 4 p.m. cutoff but still getting today's NAV (trading on post-close news) — illegal. Market timing: rapid in-and-out trading exploiting stale prices in NAV (e.g., Asian closing prices in a US fund); dilutes long-term holders — prohibited by fund rules.

**Q6.** Front running and directed brokerage
> **A:** Front running: trading for one's own account ahead of a known large fund order — illegal. Directed brokerage: a fund routes trades to a broker in exchange for the broker pushing the fund's shares (a kickback) — banned.

**Q7.** Key differences: hedge funds vs. mutual funds
> **A:** Hedge funds are lightly regulated (sell only to accredited/qualified investors): no required daily redemption (lock-ups, gates, notice periods), little disclosure, free to short/lever/concentrate/use derivatives, and charge performance fees.

**Q8.** What is "2 and 20"?
> **A:** A typical hedge fund fee: a 2% annual management fee on assets plus a 20% incentive fee on profits.

**Q9.** Hurdle rate vs. high-water mark vs. clawback
> **A:** Hurdle rate: incentive fee applies only to returns above a benchmark. High-water mark (HWM): incentive fees only on gains above the highest NAV already charged on — losses must be recouped first. Clawback: part of past incentive fees held in escrow and returned if later losses occur.

**Q10.** Incentive fee with a high-water mark — calculation
> **A:** Fee = incentive rate × (pre-fee AUM − HWM), only if positive. E.g., AUM $110M after a loss, +20% → pre-fee $132M; fee-bearing gain = $132M − $125M HWM = $7M; fee = 20% × $7M = $1.4M.

**Q11.** Exam trap: the option-like asymmetry of "2 and 20"
> **A:** The incentive fee is charged on the excess over the HWM, not the full year's gain after a losing year. The manager shares gains but not losses — an incentive to add volatility that HWMs and clawbacks only partially offset.

**Q12.** What does a prime broker provide to a hedge fund?
> **A:** Trade clearing/settlement, custody, margin financing (leverage), securities lending for short sales, and capital introduction. It takes collateral and monitors positions — often the first to see a fund in distress.

**Q13.** Name several hedge fund strategies.
> **A:** Long/short equity (and equity-market-neutral), dedicated short, distressed securities, merger arbitrage, convertible arbitrage, fixed income arbitrage, emerging markets, global macro, and managed futures.

**Q14.** The three biases that inflate reported hedge fund performance
> **A:** Measurement bias (smoothed manager-marked illiquid valuations understate volatility/correlation), backfill bias (funds import favorable history when joining a database after good performance), and survivorship bias (failed funds drop out, leaving only winners).
