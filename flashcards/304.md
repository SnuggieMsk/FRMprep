**Q1.** What is a derivative, and what is its economic purpose?
> **A:** A contract whose value derives from an underlying variable (stock, rate, FX, commodity, credit event, even weather). It transfers risk from those who do not want it to those who will bear it for a price.

**Q2.** Linear vs. nonlinear derivatives
> **A:** Linear: payoff is a straight-line function of the underlying (forwards, futures, swaps); gain per $1 move is constant. Nonlinear: kinked/curved payoffs (options); sensitivity changes with the underlying's level — hence the Greeks.

**Q3.** Exchange-traded vs. OTC derivatives
> **A:** Exchange-traded: standardized, open to anyone, minimal counterparty risk (CCP + margin), transparent. OTC: customized, bilateral (banks/funds/corporates), counterparty risk present (mitigated by collateral and post-2008 mandatory clearing), historically opaque, far larger by notional.

**Q4.** Forward vs. futures vs. option vs. swap
> **A:** Forward: OTC agreement to buy/sell at future date $$T$$ for delivery price $$K$$ set today; costs nothing to enter. Futures: exchange-traded forward, marked to market daily, CCP-guaranteed. Option: right (not obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) at $$K$$; buyer pays a premium. Swap: exchange of cash flows by formula — economically a portfolio of forwards.

**Q5.** Forward payoffs at maturity
> **A:** $$\text{Long: } S_T - K \qquad \text{Short: } K - S_T$$. The short profits when the price falls.

**Q6.** The four option payoffs (gross of premium)
> **A:** Long call $$\max(S_T-K,0)$$; short call $$-\max(S_T-K,0)$$; long put $$\max(K-S_T,0)$$; short put $$-\max(K-S_T,0)$$. Profit subtracts (long) or adds (short) the premium.

**Q7.** Why does a forward cost nothing to enter but an option always costs a premium?
> **A:** $$K$$ is set so the forward's initial value is zero (obligation on both sides). An option's payoff is never negative for the holder, so the holder must pay a premium for that one-sided right.

**Q8.** Exam trap: obligation in forwards vs. options, and zero-sum
> **A:** Forwards/futures bind both sides — a long must buy at $$K$$ even when $$S_T<K$$, taking a loss; an option holder simply walks away. Every derivative is zero-sum: the long's payoff equals the short's loss.

**Q9.** Hedger, speculator, arbitrageur — what each does
> **A:** Hedger: holds a pre-existing exposure and uses derivatives to reduce it. Speculator: takes on exposure to bet on direction, using leverage. Arbitrageur: locks in riskless profit from price discrepancies, eliminating them in the process.

**Q10.** Hedging with a forward vs. with a put — the trade-off
> **A:** A forward locks in a rate (certainty, no upside). A put sets a floor while keeping upside, at the cost of the premium. Neither dominates — hedging reduces uncertainty, it does not maximize the outcome.

**Q11.** How does an option give a speculator leverage?
> **A:** A small premium controls a large position, amplifying both directions. Example: $2,000 buys 100 shares at $20 (+35% if $S_T$=$27) or 2,000 calls at $1 (+350% at $27, but −100% if they expire worthless).

**Q12.** Arbitrage example and why no-arbitrage pricing works
> **A:** Stock at $152 NY, £100 London, FX 1.50 → London = $150. Buy 100 in London ($15,000), sell 100 in NY ($15,200): ~$200 riskless. Arbitrage demand closes the gap to within transaction costs, which is why no-arbitrage pricing formulas hold.

**Q13.** What is the common thread in rogue-trader misuse of derivatives?
> **A:** One person controlling both trading and its back-office confirmation/settlement, defeating segregation of duties — Barings/Leeson (~$1.3B, 1995) and Société Générale/Kerviel (€4.9B, 2008). Controls: separate front/back office, independent valuation, position and loss limits.

**Q14.** What is hidden tail risk in derivatives strategies?
> **A:** Strategies earning small premiums most of the time (e.g., writing options) can carry catastrophic losses in rare states; track records understate the risk until it materializes.
