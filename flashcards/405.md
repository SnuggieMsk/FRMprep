**Q1.** List the four sources of country risk.
> **A:** Economic structure (growth stage, sector/commodity concentration), political risk (continuity, corruption, expropriation, war), legal system (property rights, contract enforcement), and economic maturity (volatility of early-stage economies).

**Q2.** How is country risk distinct from sovereign default risk?
> **A:** Country risk is broader — it includes risk to any investment in the country (equity, FDI, contracts), not just risk that the government defaults on its debt.

**Q3.** Three ways country risk is measured.
> **A:** Risk services (PRS/ICRG, EIU — composite scores, not PDs), sovereign ratings (Moody's/S&P/Fitch), and market-based measures (sovereign default spreads, CDS spreads).

**Q4.** Why would a sovereign default on local-currency debt rather than print money?
> **A:** Printing causes inflation/currency collapse that may be judged more costly than restructuring; some sovereigns are institutionally barred (independent central bank, currency board, euro membership — Greece couldn't print euros).

**Q5.** True or false: "A sovereign that prints its own currency cannot default."
> **A:** False — a classic exam trap. Local-currency defaults happen (e.g., Russia 1998) when monetization's cost exceeds restructuring's, or when printing is institutionally blocked.

**Q6.** Common causes of sovereign default.
> **A:** High debt/GDP and interest burden vs revenue, revenue volatility (commodity dependence), short-maturity/foreign-currency debt structure, banking-system feedback loops, and political unwillingness to pay (default is partly a choice).

**Q7.** Consequences of sovereign default.
> **A:** Output losses/recessions, banking crises (banks hold government bonds), costlier or lost market access for a period, trade/capital-flow disruption, political fallout. Domestic economic damage usually exceeds reputational exclusion costs.

**Q8.** How do local-currency vs foreign-currency sovereign ratings compare?
> **A:** Local-currency ratings are typically equal to or 1–2 notches higher, because of the printing press and easier domestic taxation. Most sovereign defaults historically are on foreign-currency debt.

**Q9.** Main criticisms of sovereign ratings.
> **A:** They lag markets (downgrades follow spread widening), herd behavior and procyclicality (clustered multi-notch downgrades in crises), home/regional bias debates, and issuer-pays-style conflicts.

**Q10.** Define the sovereign default spread and the CDS spread.
> **A:** Default spread = sovereign's USD/EUR bond yield − matched-maturity risk-free (Treasury/Bund) yield. CDS spread = annual premium for default protection.

**Q11.** Approximate risk-neutral PD from a CDS spread.
> **A:** For small spreads, $$s \approx PD \times LGD$$, so $$PD \approx s/LGD$$. Example: 240 bp with 60% LGD → ≈ 4% annual risk-neutral PD.

**Q12.** A 5-year CDS trades at 300 bp with LGD 75%. Implied annual PD?
> **A:** $$PD \approx s/LGD = 0.03/0.75 = 4.00\%$$. (Multiplying instead of dividing gives the wrong 2.25%.)

**Q13.** Why do CDS-implied (risk-neutral) PDs exceed historical default frequencies?
> **A:** CDS spreads price risk aversion and liquidity on top of expected loss, so risk-neutral PDs systematically exceed real-world frequencies — most visibly for investment-grade sovereigns. Ratings are smoother but stale; use both.
