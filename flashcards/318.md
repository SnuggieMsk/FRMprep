**Q1.** Level payment on a fixed-rate mortgage
> **A:** $$X = \frac{L\,r}{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}$$, with $$L$$ = principal, $$r$$ = monthly rate (annual ÷ 12), $$n$$ = number of monthly payments.

**Q2.** Decomposing a mortgage payment into interest and principal
> **A:** Interest = monthly rate × beginning balance; scheduled principal = payment − interest; balance falls by the scheduled principal. Early on almost all of the payment is interest (amortization). Outstanding balance = PV of remaining payments at rate $$r$$.

**Q3.** Level-payment vs. adjustable-rate mortgage
> **A:** Level-payment fixed-rate fully amortizes with a constant payment; the borrower holds a valuable prepayment option (a call on the loan). An ARM resets off an index plus margin (often teaser rates and caps), shifting interest-rate risk to the borrower.

**Q4.** Drivers of prepayment
> **A:** Refinancing (dominant, rate-driven), housing turnover, curtailments (extra partial payments), and defaults (foreclosure proceeds pass through). Refinancing shows burnout — heavily-refinanced pools respond less to a further rate drop.

**Q5.** Contraction risk vs. extension risk
> **A:** Contraction: rates fall → prepayments accelerate → principal returns early when reinvestment rates are low; price gains are capped (negative convexity). Extension: rates rise → prepayments slow → the security lengthens just when you want your money back.

**Q6.** SMM (single monthly mortality) definition
> **A:** $$\text{SMM} = \frac{\text{Prepayment}}{\text{Beginning balance} - \text{Scheduled principal}}$$ — the fraction of the balance net of scheduled principal that prepays in the month.

**Q7.** Convert between CPR and SMM
> **A:** $$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR})^{1/12}$$ and $$\text{CPR} = 1 - (1 - \text{SMM})^{12}$$. The conversion compounds survival, not the rate.

**Q8.** CPR-to-SMM trap
> **A:** Do not divide CPR by 12. A 6% CPR gives $$\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.94^{1/12} = 0.5143\%$$, not 0.50%. And apply SMM to the balance after subtracting that month's scheduled principal.

**Q9.** The PSA benchmark (100 PSA)
> **A:** CPR starts at 0.2% in month 1, rises 0.2%/month until 6% at month 30, then stays at 6%. Multiples scale the whole path: at 150 PSA the month-$$t$$ CPR is 1.5× the 100-PSA value (capped at 9% after month 30).

**Q10.** Prepayment amount for a month
> **A:** $$\text{Prepayment} = \text{SMM} \times (\text{Beginning balance} - \text{Scheduled principal})$$. Total principal returned = scheduled principal + prepayment.

**Q11.** Agency vs. non-agency MBS
> **A:** Agency (Ginnie Mae — full faith and credit; GSEs Fannie/Freddie) bear essentially no credit risk, only prepayment risk; loans must conform. Non-agency (private-label) securitize jumbo/alt-A/subprime loans — investors bear credit risk, managed with credit enhancement.

**Q12.** Pass-through vs. CMO
> **A:** A pass-through sends each holder a pro-rata share of interest and principal (less fees), trading TBA. A CMO carves cash flows into tranches with different prepayment exposure (sequential-pay; PACs with stable schedules protected by support/companion tranches). CMOs redistribute prepayment risk, not eliminate it.

**Q13.** Dollar roll transaction
> **A:** An MBS holder sells a TBA for this month's delivery and simultaneously buys it back for next month. The price drop ("drop") plus forgone coupon/principal set the implied financing cost; below the holder's funding rate the roll is cheap financing ("trades special").

**Q14.** Weighted average life (WAL) vs. duration
> **A:** WAL = average time to receipt of principal, weighted by principal amounts — a cash-flow timing measure, not a price-sensitivity measure. Duration weights PV of all cash flows; MBS effective duration shortens as rates fall and extends as rates rise (negative convexity).
