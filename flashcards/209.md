**Q1.** What are the consequences of heteroskedasticity for OLS?
> **A:** Coefficients stay unbiased and consistent, but the usual standard errors are wrong (typically understated), so t- and F-tests mislead; OLS is no longer efficient (not BLUE).

**Q2.** Describe the White test for heteroskedasticity.
> **A:** Regress squared residuals $$\hat\epsilon_i^2$$ on the regressors, their squares, and cross-products; under homoskedasticity $$nR^2_{\text{aux}} \sim \chi^2_m$$ ($$m$$ = auxiliary slopes). Large statistic ⇒ reject.

**Q3.** What are the remedies for heteroskedasticity?
> **A:** (1) White (robust) standard errors — keep OLS coefficients, fix the SEs (default); (2) weighted least squares if the variance structure is known; (3) transform the data (e.g., logs).

**Q4.** Distinguish perfect collinearity from imperfect multicollinearity.
> **A:** Perfect: a regressor is an exact linear function of others — OLS can't be computed; drop one. Imperfect: regressors highly but not perfectly correlated — OLS still unbiased, but SEs balloon.

**Q5.** What are the symptoms of multicollinearity?
> **A:** Inflated coefficient SEs, individually insignificant t-stats, unstable coefficients with possible "wrong" signs — while $$R^2$$ and the F-stat stay healthy.

**Q6.** Give the variance inflation factor and the rule of thumb.
> **A:** $$VIF_j = \dfrac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$, where $$R_j^2$$ comes from regressing $$X_j$$ on the other regressors. $$VIF > 10$$ ($$R_j^2 > 0.9$$) signals trouble.

**Q7.** Which problems leave coefficients unbiased, and which actually bias them?
> **A:** Heteroskedasticity and multicollinearity leave coefficients unbiased (they damage precision/SEs). Omitted variable bias is the violation that biases coefficients.

**Q8.** Give the direction of omitted variable bias.
> **A:** Direction = (sign of omitted variable's effect on $$Y$$) × (sign of its correlation with the included $$X$$).

**Q9.** What is the cost of including an irrelevant variable?
> **A:** Coefficients stay unbiased, but estimator variance rises (wasted d.o.f., possible collinearity) — a pure efficiency cost.

**Q10.** Auxiliary White regression gives $$R^2_{\text{aux}} = 0.12$$ with $$n = 100$$, 5 auxiliary slopes (crit $$\chi^2_5 = 11.07$$). Decision?
> **A:** $$nR^2 = 12.0 > 11.07$$ → reject homoskedasticity; use White robust standard errors.

**Q11.** Regressing $$X_3$$ on the other regressors gives $$R_3^2 = 0.95$$. Find the VIF and interpret.
> **A:** $$VIF_3 = 1/(1-0.95) = 20$$; the variance of $$\hat\beta_3$$ is 20× larger, SE ≈ 4.5× larger — drop or combine the collinear variables.

**Q12.** Explain the bias–variance trade-off in model selection.
> **A:** Too few variables ⇒ bias (underfitting); too many ⇒ variance (overfitting, fits in-sample noise, predicts poorly out-of-sample).

**Q13.** Describe general-to-specific selection and m-fold cross-validation.
> **A:** General-to-specific: start with everything, iteratively drop the least significant regressor until all remain significant. Cross-validation: split into $$m$$ blocks, fit on $$m-1$$ and test on the held-out block, choose the best average out-of-sample fit.
