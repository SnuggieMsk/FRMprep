**Q1.** What are the three major risks banks face, and how does each arise?
> **A:** Credit risk (borrowers/counterparties fail to pay), market risk (movements in market prices on trading positions), and operational risk (failed processes, people, systems, or external events). Credit risk is usually largest; operational risk is hardest to quantify.

**Q2.** Distinguish expected loss (EL) from unexpected loss (UL) and what covers each.
> **A:** EL is the average anticipated loss — a cost of business covered by pricing (loan spreads/provisions). UL is the loss beyond average in bad years — absorbed by capital (equity).

**Q3.** Economic capital vs. regulatory capital
> **A:** Economic capital is the bank's own estimate of capital needed to keep its default probability below a target (e.g., 0.1%, AA-consistent). Regulatory capital is the supervisory minimum (Basel) motivated by systemic stability and depositor protection. Banks hold the greater of the two.

**Q4.** How do you compute economic capital from a loss distribution?
> **A:** $$\text{EC} = \text{Loss at high percentile} - \text{EL}$$ — capital covers unexpected loss only. Example: $10B portfolio, EL 0.8% = $80M, 99.9% loss 3.0% = $300M, so EC = $300M − $80M = $220M.

**Q5.** How does deposit insurance create moral hazard?
> **A:** Insured depositors have no incentive to monitor or demand higher rates from risky banks, so a bank can fund aggressive risk-taking with cheap insured deposits — heads shareholders win, tails the insurance fund pays. The 1980s S&L crisis is the classic case. Mitigants: capital regulation, supervision, risk-based premiums.

**Q6.** Best efforts vs. firm commitment underwriting — who bears the risk?
> **A:** Best efforts: the bank sells what it can at the offer price for a fee; the issuer bears shortfall risk. Firm commitment: the bank buys the entire issue at one price and resells higher; the bank bears the placement (price) risk.

**Q7.** Firm-commitment profit/loss calculation
> **A:** Profit = shares × (resale price − purchase price). Buy 50M at $30, sell at $32 → +$100M; if only sellable at $29 → 50M × ($29 − $30) = −$50M loss borne by the bank.

**Q8.** What is a Dutch auction IPO?
> **A:** Investors submit bids (shares and price); the clearing price is the highest price at which all shares sell, and every successful bidder pays that single price. Used by Google (2004) to let the market set price and reduce underwriter underpricing.

**Q9.** Private placement vs. public offering
> **A:** Private placement: sold directly to a few sophisticated institutions; faster, cheaper, no public registration, but less liquid. Public offering: sold broadly to the public; requires registration/prospectus.

**Q10.** Explain the originate-to-distribute (OTD) model and its main drawback.
> **A:** A bank originates loans then sells them (usually via securitization) rather than holding to maturity. Benefits: frees capital, transfers risk, generates fees, expands credit. Drawback: with no retained exposure, the originator's incentive to screen/monitor collapses — a key cause of the 2007-08 subprime crisis. Post-crisis rules require risk retention.

**Q11.** Banking book vs. trading book
> **A:** Banking book: loans/instruments held to maturity, carried at amortized cost, credit-risk capital charge. Trading book: held for trading/market making, marked to market daily through P&L, market-risk capital charge.

**Q12.** Exam trap: why does reclassifying assets between the banking and trading books matter?
> **A:** It changes accounting and the capital charge. In 2007-08 banks parked securitized credit in the trading book (lower capital, MTM) — regulatory arbitrage — and illiquid positions made "market" values unreliable. Regulators now restrict reclassification.

**Q13.** What are Chinese walls and which conflicts of interest do they address?
> **A:** Information barriers (internal rules, physical/IT separation, compliance monitoring) preventing information flow between departments. They address research vs. underwriting pressure, commercial banking exploiting private knowledge in securities issuance, and banking client info leaking to the trading desk.
