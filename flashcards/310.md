**Q1.** Distinguish investment assets from consumption assets for forward pricing.
> **A:** Investment assets are held purely for investment by many investors (stocks, bonds, gold, silver) — arbitrage pins their forward price exactly. Consumption assets are held to be used (oil, copper, corn) — holders won't sell inventory to exploit mispricing, so arbitrage gives only an upper bound.

**Q2.** What is a short squeeze, and why does short selling matter for arbitrage?
> **A:** A short squeeze occurs when borrowable shares dry up and shorts are forced to buy back at rising prices. Short selling makes reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage feasible for investment assets. The short must pay the lender any dividends and post margin.

**Q3.** Cost-of-carry forward price with no income.
> **A:** $$F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$$ (continuous compounding, riskless rate r).

**Q4.** Forward price with known dollar income (PV = I) and with a known yield q.
> **A:** Known dollar income: $$F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}$$. Known yield: $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$$. Income reduces the forward price because the cash-and-carry arbitrageur receives it while holding the asset.

**Q5.** Forward price of a currency.
> **A:** $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-r_f)T}$$ — a foreign currency is an asset whose yield is the foreign risk-free rate $$r_f$$ (S quoted as domestic per unit of foreign).

**Q6.** How is the forward price enforced when $$F_{mkt} > F_0$$?
> **A:** Cash-and-carry: borrow $$S_0$$, buy the asset, short the forward at $$F_{mkt}$$. At T deliver, receive $$F_{mkt}$$, repay $$S_0 e^{rT}$$ → riskless profit $$F_{mkt} - S_0 e^{rT} > 0$$.

**Q7.** How is the forward price enforced when $$F_{mkt} < F_0$$?
> **A:** Reverse cash-and-carry: short the asset, invest proceeds at r, buy the forward. At T pay $$F_{mkt}$$, collect $$S_0 e^{rT}$$, return the shares → riskless profit $$S_0 e^{rT} - F_{mkt} > 0$$.

**Q8.** Value of an existing long forward contract during its life.
> **A:** $$f = (F - K)e^{-rT}$$ where F is the current forward price for the same maturity and K the delivery price. Equivalently $$f = S_0 - I - Ke^{-rT}$$. A short is worth $$-f$$. At inception value is zero (K = $$F_0$$).

**Q9.** Trap: distinguish the futures price from the value of the contract.
> **A:** The price $$F_0$$ is not the value. At inception the contract value is zero and only the delivery price K is locked in; value changes afterward as F moves.

**Q10.** Compounding trap in forward pricing.
> **A:** All cost-of-carry formulas assume continuous compounding. Convert a discrete rate via $$r_c = m\ln(1 + r_m/m)$$, or use $$F_0 = S_0(1+r)^T$$ consistently. Mixing conventions is the classic wrong answer.

**Q11.** When are forward and futures prices equal versus unequal?
> **A:** Equal when interest rates are deterministic. When the asset price is strongly positively correlated with rates, futures > forwards (settlement gains reinvested when rates are high, losses financed cheaply when low); negative correlation reverses this. The gap matters for long-dated rate contracts.

**Q12.** Stock index futures price and index arbitrage.
> **A:** $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$$ (q = dividend yield). If futures exceed this, buy the basket of stocks and short futures; if below, short the stocks and buy futures. Executed by computers (program trading); it broke down on Black Monday 1987 when execution lagged.

**Q13.** Stock $50, dividends $0.75 at 3 and 9 months, r = 4% continuous. Price a 1-year forward.
> **A:** $$I = 0.75e^{-0.04\times0.25} + 0.75e^{-0.04\times0.75} = 0.7425 + 0.7278 = 1.4703$$. $$F_0 = (50 - 1.4703)e^{0.04} = 48.5297 \times 1.0408 = 50.51$$.

**Q14.** Long a 1-year forward when $$S_0$$=$40, r=5%, so K = $42.05. Six months later stock is $45. Value to the long?
> **A:** Current 6-month forward $$F = 45e^{0.05\times0.5} = 46.1385$$. Value $$f = (46.1385 - 42.0520)e^{-0.025} = 4.0865 \times 0.9753 = 3.99$$ per share.
