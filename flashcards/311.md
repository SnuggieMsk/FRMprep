**Q1.** How do commodities differ from financial assets for forward pricing?
> **A:** Commodities incur storage costs (negative income), have transport costs that fragment prices regionally, exhibit seasonality, often cannot be shorted easily, and are held mainly for consumption. Cost-of-carry holds with extra terms for investment commodities, but only as an upper bound for consumption commodities.

**Q2.** Forward price of an investment commodity with storage costs
> **A:** $$F_0 = (S_0 + U)e^{rT}$$ with dollar storage cost PV $$U$$, or $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u)T}$$ with proportional storage rate $$u$$. Storage is a negative income, so it raises the carry.

**Q3.** Why does a consumption commodity satisfy only an inequality, and what is convenience yield?
> **A:** Inventory holders derive physical benefit from holding stock and won't sell to exploit a "too low" futures price, so reverse cash-and-carry fails: $$F_0 \le S_0 e^{(r+u)T}$$. The convenience yield $$y$$ is the benefit-of-holding that restores equality: $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u-y)T}$$.

**Q4.** Sign trap: storage cost vs convenience yield in the carry exponent
> **A:** Storage cost $$u$$ enters with a PLUS sign (negative income, raises the forward); convenience yield $$y$$ enters with a MINUS sign (income-like, lowers the forward). More storage → higher forward; more convenience → lower forward.

**Q5.** Lease rate and the gold forward price
> **A:** $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-\delta)T}$$, where the lease rate $$\delta$$ is what a borrower (e.g., a hedging miner) pays to borrow gold — the commodity analog of a dividend yield. It equals the convenience yield net of storage costs implied by market prices.

**Q6.** Contango vs backwardation
> **A:** Contango: futures > current spot (upward-sloping curve, ample inventories, positive net carry). Backwardation: futures < current spot (downward-sloping), occurring when convenience yield exceeds $$r+u$$ (tight inventories).

**Q7.** Normal contango vs normal backwardation
> **A:** These compare futures with the EXPECTED future spot (about risk premia). Normal contango: futures > expected future spot (short speculators earn a premium). Normal backwardation (Keynes): futures < expected future spot — net-short hedgers pay long speculators a risk premium, so futures drift up toward expected spot.

**Q8.** Trap: contango/backwardation vs their "normal" versions
> **A:** Contango/backwardation compare futures with the CURRENT spot (observable term-structure shape). Normal versions compare futures with the EXPECTED future spot (unobservable). A market can be in contango and normal backwardation at the same time.

**Q9.** Why does electricity show extreme spot price spikes?
> **A:** Electricity is essentially non-storable, so arbitrage pricing fails entirely and there is no inventory buffer — demand shocks (heat waves) hit prices directly, producing extreme spikes. Markets use day-ahead/real-time and peak/off-peak contracts.

**Q10.** Key features of natural gas and agricultural commodity markets
> **A:** Natural gas: storable but expensive to store, strongly seasonal demand (winter heating, summer cooling) producing seasonal futures peaks. Agriculturals: harvest-seasonal production (prices peak pre-harvest, fall post-harvest), weather is dominant risk, storability varies.

**Q11.** Cross hedging and sources of commodity basis risk
> **A:** When no contract exists on the exposure, hedge with a correlated one (e.g., jet fuel with heating oil/crude). Basis risk arises from grade/quality, location (delivery hub vs your location), and timing (horizon vs delivery month) mismatches. The optimal hedge ratio is the regression beta of spot changes on futures changes.

**Q12.** Weather derivatives: HDD and CDD
> **A:** HDD (heating degree days) accumulates $$\max(0, 65°F - \text{daily avg temp})$$; CDD (cooling degree days) accumulates $$\max(0, \text{daily avg temp} - 65°F)$$. Payoff = tick size (e.g., $20) × index value, used to hedge volume risk (mild winters cut demand).

**Q13.** Worked: implied convenience yield from $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u-y)T}$$
> **A:** $$y = r + u - \frac{1}{T}\ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{S_0}\right)$$. With $$S_0=80$$, $$F_0=77.5$$, $$r=4\%$$, $$u=2\%$$, $$T=0.75$$: $$y = 0.06 - \frac{\ln(0.96875)}{0.75} = 0.1023$$ (10.23%) — above $$r+u$$, consistent with backwardation.

**Q14.** Memory anchor for every cost-of-carry forward formula
> **A:** $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(\text{costs} - \text{benefits})T}$$. Costs that raise the forward: financing $$r$$, storage $$u$$. Benefits that lower it: income $$q$$, lease rate $$\delta$$, convenience yield $$y$$.
