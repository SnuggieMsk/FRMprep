**Q1.** What core contributions do exchanges provide?
> **A:** Standardization (size, quality, delivery fixed so only price is negotiated, concentrating trading into liquidity), price transparency (public trade/settlement prices), liquidity (many buyers/sellers, easy close-out), and counterparty risk management via the clearing house.

**Q2.** Open outcry vs. electronic trading
> **A:** Open outcry: traders shout and hand-signal in pits. Electronic: orders matched by computer — faster, cheaper, less error-prone, enables algorithmic/HFT — but removes the human market-making judgment of the pit.

**Q3.** The three stages of clearing development
> **A:** (1) Direct clearing — bilateral settlement, same-counterparty offsets netted. (2) Clearing rings — members net offsetting positions among themselves, exiting against any ring member. (3) Complete clearing (CCP) — the modern model via novation.

**Q4.** What is novation by a central counterparty?
> **A:** The original contract is replaced by two contracts: the clearing house becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. Members face only the clearing house, not each other.

**Q5.** Initial margin vs. variation margin
> **A:** Initial margin: posted when a position opens, sized to cover potential losses over the close-out period at high confidence (driven by underlying volatility). Variation margin: daily mark-to-market settlement — losers pay, winners receive — resetting each contract's value to zero daily so exposure cannot accumulate.

**Q6.** Clearing members vs. non-members
> **A:** Only clearing members deal directly with the clearing house and contribute to its default fund. Non-members (including clients) clear through a member, who is responsible to the clearing house for the client's positions and margin.

**Q7.** Key features of OTC markets
> **A:** A network of dealers (mostly banks) trading bilaterally, often via interdealer brokers who anonymously match interests. Bilateral (each bears the other's credit risk for the trade's life), customizable, less transparent, and far larger by notional (dominated by interest rate swaps).

**Q8.** What does an ISDA master agreement with close-out netting do?
> **A:** It makes all transactions between two parties a single legal agreement. On default, all transactions terminate and net to one payment (close-out netting). Same-day, same-currency ordinary payments also net (payment netting).

**Q9.** Gross vs. net counterparty exposure calculation
> **A:** Without netting, only positive-value trades count (you still owe negatives). With netting, exposure = max(sum of all values, 0). E.g., +10, −4, +6: without = 16; with = max(12, 0) = 12.

**Q10.** What is a CSA and its key terms?
> **A:** A Credit Support Annex to the ISDA master requires the party with negative portfolio value to post collateral (like exchange VM). Terms: threshold (uncollateralized exposure allowed), independent amount (extra collateral, like IM), haircuts (discount on non-cash), minimum transfer amount (skip small calls).

**Q11.** Threshold collateral calculation
> **A:** Collateral posted = exposure − threshold (if positive). A $5M threshold against $12M exposure requires $7M of collateral. A zero threshold means full collateralization.

**Q12.** SPV vs. DPC vs. monoline as counterparty-risk mitigants
> **A:** SPV: bankruptcy-remote entity holding collateral/transactions for priority (weakness: legal/consolidation risk). DPC: separately capitalized AAA dealer subsidiary (weakness: rating tied to parent). Monolines/CDPCs: highly rated insurers selling credit protection with little collateral (weakness: massive leverage, ratings-triggered death spiral in 2008).

**Q13.** Exam trap: why did monoline insurers fail in 2008?
> **A:** Ratings-based collateral triggers created wrong-way, cliff-edge risk: a counterparty posting no collateral while AAA must suddenly post huge amounts precisely when downgraded and least able to pay — so the protection failed exactly when it was most valuable.

**Q14.** The 2009 G20 post-crisis OTC reforms
> **A:** (1) Mandatory central clearing of standardized OTC derivatives through CCPs. (2) Trade reporting to repositories. (3) Trading standardized contracts on electronic platforms (SEFs) where appropriate. (4) Higher capital and bilateral margin for non-centrally-cleared derivatives. Bespoke OTC contracts still exist — nothing forced conversion to futures.
