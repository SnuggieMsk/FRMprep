**Q1.** T-bill price from a quoted discount rate.
> **A:** $$P = F\left(1 - d\cdot\frac{n}{360}\right)$$, with d = discount rate (actual/360), n = days to maturity, F = face.

**Q2.** Price a 90-day T-bill quoted at 2.00% discount, $100 face.
> **A:** $$P = 100(1 - 0.02\times\frac{90}{360}) = 100(1-0.005) = \$99.50$$.

**Q3.** Why does the quoted discount rate understate the true yield?
> **A:** The gain is divided by face (not the lower price paid), and the 360-day year shortens the annualization. The discount rate is never the yield — never discount cash flows with it.

**Q4.** Distinguish clean (flat) and dirty (invoice) bond prices.
> **A:** $$\text{Dirty} = \text{Clean} + \text{Accrued interest}$$, where $$AI = \text{coupon}\times\frac{\text{days since last coupon}}{\text{days in period}}$$. The buyer pays the dirty price (US Treasuries: actual/actual accrual).

**Q5.** Define the discount factor d(t).
> **A:** The price today of $1 delivered at t — the convention-free statement of time value. Any default-free cash-flow stream: $$P = \sum_t CF_t\cdot d(t)$$.

**Q6.** Bootstrap d(1.0): 1-year 4% semiannual bond at 100.97, given d(0.5) = 0.9900.
> **A:** $$2(0.9900) + 102\,d(1.0) = 100.97 \Rightarrow d(1.0) = \frac{98.99}{102} = 0.9705$$.

**Q7.** What property must discount factors satisfy?
> **A:** They must decline with maturity (positive rates) and lie below 1. A d(t) above an earlier one signals a data error or negative forward rates.

**Q8.** What are C-STRIPS and P-STRIPS, and why are their prices discount factors?
> **A:** A Treasury STRIP is a single-payment claim (coupon = C-STRIP, principal = P-STRIP), i.e., a zero-coupon Treasury, so its price (×100) is a discount factor. Stripping/reconstitution arbitrage forces bond price toward the sum of parts.

**Q9.** State the law of one price.
> **A:** Identical cash flows must trade at the same price; otherwise buy the cheap version, short the expensive one, and pocket the difference with zero net future obligation.

**Q10.** Given d(0.5) = 0.9900, d(1.0) = 0.9705, fair-price a 1-year 6% semiannual Treasury (cash flows 3 and 103).
> **A:** $$P_{fair} = 3(0.9900) + 103(0.9705) = 2.97 + 99.96 = 102.93$$.

**Q11.** That bond trades at 102.60. What is the arbitrage?
> **A:** It is cheap by 0.33. Buy the bond at 102.60; short the replicating STRIPS package (receive 102.93). Net cash today +0.33; future coupons cover the short STRIPS. Riskless profit $0.33/$100 face.

**Q12.** The arbitrage direction discipline (a common trap).
> **A:** Buy LOW, short HIGH. Also match the dirty price and every cash-flow date — comparing a clean quote against a dirty replication cost manufactures phantom arbitrage.

**Q13.** Why do apparent mispricings survive in practice?
> **A:** Bid-ask spreads, repo financing costs, balance-sheet/capital charges, and liquidity premia (on-the-run Treasuries trade rich to off-the-run). A mispricing smaller than round-trip costs is not exploitable.
