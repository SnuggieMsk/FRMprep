**Q1.** Mechanics of a plain vanilla interest rate swap
> **A:** One party pays fixed and receives floating (SOFR-based) on a notional that is never exchanged. Payments are netted; the floating rate is set at the start of a period and paid at the end. A payer gains when rates rise; a receiver gains when rates fall.

**Q2.** Confirmations and the ISDA Master Agreement
> **A:** Each trade's economic terms are recorded in a confirmation, which sits under the ISDA Master Agreement governing netting, collateral, and default between the two parties.

**Q3.** Comparative advantage — total gain
> **A:** $$\text{Gain} = (\text{fixed-rate spread}) - (\text{floating-rate spread})$$ between the two borrowers. Each borrows where it is relatively cheapest, then swaps into the exposure it wants, sharing the gain.

**Q4.** Criticism of the comparative-advantage argument
> **A:** The fixed-rate spread is a long-term locked-in credit spread, while the floating spread applies only until the next reset/refinancing. The weaker firm's cheap floating rate can reprice upward if its credit deteriorates, so the gain partly compensates for rollover/spread risk — not a free lunch.

**Q5.** Value a swap as a bond portfolio
> **A:** Receive-fixed: $$V_{\text{swap}} = B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}$$. Pay-fixed (payer): $$V_{\text{swap}} = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$.

**Q6.** Valuing the fixed leg $$B_{\text{fixed}}$$
> **A:** PV of the remaining fixed coupons plus notional at maturity, discounted at current zero rates.

**Q7.** Valuing the floating leg $$B_{\text{float}}$$
> **A:** Prices at par immediately after each reset. Between resets it equals (notional + the already-fixed next floating payment) discounted to the next payment date — all later floating cash flows are worth par at that date.

**Q8.** Floating-leg valuation traps
> **A:** Do not project floating coupons beyond the next (already-fixed) payment; do not value $$B_{\text{float}}$$ at par between resets (it is par only at reset). Watch the sign: $$B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}$$ is the receiver's value.

**Q9.** Value a swap as a portfolio of FRAs
> **A:** Assume forward rates are realized: compute each period's forward from the zero curve, set each net cash flow (receiver) as $$(\text{fixed} - \text{forward})\times\tau\times L$$, discount at the matching zero rate, and sum. Gives exactly the bond-method value.

**Q10.** Are individual swap exchanges worth zero at initiation?
> **A:** No — the total swap is worth zero, but individual FRAs are not. With an upward-sloping curve a receiver's early exchanges have positive value and later ones negative.

**Q11.** Currency swap — principal exchange
> **A:** A fixed-for-fixed currency swap exchanges principal and interest in one currency for another. Unlike rate swaps, principals are exchanged at initiation and at maturity (at a rate set so each leg starts at par).

**Q12.** Value a currency swap as two bonds
> **A:** For the party receiving foreign and paying dollars: $$V_{\text{swap}} = S_0 B_F - B_D$$, where $$S_0$$ = spot (dollars per foreign unit), $$B_F$$ = foreign-currency bond, $$B_D$$ = dollar bond. Can also be valued as a portfolio of FX forwards.

**Q13.** Other swap types
> **A:** Basis swap (floating-for-floating on two reference rates plus a spread), equity swap (index total return vs. fixed/floating), amortizing/step-up (varying notional), forward swap (starts later), swaption (option to enter a swap; a payer swaption benefits from rising rates).

**Q14.** Credit risk: interest rate vs. currency swap
> **A:** Exposure exists only when a swap's value is positive (replacement cost, far below notional), mitigated by netting/collateral/CCP. Rate-swap exposure peaks mid-life; a currency swap's exposure grows toward maturity because the final principal exchange dominates — so it carries more credit risk.
