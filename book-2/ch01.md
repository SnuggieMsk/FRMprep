# 1. Fundamentals of Probability

GARP tests mechanical fluency here: conditional probability, independence, the total probability rule, and Bayes' theorem — almost always as a numeric problem you solve with a probability table.

## Learning objectives

- Describe an event and an event space, and define independent and mutually exclusive events.
- Calculate the probability of an event given discrete probabilities.
- Define and calculate conditional probability.
- Distinguish between conditional and unconditional probabilities.
- Explain and apply Bayes' theorem.

## Events, event spaces, and probability axioms

- **Sample space** $$\Omega$$: the set of all possible outcomes.
- **Event**: any subset of the sample space. The **event space** is the collection of all events you can assign probabilities to.
- Axioms: $$P(A) \ge 0$$, $$P(\Omega) = 1$$, and for mutually exclusive events $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$.

Two key structural relationships:

- **Mutually exclusive** events cannot happen together: $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$.
- **Independent** events do not inform each other: $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$$, equivalently $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$.

{: .warning }
Mutually exclusive is *not* the same as independent — it is almost the opposite. If $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive and both have positive probability, knowing $$A$$ occurred tells you $$B$$ did not, so they are strongly dependent. The exam loves this distinction.

## The core probability rules

**Complement:**

$$P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$$

**Addition rule (any two events):**

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

**Conditional probability:**

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0$$

**Multiplication rule:**

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$$

**Total probability rule** (for a partition $$B_1, \dots, B_n$$ of the sample space):

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$$

## Conditional vs. unconditional probability

An **unconditional (marginal)** probability $$P(A)$$ uses no extra information. A **conditional** probability $$P(A \mid B)$$ restricts the sample space to outcomes where $$B$$ occurred. In risk language: the unconditional default rate of a portfolio vs. the default rate *given* a recession. Conditioning on bad states typically raises loss probabilities — this is the basis of stress testing.

{: .note }
Conditional independence does not imply unconditional independence, and vice versa. Two bonds may default independently *given* the state of the economy, yet be unconditionally correlated because both depend on that state.

## Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem inverts a conditional probability — from $$P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{state})$$ to $$P(\text{state} \mid \text{evidence})$$:

$$P(B_j \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}{\sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}$$

The denominator is just the total probability rule. The fastest exam technique is a two-way table or tree: lay out the joint probabilities, then divide the relevant cell by its column (or row) total.

### Worked example: Bayes with a fund-manager screen

A risk team classifies fund managers as skilled (20% of managers) or unskilled (80%). A skilled manager beats the benchmark in a given year with probability 0.70; an unskilled one with probability 0.50. A manager beat the benchmark this year. What is the probability the manager is skilled?

Joint probabilities:

| | Beat | Did not beat | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skilled | $$0.20 \times 0.70 = 0.14$$ | $$0.06$$ | $$0.20$$ |
| Unskilled | $$0.80 \times 0.50 = 0.40$$ | $$0.40$$ | $$0.80$$ |
| **Total** | $$0.54$$ | $$0.46$$ | $$1.00$$ |

$$P(\text{Skilled} \mid \text{Beat}) = \frac{0.14}{0.54} = 0.2593 \approx 25.9\%$$

One year of outperformance barely moves the prior from 20% to 25.9% — a classic FRM message about the weakness of short track records.

### Worked example: total probability and conditioning

A bond portfolio's one-year default probability is 1% in an expansion and 8% in a recession. The probability of recession next year is 25%.

**Unconditional default probability:**

$$P(D) = 0.08 \times 0.25 + 0.01 \times 0.75 = 0.020 + 0.0075 = 0.0275 = 2.75\%$$

**Given that the bond defaulted, the probability the economy was in recession:**

$$P(R \mid D) = \frac{0.08 \times 0.25}{0.0275} = \frac{0.020}{0.0275} = 0.7273 \approx 72.7\%$$

Even though recession had only a 25% prior, a default makes recession the most likely explanation.

## Independence checks in practice

To verify independence on the exam, test whether $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)$$ holds. Example: $$P(A)=0.4$$, $$P(B)=0.5$$, $$P(A\cap B)=0.2$$. Since $$0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.2$$, the events are independent — and therefore *not* mutually exclusive.

{: .tip }
When a question gives you $$P(A \mid B) \ne P(A)$$, you already know the events are dependent — no further computation needed.

## Quick quiz

**1.** Events $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive with $$P(A)=0.3$$ and $$P(B)=0.4$$. What is $$P(A \mid B)$$?

- A. 0.12
- B. 0.30
- C. 0.00
- D. 0.70

**2.** $$P(A)=0.6$$, $$P(B)=0.5$$, $$P(A \cup B)=0.8$$. Find $$P(A \mid B)$$.

- A. 0.30
- B. 0.60
- C. 0.50
- D. 0.375

**3.** A test for rogue trading flags 90% of true rogue traders and 5% of honest traders. 1% of traders are rogue. A trader is flagged. The probability the trader is actually rogue is closest to:

- A. 90.0%
- B. 15.4%
- C. 47.4%
- D. 1.0%

**4.** If $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent with positive probabilities, which statement is TRUE?

- A. They must be mutually exclusive
- B. $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$
- C. $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$
- D. $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. C** — Mutually exclusive means $$P(A \cap B)=0$$, so $$P(A \mid B) = 0/0.4 = 0$$.

**2. B** — $$P(A \cap B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.8 = 0.3$$; $$P(A \mid B) = 0.3/0.5 = 0.6$$. (Note the events happen to be independent.)

**3. B** — Bayes: $$\frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.009}{0.0585} = 0.1538$$.

**4. C** — Independence is defined by $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$, equivalently $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0$$, ruling out A, B, and D.
