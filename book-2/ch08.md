# 8. Regression with Multiple Explanatory Variables

Extends OLS to several regressors: interpreting partial coefficients, dummy variables, $$R^2$$ vs. adjusted $$R^2$$, and the F-test for joint significance. Adjusted-$$R^2$$ and F-statistic calculations are bread-and-butter exam questions.

## Learning objectives

- Distinguish between the relative assumptions of single and multiple regression.
- Interpret regression coefficients in a multiple regression.
- Interpret goodness-of-fit measures for single and multiple regressions, including $$R^2$$ and adjusted $$R^2$$.
- Construct, apply, and interpret joint hypothesis tests and confidence intervals for multiple coefficients in a regression.

## The model and assumptions

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_k X_{ki} + \epsilon_i$$

Assumptions carry over from single regression, plus one new requirement: **no perfect multicollinearity** — no regressor can be an exact linear combination of the others (otherwise OLS cannot separate their effects). The constant counts: including dummy variables for *every* category plus an intercept creates perfect collinearity (the **dummy variable trap** — always omit one category as the base).

## Interpreting coefficients

$$\beta_j$$ is the expected change in $$Y$$ from a one-unit change in $$X_j$$, **holding all other regressors constant** (a *partial* or ceteris paribus effect). This differs from the single-regression slope whenever regressors are correlated — multiple regression "nets out" the overlap.

Omitting a relevant variable that is correlated with an included one biases the included coefficient (**omitted variable bias** — the included regressor picks up its neighbor's effect).

**Dummy variables:** a binary regressor $$D \in \{0,1\}$$ shifts the intercept by $$\beta_D$$ for the $$D=1$$ group; its coefficient is the average difference in $$Y$$ between groups, other things equal.

## Goodness of fit: $$R^2$$ and adjusted $$R^2$$

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{RSS}{TSS}$$

$$R^2$$ **never falls** when you add a regressor — even a useless one — so it cannot be used to compare models of different sizes. The fix penalizes parameters:

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{RSS/(n - k - 1)}{TSS/(n - 1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}$$

where $$k$$ = number of slope coefficients. $$\bar R^2 \le R^2$$, can decrease when a weak regressor is added, and can be negative.

{: .warning }
Adding a variable raises $$\bar R^2$$ only if its $$\lvert t \rvert > 1$$ — a much weaker hurdle than significance at 5% ($$\lvert t\rvert > 1.96$$). So "adjusted $$R^2$$ went up" does *not* mean the new variable is statistically significant. Also: a high $$R^2$$ proves neither causality nor correct specification.

## Joint hypothesis tests: the F-statistic

To test $$q$$ restrictions jointly (e.g., $$H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = 0$$), compare restricted and unrestricted models:

$$F = \frac{(RSS_r - RSS_u)/q}{RSS_u/(n - k_u - 1)} \sim F_{q,\; n-k_u-1}$$

For the regression-wide null that **all slopes are zero**, this is equivalent to

$$F = \frac{R^2 / k}{(1 - R^2)/(n - k - 1)}$$

Reject for large $$F$$ (always an upper-tail test). Single-coefficient tests still use the t-statistic; CIs for individual coefficients are $$\hat\beta_j \pm t_{\alpha/2}\,SE(\hat\beta_j)$$.

{: .note }
A regression can have a significant F (jointly useful regressors) while no individual t is significant — the signature of multicollinearity: the variables matter as a group, but their separate effects are estimated imprecisely.

### Worked example: adjusted $$R^2$$

A regression with $$n = 66$$ observations and $$k = 5$$ regressors has $$R^2 = 0.40$$.

$$\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - 0.40)\,\frac{66 - 1}{66 - 5 - 1} = 1 - 0.60 \times \frac{65}{60} = 1 - 0.65 = 0.35$$

Adding a 6th regressor raises $$R^2$$ to 0.41. New adjusted $$R^2$$:

$$\bar R^2 = 1 - 0.59 \times \frac{65}{59} = 1 - 0.65 = 0.35$$

Essentially unchanged — the new variable does not earn its degree of freedom.

### Worked example: overall F-test

A model with $$k = 3$$ regressors on $$n = 44$$ observations has $$R^2 = 0.30$$. Test $$H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0$$ at 5%.

$$F = \frac{0.30/3}{(1 - 0.30)/(44 - 3 - 1)} = \frac{0.10}{0.70/40} = \frac{0.10}{0.0175} = 5.71$$

Critical $$F_{3,40} \approx 2.84$$. Since $$5.71 > 2.84$$, reject: the regressors are jointly significant.

### Worked example: restricted vs. unrestricted

Unrestricted model ($$k_u = 4$$, $$n = 60$$): $$RSS_u = 270$$. Dropping two regressors gives $$RSS_r = 306$$. Test whether the two dropped coefficients are jointly zero ($$q = 2$$):

$$F = \frac{(306 - 270)/2}{270/(60 - 4 - 1)} = \frac{18}{270/55} = \frac{18}{4.909} = 3.667$$

Critical $$F_{2,55} \approx 3.16$$ at 5%. Reject — the pair belongs in the model, even if each individual t-statistic is marginal.

## Quick quiz

**1.** With $$n = 30$$, $$k = 4$$, and $$R^2 = 0.52$$, adjusted $$R^2$$ is closest to:

- A. 0.52
- B. 0.44
- C. 0.48
- D. 0.40

**2.** In a wage regression with an intercept, including dummies for ALL four regions causes:

- A. Omitted variable bias
- B. Heteroskedasticity
- C. Perfect multicollinearity
- D. Autocorrelation

**3.** A significant overall F-statistic with all individual t-statistics insignificant most likely indicates:

- A. The model is useless
- B. Multicollinearity among regressors
- C. Heteroskedastic errors
- D. A nonlinear relationship

**4.** $$\beta_2 = -0.6$$ in a multiple regression of bond spread on rating dummies and leverage. The correct interpretation of $$-0.6$$ on leverage is the expected change in spread per unit of leverage:

- A. Ignoring all other variables
- B. Holding the other included regressors constant
- C. Only when all other variables are zero
- D. Only for the omitted rating category

<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B** — $$\bar R^2 = 1 - 0.48 \times \frac{29}{25} = 1 - 0.5568 = 0.443$$.

**2. C** — The dummy variable trap: the four dummies sum to the constant. Drop one category.

**3. B** — Correlated regressors inflate individual SEs while the group still explains $$Y$$.

**4. B** — Multiple-regression coefficients are partial effects, holding other regressors fixed.
