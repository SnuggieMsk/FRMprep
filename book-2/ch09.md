# 9. Regression Diagnostics

What breaks OLS and what to do about it: heteroskedasticity, multicollinearity, outliers, omitted/extraneous variables, and the bias–variance trade-off in model selection. The exam tests *consequences and remedies* — which problems bias coefficients vs. which only corrupt standard errors.

## Learning objectives

- Explain how to test whether a regression is affected by heteroskedasticity.
- Describe approaches to using heteroskedastic data.
- Characterize multicollinearity and its consequences; distinguish between multicollinearity and perfect collinearity.
- Describe the consequences of excluding a relevant explanatory variable from a model and including an irrelevant regressor.
- Explain two model-selection procedures and how these relate to the bias–variance trade-off.

## Heteroskedasticity

Error variance that depends on the regressors: $$\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i \mid X_i) = \sigma_i^2$$. Common in finance (volatility clustering, size effects).

**Consequences:** OLS coefficients remain unbiased and consistent, but the usual standard errors are wrong (typically understated), so t- and F-tests mislead. OLS is no longer efficient (not BLUE).

**Detection — White test:** regress squared residuals $$\hat\epsilon_i^2$$ on the regressors, their squares, and cross-products. Under homoskedasticity,

$$nR^2_{\text{aux}} \sim \chi^2_{m}$$

where $$m$$ is the number of auxiliary slope regressors. Large statistic ⇒ reject homoskedasticity.

**Remedies:** (1) **White (heteroskedasticity-robust) standard errors** — keep OLS coefficients, fix the SEs; the default in practice. (2) Weighted least squares if the variance structure is known. (3) Transform the data (e.g., logs).

## Multicollinearity

**Perfect collinearity:** one regressor is an exact linear function of others — OLS cannot be computed; one variable must be dropped (the dummy trap is the standard example).

**Multicollinearity (imperfect):** regressors are highly but not perfectly correlated. OLS is still unbiased and consistent, but coefficient standard errors balloon: individual t-stats look insignificant, coefficients are unstable across samples and can carry "wrong" signs, while $$R^2$$ and the F-stat stay healthy.

**Detection:** high pairwise correlations; high **variance inflation factor**,

$$VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

where $$R_j^2$$ comes from regressing $$X_j$$ on the other regressors. Rule of thumb: $$VIF > 10$$ ($$R_j^2 > 0.9$$) signals trouble.

**Remedies:** drop or combine redundant regressors, get more data, or use regularization (ridge/LASSO — see Ch 14).

{: .warning }
Keep the damage straight: heteroskedasticity and multicollinearity leave OLS coefficients **unbiased** — they damage standard errors / precision. **Omitted variable bias** is the violation that actually biases coefficients. Exam answers hinge on this distinction.

## Specification errors

**Omitting a relevant variable:** if the omitted variable is correlated with an included regressor, the included coefficient is **biased and inconsistent** — it absorbs the omitted effect. Direction of bias = (sign of omitted variable's effect on $$Y$$) × (sign of its correlation with the included $$X$$).

**Including an irrelevant variable:** coefficients stay unbiased, but estimator variance rises (wasted degrees of freedom, possible collinearity) — a pure efficiency cost.

**Outliers:** observations with extreme $$X$$ values (high leverage) can dominate OLS because errors are *squared*. Inspect with scatter plots and Cook's distance; consider robust estimators or verify data validity before deleting.

## Model selection and the bias–variance trade-off

- Too few variables ⇒ **bias** (underfitting); too many ⇒ **variance** (overfitting — the model fits in-sample noise and predicts poorly out-of-sample).
- **General-to-specific:** start with everything plausible, iteratively drop the regressor with the smallest insignificant $$\lvert t \rvert$$ until all remaining are significant.
- **m-fold cross-validation:** split the data into $$m$$ blocks; repeatedly fit on $$m-1$$ blocks, evaluate out-of-sample on the held-out block; choose the model with the best average out-of-sample fit. Directly targets prediction error rather than in-sample fit.
- Information criteria (AIC/BIC) penalize parameters analytically; BIC penalizes harder, choosing smaller models.

### Worked example: White test

Auxiliary regression of squared residuals on $$X_1$$, $$X_2$$, $$X_1^2$$, $$X_2^2$$, $$X_1 X_2$$ gives $$R^2_{\text{aux}} = 0.12$$ with $$n = 100$$.

$$nR^2 = 100 \times 0.12 = 12.0$$

Critical $$\chi^2_5$$ at 5% = 11.07. Since $$12.0 > 11.07$$, reject homoskedasticity — switch to White robust standard errors before trusting any t-test.

### Worked example: VIF

Regressing regressor $$X_3$$ on the other four regressors yields $$R_3^2 = 0.95$$.

$$VIF_3 = \frac{1}{1 - 0.95} = 20$$

The variance of $$\hat\beta_3$$ is 20 times what it would be with orthogonal regressors — its standard error is $$\sqrt{20} \approx 4.5$$ times larger. Well above the 10 threshold: drop or combine collinear variables, or accept that $$\beta_3$$ cannot be isolated.

## Quick quiz

**1.** Heteroskedasticity in OLS errors causes:

- A. Biased coefficients and invalid SEs
- B. Unbiased coefficients and invalid SEs
- C. Biased coefficients and valid SEs
- D. No consequences in large samples

**2.** A regression shows $$R^2 = 0.85$$, a highly significant F-statistic, but no significant individual t-statistic. The most likely cause:

- A. Heteroskedasticity
- B. Omitted variable bias
- C. Multicollinearity
- D. Too large a sample

**3.** Leaving out a relevant regressor that is positively correlated with both $$Y$$ and an included regressor $$X_1$$ will:

- A. Bias $$\hat\beta_1$$ upward
- B. Bias $$\hat\beta_1$$ downward
- C. Leave $$\hat\beta_1$$ unbiased but inefficient
- D. Only affect the intercept

**4.** $$R_j^2 = 0.80$$ in the auxiliary regression for $$X_j$$. The VIF is:

- A. 1.25
- B. 4
- C. 5
- D. 10

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B** — Coefficients survive; inference doesn't. Use robust SEs.

**2. C** — Classic multicollinearity signature: joint significance, individual insignificance.

**3. A** — Positive effect × positive correlation ⇒ upward (positive) omitted-variable bias.

**4. C** — $$VIF = 1/(1-0.8) = 5$$.

</details>
