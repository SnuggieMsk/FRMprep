# 5. Sample Moments

Moving from population to data: estimating mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, covariance, and understanding estimator quality (bias, consistency, efficiency), the LLN, and the CLT. The $$n$$ vs. $$n-1$$ divisor and the CLT are guaranteed exam material.

## Learning objectives

- Estimate the mean, variance, and standard deviation using sample data.
- Explain the difference between a population moment and a sample moment.
- Distinguish between an estimator and an estimate.
- Describe the bias of an estimator and explain what the bias measures.
- Explain what is meant by the statement that the mean estimator is BLUE.
- Describe the consistency of an estimator and explain the usefulness of this concept.
- Explain how the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) and Central Limit Theorem (CLT) apply to the sample mean.
- Estimate and interpret the skewness and kurtosis of a random variable.
- Use sample data to estimate quantiles, including the median.
- Estimate the mean of two variables and apply the CLT.
- Estimate the covariance and correlation between two random variables.
- Explain how coskewness and cokurtosis are related to skewness and kurtosis.

## Estimators vs. estimates; sample moments

An **estimator** is the formula (a random variable); an **estimate** is the number it produces from a particular sample.

**Sample mean:**

$$\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$$

**Sample variance (unbiased):**

$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2$$

The $$n-1$$ divisor (one degree of freedom spent estimating $$\bar{X}$$) makes $$s^2$$ unbiased. Dividing by $$n$$ gives the (biased, but consistent) ML-style estimator.

{: .warning }
Divisor trap: population variance and the biased estimator use $$n$$; the unbiased sample variance uses $$n-1$$. With small $$n$$ the difference is material — for $$n=5$$ the $$n$$-divisor estimate is 20% smaller. Read the question stem carefully for "unbiased" or "sample."

**Standard error of the mean:**

$$SE(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \approx \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

## Estimator properties

- **Bias:** $$\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta) = E[\hat\theta] - \theta$$. Unbiased means zero bias.
- **Efficiency:** among unbiased estimators, the one with smallest variance.
- **BLUE:** the sample mean is the **Best Linear Unbiased Estimator** of the population mean — minimum variance among all estimators that are linear in the data and unbiased (no distributional assumption needed beyond iid with finite variance).
- **Consistency:** $$\hat\theta \to \theta$$ in probability as $$n \to \infty$$. A consistent estimator can be biased in finite samples (e.g., the $$n$$-divisor variance) — the bias just vanishes asymptotically.

## LLN and CLT

**Law of Large Numbers:** for iid data with finite variance, $$\bar{X} \to \mu$$ as $$n \to \infty$$. Justifies estimation and Monte Carlo.

**Central Limit Theorem:** for iid data with mean $$\mu$$ and finite variance $$\sigma^2$$, regardless of the underlying distribution,

$$\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)$$

The CLT is why z- and t-tests on means work even for non-normal data once $$n$$ is reasonably large (rule of thumb 30+).

{: .note }
LLN says *where* the sample mean goes (to $$\mu$$); CLT says *how it fluctuates* on the way (normally, with standard deviation shrinking like $$1/\sqrt{n}$$).

## Higher sample moments

Sample skewness and kurtosis replace population moments with sample averages:

$$\hat{\text{skew}} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum (X_i - \bar X)^3}{\hat\sigma^3}, \qquad \hat{\text{kurt}} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum (X_i - \bar X)^4}{\hat\sigma^4}$$

Interpretation matches the population versions: negative skew = long left tail; kurtosis > 3 = fat tails. Both are estimated very imprecisely in small samples because they depend on rare extreme observations.

**Quantiles and the median:** sort the data; the median is the middle observation ($$n$$ odd) or the average of the two middle observations ($$n$$ even). Sample quantiles interpolate between order statistics. The median is robust to outliers; the mean is not.

## Covariance, correlation, and co-moments

$$\hat\sigma_{XY} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)(Y_i - \bar Y), \qquad \hat\rho = \frac{\hat\sigma_{XY}}{s_X s_Y}$$

**Coskewness** and **cokurtosis** extend skewness and kurtosis to pairs, e.g. standardized cross third moments like $$E[(X-\mu_X)^2 (Y-\mu_Y)]/(\sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y)$$. Negative coskewness means one asset tends to suffer when the other is volatile or falling — assets with negative coskewness with the market deserve extra risk premia. There are two distinct coskewness measures for a pair and three cokurtosis measures (for jointly normal variables, coskewness is 0 and the central cokurtosis measure is 1).

### Worked example: full set of sample moments

Five monthly returns (%): 2, −1, 4, 0, 5.

**Mean:** $$\bar X = (2 - 1 + 4 + 0 + 5)/5 = 10/5 = 2.0$$

**Deviations:** 0, −3, 2, −2, 3. Squared: 0, 9, 4, 4, 9; sum = 26.

**Sample variance:** $$s^2 = 26/4 = 6.5$$, so $$s = 2.550$$.

**Biased version:** $$26/5 = 5.2$$ — note how much smaller.

**Standard error of the mean:** $$SE = 2.550/\sqrt{5} = 1.140$$.

**Skewness (using $$\hat\sigma = \sqrt{5.2} = 2.280$$):** cubes: 0, −27, 8, −8, 27; sum = 0, so skewness $$= 0$$ — a symmetric sample.

### Worked example: sample correlation

Three paired observations of returns (%): $$X$$: 1, 3, 5; $$Y$$: 2, 2, 8. Means: $$\bar X = 3$$, $$\bar Y = 4$$.

| $$x_i - \bar X$$ | $$y_i - \bar Y$$ | product | $$(x_i-\bar X)^2$$ | $$(y_i-\bar Y)^2$$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −2 | −2 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| 0 | −2 | 0 | 0 | 4 |
| 2 | 4 | 8 | 4 | 16 |

Sums: 12, 8, 24.

$$\hat\sigma_{XY} = \frac{12}{2} = 6, \quad s_X = \sqrt{8/2} = 2, \quad s_Y = \sqrt{24/2} = 3.464$$

$$\hat\rho = \frac{6}{2 \times 3.464} = 0.866$$

### Worked example: CLT application

Losses have mean \$10M and standard deviation \$30M, distribution unknown and skewed. For $$n = 100$$ independent losses, the average loss is approximately

$$\bar X \sim N\!\left(10, \left(\frac{30}{\sqrt{100}}\right)^2\right) = N(10, 3^2)$$

$$P(\bar X > 16) \approx P\!\left(Z > \frac{16-10}{3}\right) = P(Z > 2) = 2.28\%$$

The CLT licenses the normal approximation despite skewed individual losses.

## Quick quiz

**1.** Sample: 4, 6, 8, 10. The unbiased sample variance is:

- A. 5.0
- B. 6.67
- C. 20.0
- D. 6.0

**2.** An estimator whose expected value equals the true parameter for every sample size is:

- A. Consistent
- B. Efficient
- C. Unbiased
- D. BLUE

**3.** The CLT states that for iid data with finite variance, as $$n$$ grows:

- A. The data become normally distributed
- B. The sample mean's distribution approaches normal
- C. The sample variance approaches zero
- D. The population mean approaches the sample mean

**4.** Quadrupling the sample size changes the standard error of the mean by a factor of:

- A. 1/4
- B. 1/2
- C. 2
- D. 1/16

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show answers</summary>

**1. B** — $$\bar X = 7$$; squared deviations 9, 1, 1, 9 sum to 20; $$s^2 = 20/3 = 6.67$$.

**2. C** — That is the definition of unbiasedness; consistency is an asymptotic property.

**3. B** — The CLT concerns the *sampling distribution of the mean*, not the raw data.

**4. B** — $$SE \propto 1/\sqrt{n}$$, and $$1/\sqrt{4} = 1/2$$.

</details>
