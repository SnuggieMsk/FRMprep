# 11. Non-Stationary Time Series

Three sources of non-stationarity — time trends, seasonality, and unit roots — and how to handle each. Random-walk properties, spurious regression, and the Dickey–Fuller test are the core exam targets.

## Learning objectives

- Describe linear and nonlinear time trends and how to model them.
- Explain how to use regression analysis to model seasonality.
- Describe a random walk and a unit root, and their key properties.
- Explain the challenges of modeling time series containing unit roots.
- Describe how to test if a time series contains a unit root (Dickey–Fuller intuition).
- Explain how to construct a time-series model from a series containing a trend, seasonality, or a unit root.

## Time trends

**Linear trend:** $$Y_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1 t + \epsilon_t$$ — the mean changes with time, so the series is non-stationary even if shocks are white noise.

**Nonlinear trends:** polynomial ($$+\,\delta_2 t^2$$) or, most common in finance, **log-linear**: $$\ln Y_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1 t + \epsilon_t$$, which models constant *percentage* growth $$\delta_1$$ per period.

A trend-stationary series becomes stationary after **detrending** (subtracting the fitted trend); shocks die out and the series returns to the trend line.

## Seasonality

Recurring calendar patterns (quarterly earnings, year-end funding squeezes). Model with **seasonal dummies** — for quarterly data include 3 dummies plus an intercept (or 4 dummies and no intercept; never both, the dummy trap):

$$Y_t = \delta_1 D_{1t} + \delta_2 D_{2t} + \delta_3 D_{3t} + \delta_4 D_{4t} + \epsilon_t$$

Alternatively, seasonal differencing $$\Delta_s Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-s}$$ removes a stable seasonal pattern.

## Random walks and unit roots

A series has a **unit root** when the AR lag polynomial has a root equal to one — in AR(1) terms, $$\phi = 1$$. The canonical case is the **random walk**:

$$Y_t = Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t \quad\Rightarrow\quad Y_t = Y_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{t}\epsilon_i$$

Properties (given $$Y_0$$):

$$E[Y_t] = Y_0, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = t\,\sigma^2$$

Variance grows without bound ⇒ non-stationary; shocks are **permanent** (no mean reversion); the best forecast at any horizon is the current value. A **random walk with drift**, $$Y_t = \delta + Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$, adds a deterministic tilt: $$E[Y_t] = Y_0 + \delta t$$ and variance is still $$t\sigma^2$$.

{: .note }
Trend-stationary vs. difference-stationary: a trend-stationary series reverts to its trend after a shock (detrend it); a unit-root series never reverts (difference it). Using the wrong fix — detrending a random walk or differencing a trend-stationary series — produces misspecified models.

## Why unit roots are dangerous

1. **Shock persistence:** effects never decay, so "long-run mean" forecasts are meaningless.
2. **Nonstandard distributions:** t-statistics on $$\phi$$ no longer follow the t-distribution, so naive tests of $$\phi = 1$$ are invalid.
3. **Spurious regression:** regressing one independent random walk on another routinely yields large $$R^2$$ and hugely significant t-stats despite zero true relationship. Telltale sign: high $$R^2$$ with strongly autocorrelated residuals (very low Durbin–Watson).

{: .warning }
A regression of one trending price *level* on another with $$R^2 = 0.9$$ and $$t = 15$$ proves nothing — it is the spurious-regression signature. Work with *returns/differences*, or establish cointegration, before believing any relationship between unit-root series. GARP loves this trap.

## Testing for a unit root: Dickey–Fuller

Rewrite the AR(1) by subtracting $$Y_{t-1}$$:

$$\Delta Y_t = \delta + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad \gamma = \phi - 1$$

- $$H_0: \gamma = 0$$ (unit root, $$\phi = 1$$) vs. $$H_1: \gamma < 0$$ (stationary, $$\phi < 1$$) — a **one-sided, lower-tail** test.
- The statistic $$\hat\gamma / SE(\hat\gamma)$$ does **not** follow a t-distribution under $$H_0$$; it follows the Dickey–Fuller distribution with more-negative critical values (about $$-2.86$$ at 5% with a constant, vs. $$-1.645$$ for a normal one-tailed test).
- The **augmented** Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test adds lagged $$\Delta Y$$ terms to soak up serial correlation; versions with constant and/or trend exist.
- Failing to reject does not prove a unit root — the test has low power against near-unit-root alternatives ($$\phi = 0.98$$).

## Building a model from a non-stationary series

1. Plot the series; identify trend / seasonality / random-walk behavior.
2. If deterministic trend ⇒ detrend (include $$t$$ in the regression). If seasonality ⇒ dummies or seasonal differencing.
3. Test for a unit root (ADF). If present ⇒ **first-difference** (for prices, use log differences = returns), then model the stationary differenced series with ARMA.
4. Verify the residuals are white noise (Ljung–Box).

### Worked example: random walk risk over horizons

A commodity price follows a driftless random walk with daily shock volatility $$\sigma = \$2$$. Today's price is \$150.

**Forecast for any horizon:** $$\hat Y_{t+h} = 150$$.

**Uncertainty at 30 days:**

$$\mathrm{Var}(Y_{t+30}) = 30 \times 4 = 120, \qquad \sigma_{30} = \sqrt{120} = 10.95$$

95% interval: $$150 \pm 1.96 \times 10.95 = (128.5,\; 171.5)$$. With drift $$\delta = 0.10$$/day, the 30-day forecast shifts to $$150 + 3 = 153$$; the variance is unchanged.

### Worked example: Dickey–Fuller decision

ADF regression on an equity index *level* series ($$T = 250$$) gives $$\hat\gamma = -0.020$$ with $$SE = 0.011$$.

$$DF = \frac{-0.020}{0.011} = -1.82$$

5% Dickey–Fuller critical value (with constant) $$\approx -2.86$$. Since $$-1.82 > -2.86$$ (not negative enough), **fail to reject the unit root**: treat the index as a random walk and model its *returns*, not its level. Note that against a naive one-sided normal critical value ($$-1.645$$) you would have wrongly rejected — exactly why DF critical values exist.

- D. $$\sigma^2/h$$

**2.** Regressing one independent random walk on another typically produces:

- A. $$R^2 \approx 0$$ and insignificant t-stats
- B. High $$R^2$$ and significant t-stats despite no true relationship
- C. Unbiased, efficient estimates
- D. Residuals that are white noise

**3.** The Dickey–Fuller test's null hypothesis is:

- A. The series is stationary
- B. The series contains a unit root
- C. The series has no drift
- D. The errors are homoskedastic

**4.** A series with a deterministic linear trend and stationary shocks is best handled by:

- A. First-differencing
- B. Detrending (regressing on time)
- C. Seasonal dummies
- D. Taking logs of returns
