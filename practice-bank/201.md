**Q1.** Events $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive with $$P(A) = 0.3$$ and $$P(B) = 0.5$$. What is $$P(A \cup B)$$?
- A. 0.15
- B. 0.65
- C. 0.80
- D. 1.00

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the probability that *at least one* of $$A$$ or $$B$$ happens, and it tells us the two events are "mutually exclusive."

Mutually exclusive means the two events can never happen at the same time, so their overlap is zero: $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$. Think of two doors in a hallway where you can only walk through one at a time — you can never be standing in both doorways at once.

The general addition rule is:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Plug in the numbers:

$$P(A \cup B) = 0.3 + 0.5 - 0 = 0.8$$

So the answer is 0.80 (option C). Because there is no overlap to subtract, we simply add the two probabilities.

- A (0.15) multiplies the probabilities ($$0.3 \times 0.5$$). That is the formula for independent events' *intersection*, not the union of mutually exclusive events.
- B (0.65) wrongly subtracts an overlap of 0.15 ($$0.3 + 0.5 - 0.15$$), treating the events as if they were independent. But mutually exclusive events have zero overlap.
- D (1.00) assumes the two events cover the entire sample space. Nothing in the problem says that.

📌 Exam tip: For mutually exclusive events the overlap is exactly zero, so you just add. Do not confuse "mutually exclusive" (cannot co-occur) with "independent" (do not inform each other).

**Q2.** Events $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive with $$P(A) = 0.3$$ and $$P(B) = 0.4$$. What is $$P(A \mid B)$$?
- A. 0.00
- B. 0.12
- C. 0.30
- D. 0.75

**Answer: A**

The question asks: given that $$B$$ has happened, how likely is $$A$$?

The conditional probability formula is:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

Because $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive, they can never happen together, so $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$. Therefore:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{0}{0.4} = 0$$

If you already know $$B$$ happened, then $$A$$ is impossible (they can't co-occur), so the chance of $$A$$ is zero. Analogy: if I tell you the coin landed tails, the probability it landed heads is zero — they exclude each other.

- B (0.12) is $$P(A) \times P(B) = 0.3 \times 0.4$$, the independence formula, which does not apply here.
- C (0.30) is just $$P(A)$$, which would be correct only if the events were independent — but mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent.
- D (0.75) is $$0.3 / 0.4$$, dividing the two marginal probabilities — a meaningless mix-up of the formula.

📌 Exam tip: Mutually exclusive + "what is $$P(A \mid B)$$?" almost always equals 0. Knowing one event ruled out the other.

**Q3.** $$P(A) = 0.6$$, $$P(B) = 0.5$$, and $$P(A \cup B) = 0.8$$. What is $$P(A \mid B)$$?
- A. 0.375
- B. 0.50
- C. 0.60
- D. 0.30

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the chance of $$A$$ given $$B$$, but first we must find the overlap $$P(A \cap B)$$, which isn't given directly.

Step 1 — find the overlap using the addition rule rearranged:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.8 = 0.3$$

Step 2 — apply the conditional probability formula:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0.3}{0.5} = 0.6$$

So $$P(A \mid B) = 0.60$$ (option C). Interestingly, this equals $$P(A) = 0.6$$, which means $$A$$ and $$B$$ happen to be independent here.

- A (0.375) divides by the wrong number ($$0.3 / 0.8$$), using the union instead of $$P(B)$$ in the denominator.
- B (0.50) just reports $$P(B)$$, ignoring the calculation.
- D (0.30) stops at $$P(A \cap B) = 0.3$$ and forgets to divide by $$P(B)$$.

📌 Exam tip: When the joint probability is missing, recover it from the addition rule first, then divide. The denominator in $$P(A \mid B)$$ is always $$P(B)$$.

**Q4.** A test for rogue traders flags 90% of true rogue traders and 5% of honest traders. 1% of all traders are rogue. If a trader is flagged, the probability the trader is actually rogue is closest to:
- A. 1.0%
- B. 15.4%
- C. 47.4%
- D. 90.0%

**Answer: B**

This is a Bayes' theorem problem. The test "flagging" someone is the evidence; "being rogue" is the hidden state. We want $$P(\text{rogue} \mid \text{flagged})$$.

It helps to imagine 10,000 traders:
- Rogue traders: $$1\% \times 10{,}000 = 100$$. Of these, 90% get flagged: $$0.90 \times 100 = 90$$.
- Honest traders: $$99\% \times 10{,}000 = 9{,}900$$. Of these, 5% get flagged: $$0.05 \times 9{,}900 = 495$$.

Total flagged $$= 90 + 495 = 585$$. Of those, the truly rogue ones number 90.

$$P(\text{rogue} \mid \text{flagged}) = \frac{90}{585} = 0.1538 \approx 15.4\%$$

In formula form:

$$\frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.009}{0.009 + 0.0495} = \frac{0.009}{0.0585} = 0.1538$$

Even a flagged trader is probably honest, because rogue traders are so rare to begin with (this is the "base rate" effect).

- A (1.0%) is just the prior 1% — it ignores the flag entirely.
- C (47.4%) comes from forgetting the base rates and computing $$0.90 / (0.90 + 0.05 \times ...)$$ incorrectly, or weighting both groups equally.
- D (90.0%) is the test's sensitivity $$P(\text{flagged} \mid \text{rogue})$$ — the question reversed. Confusing $$P(B\mid A)$$ with $$P(A\mid B)$$ is the classic trap.

📌 Exam tip: When a rare condition is tested, most positive flags are false positives. Always weight by the base rate — set up 10,000 people if the formula feels slippery.

**Q5.** A bond's one-year default probability is 2% in an expansion and 10% in a recession. The probability of recession next year is 30%. What is the unconditional probability of default?
- A. 2.0%
- B. 4.4%
- C. 6.0%
- D. 10.0%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the overall (unconditional) chance of default, blending the two economic scenarios using the total probability rule.

The total probability rule says: weight each scenario's default rate by how likely that scenario is, then add.

$$P(D) = P(D \mid \text{Rec}) \times P(\text{Rec}) + P(D \mid \text{Exp}) \times P(\text{Exp})$$

The recession probability is 30%, so expansion is $$100\% - 30\% = 70\%$$.

$$P(D) = 0.10 \times 0.30 + 0.02 \times 0.70$$

$$= 0.030 + 0.014 = 0.044 = 4.4\%$$

So the answer is 4.4% (option B). It's a weighted average: closer to the expansion rate because expansion is more likely.

- A (2.0%) uses only the expansion default rate, ignoring the recession scenario.
- C (6.0%) is the simple (unweighted) average of 2% and 10% ($$(2+10)/2$$), forgetting to weight by the 30%/70% probabilities.
- D (10.0%) uses only the recession default rate.

📌 Exam tip: "Unconditional probability" from scenario data = a probability-weighted average. Make sure the scenario weights add to 1 (here 30% + 70%).

**Q6.** Using the data in Q5 (default rate 10% in recession, 2% in expansion; recession probability 30%; unconditional default 4.4%), the bond defaults. What is the probability the economy was in recession?
- A. 30.0%
- B. 44.0%
- C. 68.2%
- D. 88.0%

**Answer: C**

This flips the conditioning: we know a default happened, and we want the chance the cause was a recession. That is Bayes' theorem.

$$P(\text{Rec} \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid \text{Rec}) \times P(\text{Rec})}{P(D)}$$

We already computed the denominator $$P(D) = 0.044$$ in Q5. The numerator is the recession-and-default joint probability:

$$P(D \mid \text{Rec}) \times P(\text{Rec}) = 0.10 \times 0.30 = 0.030$$

So:

$$P(\text{Rec} \mid D) = \frac{0.030}{0.044} = 0.6818 \approx 68.2\%$$

Even though recession had only a 30% prior chance, seeing a default makes recession the more likely explanation, because defaults are much more common in recessions.

- A (30.0%) is just the prior probability of recession, ignoring the new default information.
- B (44.0%) misreads the unconditional default rate (4.4%) as a percentage answer — unrelated quantity.
- D (88.0%) likely comes from dividing the recession default rate by the total of the two rates ($$0.10/0.114$$ or similar mix-up) rather than using proper joint probabilities.

📌 Exam tip: Bayes "updates" the prior. The numerator is one joint cell; the denominator is the total probability of the evidence. Build a 2x2 table if unsure.

**Q7.** If events $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent with $$P(A) = 0.4$$ and $$P(B) = 0.5$$, what is $$P(A \cap B)$$?
- A. 0.00
- B. 0.20
- C. 0.50
- D. 0.90

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the probability that both independent events happen together.

For independent events, the joint probability is simply the product:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B) = 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.20$$

Independent means knowing one event tells you nothing about the other — like two separate coin flips. So the chance of both is each chance multiplied.

- A (0.00) is the answer for *mutually exclusive* events, not independent ones. Independent events with positive probabilities definitely can co-occur.
- C (0.50) just reports $$P(B)$$.
- D (0.90) adds the probabilities ($$0.4 + 0.5$$), which is neither a valid intersection nor a correct union (the union would be $$0.4 + 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.7$$).

📌 Exam tip: Independent → multiply for the joint ($$\cap$$). Mutually exclusive → add for the union ($$\cup$$), and the joint is zero. These two are opposites; don't swap them.

**Q8.** $$P(A) = 0.4$$, $$P(B) = 0.5$$, and $$P(A \cap B) = 0.2$$. Which statement is TRUE?
- A. $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive
- B. $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent
- C. $$A$$ and $$B$$ are dependent
- D. $$P(A \mid B) = 0.4$$ is impossible

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to classify the relationship between the two events using the numbers given.

Test for independence: check whether $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$.

$$P(A) \times P(B) = 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.20$$

The given $$P(A \cap B) = 0.20$$ matches exactly, so the events ARE independent (option B). We can also confirm: $$P(A \mid B) = 0.2 / 0.5 = 0.4 = P(A)$$, the hallmark of independence.

- A is wrong: mutually exclusive would require $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$, but here it is 0.2.
- C (dependent) is the opposite of the truth; the independence test passed exactly.
- D is wrong: $$P(A \mid B) = 0.4$$ is not only possible, it is exactly what independence produces here.

📌 Exam tip: To check independence, compute $$P(A) \times P(B)$$ and compare to the given joint. Equal → independent; unequal → dependent.

**Q9.** A manager is classified as skilled (20% of managers) or unskilled (80%). A skilled manager beats the benchmark with probability 0.70, an unskilled one with probability 0.50. A randomly chosen manager beat the benchmark. The probability the manager is skilled is closest to:
- A. 20.0%
- B. 25.9%
- C. 54.0%
- D. 70.0%

**Answer: B**

We observe outperformance (the evidence) and want the chance the manager is truly skilled (the hidden state). That is Bayes' theorem, easiest with a small table.

Joint probabilities of (type AND beating):
- Skilled and beat: $$0.20 \times 0.70 = 0.14$$
- Unskilled and beat: $$0.80 \times 0.50 = 0.40$$

Total probability of beating $$= 0.14 + 0.40 = 0.54$$.

$$P(\text{Skilled} \mid \text{Beat}) = \frac{0.14}{0.54} = 0.2593 \approx 25.9\%$$

One year of beating the benchmark barely raises the belief in skill from 20% to about 26%, because plenty of unskilled managers also beat the benchmark by luck.

- A (20.0%) is the prior, ignoring the new evidence.
- C (54.0%) is the total probability of beating the benchmark (the denominator) — not the conditional answer.
- D (70.0%) is $$P(\text{beat} \mid \text{skilled})$$, the reversed conditional.

📌 Exam tip: A single year of outperformance is weak evidence of skill — Bayes shows the posterior moves only slightly. Watch for the reversed-conditional trap (70%).

**Q10.** Which of the following statements about probability is TRUE?
- A. If $$A$$ and $$B$$ are mutually exclusive, they must also be independent.
- B. Zero covariance between two events guarantees they are mutually exclusive.
- C. If $$A$$ and $$B$$ are independent, then $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$.
- D. Conditioning on a recession always lowers a portfolio's default probability.

**Answer: C**

The question is a "which statement is true" definitional check.

By definition, two events are independent exactly when knowing one tells you nothing about the other, i.e.:

$$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$

This is equivalent to $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$. So option C is the correct definition of independence.

- A is false and is the chapter's favorite trap: mutually exclusive events (with positive probabilities) are actually *strongly dependent* — if one happens, the other cannot. They are nearly the opposite of independent.
- B confuses unrelated ideas. Zero covariance/independence does not mean mutually exclusive; independent events with positive probability still co-occur.
- D is backward: conditioning on a recession (a bad state) typically *raises* default probabilities — that is the whole idea behind stress testing.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the definition $$P(A \mid B) = P(A) \Leftrightarrow$$ independence. And remember: mutually exclusive ≠ independent; bad-state conditioning raises loss probabilities.
