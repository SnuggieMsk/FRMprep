**Q1.** A derivative is best defined as:
- A. A security that always pays a fixed coupon regardless of market conditions
- B. A contract whose value depends on the value of an underlying variable
- C. A government bond issued to finance public spending
- D. A share of ownership in a company

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the basic definition of a derivative.

A derivative is a contract whose value is "derived from" something else — the underlying variable. That underlying could be a stock price, an interest rate, an FX rate, a commodity price, a credit event, or even the weather. The derivative itself has no independent value; it only moves because the underlying moves. Derivatives let people transfer risk from those who don't want it to those willing to bear it for a price.

Analogy: a derivative is like a bet on tomorrow's weather. The bet has no weather of its own — its value depends entirely on what the actual weather (the underlying) does.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes a fixed-coupon bond, which is a standalone security, not a derivative.
- C describes a government bond — again, an underlying-type security, not a derivative.
- D describes a share of stock — an underlying asset, not something derived from one.

📌 Exam tip: "Value depends on an underlying" is the heart of the definition. Derivatives transfer risk; they don't create independent value.

**Q2.** Which of the following is a NONLINEAR derivative?
- A. A forward contract
- B. A futures contract
- C. An interest rate swap
- D. An option

**Answer: D**

The question asks which instrument has a payoff that is NOT a straight line in the underlying.

Linear derivatives (forwards, futures, swaps) have payoffs that move in a straight line with the underlying — each $1 move in the underlying changes the payoff by a constant amount. Options are different: their payoff is "kinked." For example, a call pays nothing until the price passes the strike, then rises one-for-one. Because the sensitivity changes with the level of the underlying, options are nonlinear — which is exactly why we need the Greeks to manage their risk.

Analogy: a forward is like a straight ramp (steady slope). An option is like a hockey stick — flat for a while, then it bends upward.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (forward), B (futures), and C (swap) are all LINEAR — constant gain per unit move in the underlying. A swap is economically just a bundle of forwards.

📌 Exam tip: Linear = forwards, futures, swaps (constant sensitivity). Nonlinear = options (kinked payoff, changing sensitivity → Greeks). This distinction shows up everywhere.

**Q3.** An investor is SHORT a forward contract on gold with a delivery price of $50 per unit. At maturity the spot price is $44. What is the payoff per unit to the short?
- A. −$6
- B. +$6
- C. $0 — the short can walk away
- D. +$44

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the short forward's payoff when the price ends below the delivery price.

The short forward payoff formula is:
$$\text{Short forward payoff} = K - S_T$$
where K is the agreed delivery price and S_T is the spot price at maturity.

Plug in:
$$50 - 44 = +6$$

So the short gains $6 per unit. This makes sense: the short agreed to SELL gold at $50, but gold is now only worth $44 in the market. Selling at $50 something worth $44 is a $6 gain.

Analogy: you promised to sell your bike for \$50 next month. By next month bikes like yours are only worth \$44. You're happy — you still get \$50, gaining \$6 over the market.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (−$6) is the LONG's payoff (S_T − K = 44 − 50 = −6). The short is the mirror image.
- C is false — forwards bind BOTH parties; nobody can walk away (that's options).
- D (+$44) confuses the spot price with the payoff.

📌 Exam tip: Long forward = S_T − K. Short forward = K − S_T. The short profits when prices FALL. Forwards are obligations for both sides — no walking away.

**Q4.** A trader buys a European put with strike $40 for a premium of $3. At expiration the stock is at $35. What are the payoff (gross of premium) and the profit (net of premium)?
- A. Payoff $5; profit $2
- B. Payoff $2; profit $2
- C. Payoff $5; profit $5
- D. Payoff $0; profit −$3

**Answer: A**

The question asks for both the gross payoff and the net profit of a long put that finished in the money.

A put gives the right to SELL at the strike. Its payoff is:
$$\text{Long put payoff} = \max(K - S_T,\, 0)$$
Plug in:
$$\max(40 - 35,\, 0) = \max(5, 0) = 5$$
So the gross payoff is $5.

Profit subtracts the premium paid ($3):
$$\text{Profit} = 5 - 3 = 2$$

So payoff $5, profit $2. Answer A.

Analogy: a put is like insurance letting you sell at \$40. The stock dropped to \$35, so your right to sell at \$40 is worth \$5. But you paid \$3 for that insurance, so you're \$2 ahead.

Why the others are wrong:
- B says payoff $2 — that wrongly subtracts the premium from the payoff; payoff is gross ($5).
- C forgets to subtract the $3 premium from the profit.
- D treats the put as out of the money — but at $35 (below the $40 strike) the put IS in the money.

📌 Exam tip: Long put payoff = max(K − S_T, 0). Profit = payoff − premium. "Payoff" is always gross of premium; "profit" is net of premium.

**Q5.** A speculator with $1,000 can either buy 40 shares of a $25 stock or buy 2,000 calls (strike $27) at $0.50 each. If the stock ends at $30, what is the return on the OPTION strategy?
- A. +20%
- B. +100%
- C. +500%
- D. +600%

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the percentage return on the call-option strategy, to show how leverage amplifies gains.

Step 1 — Option payoff per call = max(S_T − K, 0) = max(30 − 27, 0) = $3.
Step 2 — Total payoff = 2,000 calls × $3 = $6,000.
Step 3 — Profit = total payoff − cost = $6,000 − $1,000 = $5,000.
Step 4 — Return = profit / amount invested:
$$\frac{5{,}000}{1{,}000} = 5 = 500\%$$

So +500%. Answer C. (For comparison, the stock gives only 40 × $5 = $200 profit, a +20% return — far smaller.)

Analogy: with the same \$1,000, the options act like a magnifying glass on the stock's move — a modest stock gain becomes a huge percentage gain (and would become a total wipeout if the stock fell).

Why the others are wrong:
- A (+20%) is the STOCK return, not the option return.
- B (+100%) understates the leverage; it's not the correct figure.
- D (+600%) wrongly uses the gross payoff ($6,000) as profit instead of subtracting the $1,000 cost.

📌 Exam tip: Option return = (total payoff − premium paid) / premium paid. Leverage magnifies BOTH directions — same modest move that gives +20% on stock can give +500% (or −100%) on options.

**Q6.** Which statement correctly distinguishes forwards from options?
- A. Both parties to a forward have an obligation; only the option writer has an obligation
- B. Both forwards and options require the buyer to pay a premium upfront
- C. The forward buyer can abandon the contract if prices move against them
- D. Options are linear derivatives while forwards are nonlinear

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the key structural difference between forwards and options.

In a forward, BOTH sides are obligated: the long must buy and the short must sell at the agreed price, no matter what — even at a loss. In an option, only the WRITER (seller) has an obligation; the holder has a right and can simply walk away if it's unfavorable. Because the holder can never be forced into a loss on the contract itself, they must pay a premium upfront to get that one-sided right.

Analogy: a forward is like both of you signing a binding promise to trade a car at a set price. An option is like paying a small deposit for the RIGHT to buy the car later if you want — you can back out, the seller can't.

Why the others are wrong:
- B is false — a forward costs nothing to enter (no premium); only options require a premium.
- C is false — the forward buyer is OBLIGATED and cannot abandon it; only option holders can walk away.
- D is reversed — options are NONLINEAR, forwards are LINEAR.

📌 Exam tip: Forward = obligation for both sides, costs nothing to enter. Option = right for the holder, obligation for the writer, requires a premium. That premium exists because the holder's payoff is never negative.

**Q7.** Which feature is an advantage of EXCHANGE-TRADED derivatives over OTC derivatives?
- A. Fully customizable contract terms tailored to each user
- B. Minimal counterparty risk due to a central counterparty plus margining
- C. Larger total notional outstanding
- D. Complete privacy with no public price reporting

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a benefit of exchange-traded derivatives.

On an exchange, a central counterparty (CCP) stands between the two sides, and both post margin. This means you essentially face the financially strong clearing house instead of an unknown trading partner, so counterparty (default) risk is minimal. Exchange-traded contracts are also standardized and have transparent public prices.

Analogy: trading on an exchange is like buying through a trusted escrow service that guarantees the deal — you don't have to worry whether the stranger on the other side will pay.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (customization) is an advantage of OTC, not exchange-traded; exchange contracts are standardized.
- C (larger notional) describes the OTC market, which is much bigger by notional.
- D (privacy/no public prices) describes OTC opacity; exchanges have HIGH price transparency, which is a benefit, but "no public reporting" is not an advantage and is the opposite of how exchanges work.

📌 Exam tip: Exchange-traded = standardized, transparent, minimal counterparty risk (CCP + margin). OTC = customizable, larger by notional, more counterparty risk. Match the feature to the right market.

**Q8.** A stock trades at $152 in New York and £100 in London, when the exchange rate is 1.50 USD/GBP. Ignoring transaction costs, an arbitrageur can lock in a riskless profit per share of about:
- A. $52
- B. $2
- C. $0 — there is no arbitrage
- D. $150

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the riskless arbitrage profit from the price gap between two markets.

Step 1 — Convert the London price into dollars:
$$£100 \times 1.50 = \$150$$

Step 2 — Compare to the New York price of $152. The stock is cheaper in London ($150) than in New York ($152).

Step 3 — Arbitrage: buy in London for $150, simultaneously sell in New York for $152:
$$152 - 150 = \$2 \text{ per share}$$

So about $2 per share, riskless (before costs). Answer B.

Analogy: if mangoes cost \$150 a crate in one market and sell for \$152 in another a street away, you buy cheap, sell dear, and pocket \$2 — risk-free, until everyone does it and the gap closes.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($52) wrongly compares $152 to £100 without converting the currency.
- C is false — there clearly IS a $2 gap after converting correctly.
- D ($150) is just the converted London price, not the profit.

📌 Exam tip: Always convert prices into a common currency first, THEN find the gap. Arbitrage = buy cheap, sell dear, riskless. Arbitrage activity itself closes the gap, which is why "no-arbitrage" pricing works.

**Q9.** A US exporter will receive €1,000,000 in 3 months. Which statement best compares hedging with a forward versus hedging with a put option on euros?
- A. The forward locks in a certain amount with no upside; the put sets a floor while keeping upside, at the cost of a premium
- B. The forward keeps upside while the put eliminates it
- C. Both strategies guarantee the exact same final dollar amount
- D. The put costs nothing, while the forward requires an upfront premium

**Answer: A**

The question asks how forwards and options differ as hedging tools for the exporter.

Selling euros forward locks in a fixed exchange rate (say 1.10), so the exporter knows exactly how many dollars they'll get — certainty, but if the euro rises they don't benefit (no upside). Buying a put gives the right (not obligation) to sell euros at a floor rate; if the euro falls the put protects them, but if the euro rises they let the put expire and enjoy the gain. The catch: the put costs an upfront premium, which a forward doesn't.

Analogy: the forward is like locking in a fixed price for your crop now — safe but no bonus if prices rise. The put is like insurance with a deductible — you keep the upside if prices rise, but you pay for the policy.

Why the others are wrong:
- B is reversed — the FORWARD eliminates upside; the PUT keeps it.
- C is false — the outcomes differ (the put keeps upside, the forward doesn't), and the put costs a premium.
- D is reversed — the PUT costs a premium; the forward costs nothing to enter.

📌 Exam tip: Forward = certainty, no premium, no upside. Option (put) = downside protection + upside retained, but you pay a premium. Neither dominates; hedging reduces uncertainty, not necessarily maximizes outcome.

**Q10.** The collapses of Barings Bank (1995) and Société Générale (2008) share which common control failure?
- A. Excessive use of central clearing
- B. One person controlling both trading and the back-office confirmation/settlement, defeating segregation of duties
- C. Too much transparency in reported trading profits
- D. Over-hedging that eliminated all risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks what control weakness allowed these famous rogue-trader losses.

In both cases, a single individual was able to both place trades AND control the back-office function that confirms and settles those trades. With no independent check, the trader could hide growing losses (Nick Leeson at Barings, ~$1.3B; Jérôme Kerviel at Société Générale, €4.9B). The lesson: the front office (trading) and back office (confirmation/settlement) must be separated — this is "segregation of duties."

Analogy: it's like letting the same person both spend the company's money AND approve their own expense reports. With no one checking, fraud and hidden losses can pile up unnoticed.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — central clearing reduces counterparty risk; it wasn't the cause here.
- C is backwards — the problem was HIDDEN losses and concealment, not too much transparency. (A warning sign is profits that look too good for the mandate.)
- D is false — these traders were taking on huge HIDDEN directional risk, not eliminating risk.

📌 Exam tip: Rogue-trader disasters → root cause is broken segregation of duties (one person over trading + back office). Controls: separate front/back office, independent valuation, position and loss limits.
