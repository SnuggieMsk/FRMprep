**Q1.** The "issuer-pays" model used by rating agencies contributed to the 2007–2009 crisis primarily by:
- A. Making ratings too expensive for small issuers to obtain
- B. Creating a conflict of interest that biased structured-product ratings upward
- C. Forcing agencies to rate only government bonds
- D. Eliminating competition among the rating agencies

**Answer: B**

The question asks how the way rating agencies got paid helped cause the crisis.

Under the issuer-pays model, the company creating and selling a bond pays the agency to rate it. That creates a conflict of interest: agencies competing for lucrative structured-finance fees had an incentive to give friendly (too high) ratings, and issuers could shop around for the best grade. This pushed many risky tranches up to AAA. So B is correct.

Analogy: it's like a student paying the examiner directly to grade their own homework — grades tend to drift upward.

- A is wrong: the problem was ratings being too generous, not too expensive for small issuers.
- C is wrong: agencies rated lots of private structured products, not just government bonds.
- D is wrong: the issuer-pays conflict did not eliminate competition; if anything, competition for fees worsened the upward bias.

📌 Exam tip: Issuer-pays = conflict of interest → inflated ratings + ratings shopping. This "legitimized" risky structured products.

**Q2.** Which best describes the "run on repo" during 2007–2008?
- A. Insured depositors withdrew their savings from commercial banks
- B. Repo lenders raised haircuts and refused to roll funding against securitized collateral, forcing fire sales
- C. The Federal Reserve stopped accepting Treasury securities as collateral
- D. Hedge funds voluntarily increased their use of repo leverage

**Answer: B**

The question asks what the "run on repo" actually was.

Dealers financed their holdings overnight using repo (borrowing against securities as collateral). As the value of mortgage-related collateral fell, lenders demanded bigger haircuts (more collateral per dollar lent) and eventually refused to roll the loans at all. Cut off from funding, dealers had to dump assets in fire sales, pushing prices down further — a margin/loss spiral. So B is correct.

Analogy: it's a bank run, but instead of depositors lining up, it's the lenders who finance trading desks suddenly demanding more collateral and then pulling their money.

- A is wrong: that describes a traditional retail deposit run, not the repo (wholesale) run.
- C is wrong: the Fed did not stop accepting Treasuries; in fact it broadened its lending facilities.
- D is wrong: the run was about lenders WITHDRAWING repo funding, not hedge funds increasing leverage.

📌 Exam tip: Repo run = haircuts spike + lenders refuse to roll → forced fire sales. It is the wholesale-market equivalent of a deposit run, but with no insurance.

**Q3.** ABS CDOs built largely from mezzanine (BBB) RMBS tranches proved especially toxic because:
- A. They held only prime, high-quality mortgages
- B. Their AAA ratings rested on low cross-pool correlation, while a nationwide house-price decline was a common systematic shock
- C. They were fully insured by the FDIC
- D. They were exchange-traded and heavily over-margined

**Answer: B**

The question asks why re-packaging weak mortgage tranches into CDOs backfired so badly.

Mezzanine RMBS tranches (rated BBB) were re-pooled into ABS CDOs, and the agencies again rated much of them AAA. That high rating depended on the assumption that mortgages in different regions would NOT all default together (low correlation). But a nationwide fall in house prices hit all the collateral at once — a common, systematic shock. The "diversification" assumption collapsed, and supposedly safe AAA tranches took heavy losses. So B is correct.

Analogy: bundling many "mostly safe" umbrellas only protects you if it doesn't rain everywhere at once. A nationwide storm soaks them all together.

- A is wrong: these CDOs were built from subprime/mezzanine tranches, not prime mortgages.
- C is wrong: CDO tranches were not FDIC-insured.
- D is wrong: these were largely OTC structured products, not over-margined exchange-traded instruments.

📌 Exam tip: Tranching REDISTRIBUTES risk, it doesn't destroy it. AAA ratings assumed low correlation; a systematic (nationwide) shock hits all collateral together.

**Q4.** Which event was a direct consequence of the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy in September 2008?
- A. The first Basel III rules took immediate effect
- B. The Reserve Primary money market fund "broke the buck," triggering a run on money funds
- C. US house prices reached their peak
- D. Northern Rock experienced a retail deposit run

**Answer: B**

The question asks what followed directly from Lehman's failure.

The Reserve Primary fund held Lehman commercial paper. When Lehman went bankrupt, that paper became nearly worthless, and the fund's value per share fell below $1 — it "broke the buck." That shocked investors, who then ran on money market funds and the commercial paper market seized up. So B is the direct consequence.

Analogy: people thought their money fund was as safe as cash; once one fund proved it wasn't, everyone rushed to pull money from all of them.

- A is wrong: Basel III was developed AFTER the crisis and phased in over later years, not instantly in September 2008.
- C is wrong: US house prices peaked back in 2006, before Lehman.
- D is wrong: Northern Rock's run happened in September 2007, a YEAR before Lehman.

📌 Exam tip: Lehman (Sep 15, 2008) → Reserve Primary breaks the buck → money-fund run and CP market freeze. Watch the dates: house prices peaked 2006, Northern Rock ran in 2007.

**Q5.** During the build-up to the crisis, the "originate-to-distribute" (OTD) model of mortgage lending mainly:
- A. Forced lenders to hold every loan they made to maturity
- B. Severed the link between underwriting quality and who ultimately bore the losses
- C. Required all mortgages to be government-guaranteed
- D. Eliminated the use of securitization

**Answer: B**

The question asks what the originate-to-distribute model did to lending incentives.

Under OTD, brokers and lenders made loans for FEES and then quickly sold (distributed) them to be securitized, passing the default risk on to others. Because the originator no longer bore the loss if the borrower defaulted, they had little reason to check the loan carefully — "no skin in the game." This broke the link between underwriting quality and loss-bearing. So B is correct.

Analogy: if a chef knows someone else will eat the meal and complain, they may stop tasting the food for quality.

- A is wrong: OTD is the OPPOSITE of holding loans to maturity; loans were sold off.
- C is wrong: OTD did not require government guarantees; much of it was private-label securitization.
- D is wrong: OTD RELIED on securitization, it did not eliminate it.

📌 Exam tip: OTD = make loans for fees, sell off the risk. "No skin in the game" → weak underwriting (e.g., liar loans, NINJA loans).

**Q6.** AIG's central role in the crisis came from:
- A. Originating most US subprime mortgages
- B. Selling large amounts of credit default swap (CDS) protection on CDO tranches, a one-way bet that led to huge collateral calls
- C. Running the largest money market fund in the world
- D. Acting as the main repo lender to broker-dealers

**Answer: B**

The question asks why AIG ended up needing a giant rescue.

AIG (and the monoline insurers) sold CDS protection on CDO tranches — effectively promising to pay if those tranches lost value. This was a one-way bet: it collected premiums while times were good but had enormous exposure if the tranches fell. When AIG was downgraded, it faced massive collateral calls it couldn't meet, leading to a $182 billion rescue. So B is correct.

Analogy: AIG was like an insurer that sold flood insurance to the whole town cheaply, never expecting a flood — then the flood came and it couldn't pay all the claims at once.

- A is wrong: AIG did not originate most subprime mortgages; brokers and lenders like New Century and Countrywide did that.
- C is wrong: that describes money market funds (e.g., Reserve Primary), not AIG.
- D is wrong: AIG was not the main repo lender; it was a seller of CDS protection.

📌 Exam tip: AIG = sold CDS protection on CDO tranches (one-way bet) → downgrade-triggered collateral calls → $182bn rescue.

**Q7.** In the securitization chain, what does "tranching" of a mortgage pool accomplish?
- A. It destroys the credit risk in the pool entirely
- B. It redistributes the pool's cash flows and losses into layers of different seniority, so senior tranches can earn high ratings
- C. It guarantees every tranche the same rating
- D. It converts the mortgages into insured bank deposits

**Answer: B**

The question asks what tranching does in a securitization.

Tranching slices a pool's cash flows into layers (tranches) ranked by who gets paid first and who absorbs losses first. The most senior tranche is paid first and is protected by the junior layers beneath it, so it can earn a high (often AAA) rating, while junior tranches take losses first. Importantly, tranching redistributes risk; it does not make the risk disappear. So B is correct.

Analogy: it's like a stadium where the top-deck seats stay dry longest in a storm because the lower decks flood first — but the rain (risk) is still there.

- A is wrong: tranching does NOT destroy credit risk; it merely reshuffles who bears it.
- C is wrong: tranches deliberately get DIFFERENT ratings (senior high, junior low), not the same.
- D is wrong: tranching does not turn mortgages into insured deposits.

📌 Exam tip: Tranching redistributes risk into seniority layers; it does not eliminate it. Senior tranches look safe only while loss correlation stays low.

**Q8.** Which factor in the early-2000s build-up most directly fed the housing bubble?
- A. Very high interest rates and tight money
- B. Cheap money (low Fed funds rate) and a global savings glut pushing investors to reach for yield
- C. A ban on mortgage securitization
- D. A nationwide decline in house prices before 2006

**Answer: B**

The question asks which condition fueled the housing boom before the crisis.

After 2001, the Fed funds rate was very low (around 1%), and a global "savings glut" sent money searching for higher returns. Cheap, plentiful money made mortgages easy to get and pushed investors toward higher-yielding mortgage products, inflating the housing bubble. So B is correct.

Analogy: when money is cheap and everyone is hunting for a bit more return, they pile into the same hot market — here, housing — and prices balloon.

- A is wrong: rates were LOW, not high; cheap money fueled the boom.
- C is wrong: securitization was booming, not banned; it helped spread the lending.
- D is wrong: house prices were RISING for years before peaking in 2006; the decline came later and triggered the bust.

📌 Exam tip: Build-up ingredients = cheap money + savings glut + housing boom + collapsed underwriting + securitization + heavy leverage + regulatory gaps.

**Q9.** A major reason short-term wholesale funding made the crisis SYSTEMIC was that:
- A. It was always fully insured, so losses fell only on the government
- B. It made many institutions' funding runnable like deposits but without insurance, so runs forced asset sales that hurt everyone
- C. It was used only by a single small bank
- D. It carried no rollover risk because it was long-term

**Answer: B**

The question asks why reliance on short-term wholesale funding spread trouble across the whole system.

Short-term wholesale funding (ABCP, repo, interbank) had to be rolled over constantly, which made lenders' positions "runnable" — they could be pulled quickly, just like deposits, but WITHOUT deposit insurance. When one institution faced a run and had to sell assets, those fire sales pushed prices down for everyone holding similar assets, transmitting one firm's trouble across the system (fire-sale externalities). So B is correct.

Analogy: if many shops all depend on the same overnight loans and one is forced to dump its inventory cheaply, it crashes the price for every other shop's stock too.

- A is wrong: wholesale funding was NOT insured; that's exactly why runs were so dangerous.
- C is wrong: many institutions, not just one small bank, relied on it — that's what made it systemic.
- D is wrong: it was SHORT-term and carried heavy rollover risk; that was the core danger.

📌 Exam tip: Short-term wholesale funding = runnable like deposits but uninsured. Runs → fire sales → falling prices for all holders = systemic contagion.

**Q10.** Which of the following was part of the central banks' response to the crisis?
- A. Raising policy rates sharply to fight a boom
- B. Slashing policy rates toward zero and broadening lender-of-last-resort facilities, plus quantitative easing
- C. Refusing to provide any emergency liquidity to markets
- D. Banning all government bond purchases

**Answer: B**

The question asks what central banks actually did during the crisis.

Central banks cut policy rates dramatically (the Fed funds rate fell to about 0–0.25% by December 2008), broadened lender-of-last-resort tools (term auction facilities, primary dealer and commercial paper facilities, money-fund backstops, FX swap lines), and launched quantitative easing — large-scale purchases of Treasuries and agency MBS. So B is correct.

Analogy: like firefighters opening every hydrant and cutting the cost of water to zero so the blaze can be smothered fast.

- A is wrong: central banks CUT rates, not raised them; there was no boom to fight.
- C is wrong: they provided massive emergency liquidity, the opposite of refusing.
- D is wrong: under QE they BOUGHT large amounts of government bonds, not banned purchases.

📌 Exam tip: Central-bank response = near-zero rates + broadened lender-of-last-resort facilities + QE. Governments added TARP, guarantees, and stress tests.
