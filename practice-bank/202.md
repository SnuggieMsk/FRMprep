**Q1.** A random variable $$X$$ has $$E[X] = 2$$ and $$E[X^2] = 13$$. What is the standard deviation of $$X$$?
- A. 3
- B. 9
- C. 11
- D. 3.61

**Answer: A**

The question gives the mean and the "mean of the squares" and asks for the standard deviation.

Use the shortcut formula for variance:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$$

Plug in:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = 13 - (2)^2 = 13 - 4 = 9$$

Standard deviation is the square root of variance:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{9} = 3$$

So the answer is 3 (option A). Variance measures spread in squared units; taking the square root brings it back to the original units.

- B (9) is the variance, not the standard deviation — they forgot the square root.
- C (11) comes from $$13 - 2 = 11$$, subtracting the mean instead of the mean squared.
- D (3.61) is $$\sqrt{13}$$, forgetting to subtract $$(E[X])^2$$ altogether.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize $$\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$$. Then remember to take the square root for standard deviation — a one-step slip turns the right variance into a wrong answer.

**Q2.** If $$Y = -2X + 5$$, and $$X$$ has skewness $$+0.8$$ and kurtosis 5, then $$Y$$ has:
- A. Skewness $$+0.8$$, kurtosis 5
- B. Skewness $$-0.8$$, kurtosis 5
- C. Skewness $$-0.8$$, kurtosis $$-5$$
- D. Skewness $$+0.8$$, kurtosis 10

**Answer: B**

The question asks how a linear transformation $$Y = aX + b$$ (here $$a = -2$$, $$b = 5$$) affects the *shape* statistics skewness and kurtosis.

Rules for linear transformations:
- Skewness is unchanged if the scale $$a$$ is positive, but its sign flips if $$a$$ is negative. Here $$a = -2 < 0$$, so skewness flips: $$+0.8 \to -0.8$$.
- Kurtosis is unchanged by any linear transformation, regardless of the sign of $$a$$. So kurtosis stays at 5.

Therefore $$Y$$ has skewness $$-0.8$$ and kurtosis 5 (option B). Multiplying by a negative number mirrors the distribution left-to-right (flipping which tail is long), but stretching/shifting never changes how "fat" the tails are.

- A keeps skewness positive, ignoring that a negative scale flips the sign.
- C wrongly makes kurtosis negative; kurtosis is always positive and is unaffected here.
- D doubles kurtosis to 10, as if the scale factor $$|a| = 2$$ multiplied it — but kurtosis is standardized and does not scale.

📌 Exam tip: Linear transform shape rules: skewness flips sign only if $$a<0$$; kurtosis never changes. The constant $$b$$ does nothing to any shape statistic.

**Q3.** Daily P&L $$X$$ has mean 0.5 and standard deviation 4. The desk applies $$Y = 0.9X - 0.2$$. What is the standard deviation of $$Y$$?
- A. 3.4
- B. 3.6
- C. 3.8
- D. 12.96

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the standard deviation of a linearly transformed variable $$Y = aX + b$$ with $$a = 0.9$$ and $$b = -0.2$$.

The rule for standard deviation under a linear transformation is:

$$\sigma_Y = |a| \times \sigma_X$$

The additive constant $$b = -0.2$$ does NOT affect spread — shifting every value down by 0.2 moves the whole distribution but does not stretch or squeeze it.

$$\sigma_Y = 0.9 \times 4 = 3.6$$

So the answer is 3.6 (option B).

- A (3.4) subtracts 0.2 from something, wrongly letting the constant $$b$$ reduce the spread. Constants never change spread.
- C (3.8) adds instead of multiplying ($$4 - 0.2$$ mis-handled), again mixing the constant into the scale.
- D (12.96) is the *variance* of $$Y$$ ($$0.9^2 \times 4^2 = 0.81 \times 16$$), not the standard deviation.

📌 Exam tip: For $$Y=aX+b$$, standard deviation scales by $$|a|$$ and ignores $$b$$. Variance scales by $$a^2$$. Don't report variance when asked for standard deviation.

**Q4.** Which statement about a continuous random variable is TRUE?
- A. Its PDF value can never exceed 1.
- B. $$P(X = x) = 0$$ for every single point $$x$$.
- C. Its CDF can decrease over some intervals.
- D. The PDF directly gives $$P(X \le x)$$.

**Answer: B**

This is a "which is true" check on the definitions of PDF and CDF for continuous variables.

For a continuous variable, the probability of landing on any exact single point is zero, because there are infinitely many possible values. Probability only accumulates over a *range* (an area under the PDF curve). So $$P(X = x) = 0$$ for every point — option B is correct.

Analogy: ask "what's the chance a dart lands at exactly 3.0000... cm?" — effectively zero, because there are infinitely many points; only "between 2 and 4 cm" has real probability.

- A is false: a PDF value (a density) can exceed 1. For example, a uniform distribution on $$[0, 0.5]$$ has height $$f(x) = 2$$. Only *areas* (probabilities) must stay in $$[0,1]$$.
- C is false: a CDF is always nondecreasing — it can be flat but never goes down, since probability cannot become negative.
- D is false: the *CDF* (not the PDF) gives $$P(X \le x)$$. The PDF gives density; you integrate it to get probability.

📌 Exam tip: Three facts to lock in: point probabilities are 0 for continuous variables; PDF heights can exceed 1; CDFs never decrease.

**Q5.** A distribution of returns has kurtosis of 6. Relative to a normal distribution with the same variance, this distribution has:
- A. Thinner tails and excess kurtosis of 6.
- B. Fatter tails and excess kurtosis of 3.
- C. Fatter tails and excess kurtosis of 6.
- D. The same tails; kurtosis only measures asymmetry.

**Answer: B**

The question tests the meaning of kurtosis and "excess kurtosis."

A normal distribution has kurtosis of exactly 3. Excess kurtosis is defined as:

$$\text{excess kurtosis} = \text{kurtosis} - 3 = 6 - 3 = 3$$

A positive excess kurtosis (here +3) means the distribution is "leptokurtic" — it has fatter tails than the normal. Fatter tails mean extreme outcomes (big gains or big losses) happen more often than a normal would predict. So option B is correct: fatter tails, excess kurtosis of 3.

- A says thinner tails, but kurtosis above 3 means fatter tails, not thinner.
- C correctly says fatter tails but wrongly reports excess kurtosis as 6 (it forgot to subtract 3).
- D confuses kurtosis with skewness. Kurtosis measures tail heaviness/peakedness; *skewness* measures asymmetry.

📌 Exam tip: Excess kurtosis = kurtosis − 3. Positive → fat tails (typical of financial returns). Don't confuse kurtosis (tails) with skewness (asymmetry).

**Q6.** A trade pays $$-10$$ with probability 0.2, $$0$$ with probability 0.5, and $$+8$$ with probability 0.3. What is its expected value $$E[X]$$?
- A. $$-0.67$$
- B. 0.40
- C. 0.67
- D. 2.40

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the expected (average) payoff of a discrete trade, weighting each outcome by its probability.

For a discrete variable:

$$E[X] = \sum_i x_i \, p_i$$

Compute term by term:

$$E[X] = (-10)(0.2) + (0)(0.5) + (8)(0.3)$$

$$= -2.0 + 0 + 2.4 = 0.4$$

So the expected value is 0.40 (option B). It is a probability-weighted average, not a simple average.

- A ($$-0.67$$) and C (0.67) come from taking a plain average of the three payoff values ($$(-10 + 0 + 8)/3 = -0.67$$) ignoring the probabilities entirely (and C flips the sign).
- D (2.40) keeps only the positive contribution $$8 \times 0.3$$ and forgets the $$-2.0$$ from the loss term.

📌 Exam tip: Expected value always weights each outcome by its probability — never take a plain average of the outcome values. Multiply each payoff by its probability, then sum.

**Q7.** A continuous variable is uniform on the interval $$[0, 0.5]$$. What is the height of its probability density function $$f(x)$$ on that interval?
- A. 0.5
- B. 1.0
- C. 2.0
- D. It is impossible; a PDF cannot exceed 1.

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the density height of a uniform distribution over a short interval.

For a continuous uniform distribution on $$[a, b]$$, the PDF is constant and equal to:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{b - a}$$

Here $$a = 0$$ and $$b = 0.5$$:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{0.5 - 0} = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2.0$$

So the density height is 2.0 (option C). The total *area* must equal 1 (it's a probability), and area = height × width = $$2.0 \times 0.5 = 1$$. Because the base is narrow (0.5 wide), the height must be tall (2.0) to keep the area at 1.

- A (0.5) reports the width of the interval, not the density.
- B (1.0) assumes the density must be capped at 1 — it doesn't.
- D states the common myth that a PDF cannot exceed 1. A density value can be any positive number; only the total area (probability) is capped at 1.

📌 Exam tip: Uniform PDF height = $$1/(b-a)$$, which exceeds 1 whenever the interval is narrower than 1 unit. Density is not probability — only areas are.

**Q8.** Which of the following is left unchanged when a variable is shifted by adding a constant, $$Y = X + b$$?
- A. The mean
- B. The median
- C. The variance
- D. The 75th percentile

**Answer: C**

The question asks which statistic is *unaffected* by adding a constant (a pure shift, no stretching).

Adding a constant $$b$$ slides every value of the distribution by the same amount. This moves all the location measures (mean, median, percentiles) up or down by $$b$$, but it does NOT change how spread out the values are. Variance measures spread, so:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X + b) = \mathrm{Var}(X)$$

The constant $$b$$ leaves variance (and standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis) unchanged. So option C is correct.

Analogy: if every student's exam score goes up by 5 bonus points, the *average* rises by 5, but the *spread* between high and low scorers is exactly the same.

- A: the mean shifts by $$b$$ ($$E[X+b] = E[X] + b$$), so it changes.
- B: the median is a location measure, so it shifts by $$b$$.
- D: the 75th percentile is also a location measure and shifts by $$b$$.

📌 Exam tip: An additive constant moves all *location* measures (mean, median, quantiles) but never touches *spread/shape* (variance, SD, skewness, kurtosis).

**Q9.** The interquartile range (IQR) of a distribution is defined as:
- A. $$Q(0.5)$$
- B. $$Q(0.75) - Q(0.25)$$
- C. $$Q(0.9) - Q(0.1)$$
- D. $$Q(1.0) - Q(0.0)$$

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the definition of the interquartile range.

The IQR is the spread of the "middle 50%" of the data. It is the difference between the third quartile (75th percentile) and the first quartile (25th percentile):

$$\text{IQR} = Q(0.75) - Q(0.25)$$

So option B is correct. Because it ignores the most extreme 25% on each end, the IQR is robust to outliers — a single huge value won't blow it up the way it would inflate the variance.

- A ($$Q(0.5)$$) is just the median — a single point, not a range.
- C ($$Q(0.9) - Q(0.1)$$) is the interdecile range (middle 80%), a different measure.
- D ($$Q(1.0) - Q(0.0)$$) is the full range (max minus min), which is extremely sensitive to outliers.

📌 Exam tip: IQR = 75th − 25th percentile = middle 50% spread. It is a *robust* dispersion measure, unlike variance and the full range.

**Q10.** The expectation operator $$E[\cdot]$$ is linear. Which statement is therefore always TRUE, even when $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are dependent?
- A. $$E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$$
- B. $$E[XY] = E[X]\,E[Y]$$
- C. $$E[X^2] = (E[X])^2$$
- D. $$\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)$$

**Answer: A**

The question tests which property holds *always*, regardless of dependence.

Linearity of expectation says the expected value of a sum equals the sum of the expected values:

$$E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$$

This holds always — even if $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are strongly dependent. That is what makes it so useful. So option A is correct.

- B ($$E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$$) holds only when $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are *uncorrelated/independent*, not in general. Dependence breaks it.
- C ($$E[X^2] = (E[X])^2$$) is false in general; the difference between the two sides is exactly the variance, which is usually positive.
- D ($$\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)$$) holds only when the covariance is zero. In general there is a $$+2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$$ term.

📌 Exam tip: Expectation of a *sum* always splits apart (linearity, no independence needed). Expectation of a *product* and variance of a *sum* do NOT split unless the variables are uncorrelated.
