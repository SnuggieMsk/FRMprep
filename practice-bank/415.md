**Q1.** A stock has an expected (simple) return of 12% and a volatility of 30%. Under the lognormal model, the mean of the continuously compounded annual return is:
- A. 12.0%
- B. 7.5%
- C. 16.5%
- D. 10.2%

**Answer: B**

The question asks: convert the expected simple return into the mean continuously compounded return.

The mean continuously compounded return is:
$$\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} = 0.12 - \frac{0.30^2}{2} = 0.12 - \frac{0.09}{2} = 0.12 - 0.045 = 0.075 = 7.5\%$$

- A (12.0%) ignores the variance drag $$\sigma^2/2$$ — it treats simple and continuously compounded means as equal.
- C (16.5%) ADDS the drag instead of subtracting it ($$0.12 + 0.045$$) — wrong sign.
- D (10.2%) subtracts σ (0.30 × something) incorrectly rather than $$\sigma^2/2$$.

Analogy: volatility is like a bumpy road — the rougher the ride (higher σ), the more your average COMPOUND speed falls below your expected speed, even though you set out at 12%.

📌 Exam tip: Mean continuously compounded return = $$\mu - \sigma^2/2$$. The $$\sigma^2/2$$ "drag" always SUBTRACTS — higher volatility lowers compound growth.

**Q2.** In the Black-Scholes-Merton call formula, $$N(d_2)$$ is best interpreted as:
- A. The option's delta
- B. The risk-neutral probability that the call expires in the money
- C. The real-world probability of exercise
- D. The hedge ratio for the corresponding put

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what does $$N(d_2)$$ represent in the BSM formula?

$$N(d_2)$$ is the risk-neutral probability that the call finishes in the money (i.e., gets exercised). It appears multiplied by the discounted strike $$Ke^{-rT}$$, representing the probability-weighted cost of paying the strike.

- A is wrong; the call's delta is $$N(d_1)$$, not $$N(d_2)$$.
- C is wrong on a subtle point: it is the RISK-NEUTRAL probability, not the real-world one.
- D is wrong; $$N(d_2)$$ is not a hedge ratio.

Analogy: think of $$N(d_2)$$ as the "chance you'll actually pay the strike and collect the stock" in the make-believe risk-neutral world — it tells you how much of the strike cost to count.

📌 Exam tip: $$N(d_1)$$ = call delta; $$N(d_2)$$ = risk-neutral probability of exercise. Don't swap them — both appear among the answer choices.

**Q3.** A European call on a stock index that pays a continuous dividend yield q is valued with BSM by:
- A. Replacing r with r + q
- B. Replacing $$S_0$$ with $$S_0 e^{-qT}$$
- C. Replacing K with $$Ke^{-qT}$$
- D. Adding the present value of dividends to $$S_0$$

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how do you adjust BSM for a continuous dividend yield?

For a continuous dividend yield q, you replace the spot price $$S_0$$ with $$S_0 e^{-qT}$$ everywhere it appears (and q also enters $$d_1$$ through the $$r - q$$ term). This shrinks the effective stock price because the option holder does NOT receive the dividends.

- A adjusts the wrong variable in the wrong direction.
- C discounts the strike by q, which is not how the adjustment works (the strike is discounted by r).
- D goes the wrong way and describes the DISCRETE-dividend case, where you SUBTRACT (not add) the PV of dividends from $$S_0$$.

Analogy: dividends "leak" value out of the stock that the option holder never gets, so you value the option as if the stock were smaller by the present value of that leakage — $$S_0 e^{-qT}$$.

📌 Exam tip: Continuous yield → use $$S_0 e^{-qT}$$ (and $$r - q$$ in $$d_1$$). Discrete dividends → use $$S_0 - PV(\text{divs})$$. Dividends LOWER call values and RAISE put values.

**Q4.** A European call is worth \$4.20. The stock is at \$50, the strike is \$50, r = 4%, and T = 1 year. Using put-call parity, the European put price is closest to:
- A. \$4.20
- B. \$2.24
- C. \$6.16
- D. \$2.50

**Answer: B**

The question asks: find the put price from the call price using put-call parity.

Put-call parity: $$p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0$$.

Step 1 — discount the strike:
$$Ke^{-rT} = 50 \times e^{-0.04 \times 1} = 50 \times 0.96079 = 48.039$$

Step 2 — plug in:
$$p = 4.20 + 48.039 - 50 = 2.239 \approx \$2.24$$

- A (\$4.20) is the result of forgetting to discount the strike — then parity gives $$p = c$$, the trap.
- C (\$6.16) adds the strike-discount the wrong way.
- D (\$2.50) is a rounding/setup slip.

Analogy: put-call parity is a balance scale linking calls and puts. Once you know one side (the call) and the "interest savings" on the strike ($$Ke^{-rT}$$), the other side (the put) is forced.

📌 Exam tip: Parity $$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$ is the fastest route to the second price and a free error check. The most common mistake is using K instead of $$Ke^{-rT}$$.

**Q5.** For a non-dividend-paying stock, $$S_0 = 100$$, K = 95, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 0.5. The value of $$d_1$$ is closest to (use $$\ln(100/95) = 0.05129$$):
- A. 0.6102
- B. 0.4688
- C. 0.7500
- D. 0.3624

**Answer: A**

The question asks: compute $$d_1$$ in the BSM formula.

The formula is:
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

Step 1 — numerator. $$\ln(100/95) = 0.05129$$. The drift term: $$(0.05 + 0.20^2/2)(0.5) = (0.05 + 0.02)(0.5) = 0.07 \times 0.5 = 0.035$$.
Numerator $$= 0.05129 + 0.035 = 0.08629$$.

Step 2 — denominator: $$\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.20 \times \sqrt{0.5} = 0.20 \times 0.7071 = 0.14142$$.

Step 3 — divide:
$$d_1 = \frac{0.08629}{0.14142} = 0.6102$$

- B (0.4688) is $$d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.6102 - 0.1414$$ — a different quantity.
- C (0.7500) drops or mis-scales a term.
- D (0.3624) likely uses $$r - \sigma^2/2$$ instead of $$r + \sigma^2/2$$ — the classic sign trap.

Analogy: $$d_1$$ measures how far "in the money" the option is, in standardized (volatility-adjusted) units — the bigger $$d_1$$, the deeper in the money and the higher the call's delta.

📌 Exam tip: Use $$r + \sigma^2/2$$ in $$d_1$$ (the PLUS version). The minus version belongs to the lognormal mean of $$\ln S_T$$, not to $$d_1$$ — GARP plants both among the answers.

**Q6.** Implied volatility is best defined as:
- A. The historical standard deviation of past returns
- B. The volatility input that makes the BSM model price equal the observed market price
- C. The volatility forecast published by exchanges
- D. The average of daily volatilities over the past month

**Answer: B**

The question asks: define implied volatility.

Implied volatility is the value of σ that, when plugged into the BSM formula, produces a model price equal to the option's actual market price. Because BSM cannot be inverted algebraically for σ, it is found by iteration (trial and error). It reflects the market's forward-looking view of volatility.

- A and D both describe REALIZED (historical) volatility — backward-looking, computed from past returns.
- C is not a definition of implied volatility.

Analogy: implied volatility is like reverse-engineering a recipe — you taste the finished dish (the market price) and figure out how much of the key ingredient (σ) must have gone in to produce it.

📌 Exam tip: Realized vol looks backward (from past returns); implied vol looks forward (backed out of current market prices). Implied vol is the market's consensus and varies by strike/maturity (the volatility smile/skew).

**Q7.** Which of the following is NOT an assumption of the Black-Scholes-Merton model?
- A. The stock price follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility
- B. There are no transaction costs or taxes
- C. The risk-free rate is constant
- D. Investors are risk-averse and require a risk premium that enters the option price

**Answer: D**

The question asks: identify the statement that is NOT a BSM assumption.

BSM prices options by no-arbitrage replication, in which the expected return μ — and any risk premium — drops out entirely. Only σ matters. So the option price does NOT depend on investors' risk preferences; statement D contradicts the model.

- A is a core BSM assumption (GBM, constant σ, no jumps).
- B is a BSM assumption (frictionless markets).
- C is a BSM assumption (constant r).

Analogy: just like the binomial tree, BSM's price comes from "what it costs to replicate the option," not from how much investors fear risk. Risk premiums cancel out.

📌 Exam tip: A defining feature of BSM (and binomial) pricing: μ and risk premia are irrelevant — only volatility, rates, time, and the strike matter. Any answer invoking "risk premium in the price" is wrong.

**Q8.** Other things equal, increasing the continuous dividend yield q on a stock will:
- A. Increase call values and decrease put values
- B. Decrease call values and increase put values
- C. Increase both call and put values
- D. Have no effect on option values

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how do higher dividends affect call and put prices?

Dividends transfer value out of the stock (the price drops on ex-dividend dates), and the option holder does not receive them. This makes the effective stock price lower ($$S_0 e^{-qT}$$):
- Lower effective stock price → calls worth LESS (a call benefits from a high stock price).
- Lower effective stock price → puts worth MORE (a put benefits from a low stock price).

- A reverses the effect.
- C and D are both wrong; dividends have opposite effects on calls vs puts, not equal/no effect.

Analogy: dividends are like air slowly leaking from a balloon (the stock). A call (betting the balloon stays big) loses value; a put (betting it shrinks) gains value.

📌 Exam tip: Higher dividends → calls DOWN, puts UP. This is also why an American call on a dividend stock might be exercised early (just before ex-dividend), unlike on a non-dividend stock.

**Q9.** An American call option on a NON-dividend-paying stock should be:
- A. Exercised early as soon as it is in the money
- B. Never exercised early, so its value equals the European call value
- C. Always worth more than the underlying stock
- D. Valued only with a binomial tree, never with BSM

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how should an American call on a non-dividend stock be treated?

An American call on a non-dividend stock is NEVER optimally exercised early — you always do better selling the option or holding it (you keep the time value and the interest on the unpaid strike). Therefore its value equals the European call's, and BSM applies directly.

- A is the classic mistake; early exercise throws away time value with nothing (no dividend) to gain.
- C is false; an option is worth less than the stock (delta < 1).
- D is false; precisely because early exercise is never optimal, BSM (European) gives the right value.

Analogy: exercising early is like cashing in a coupon before it's due when there's no penalty for waiting — you lose the remaining flexibility for no reason.

📌 Exam tip: American call on a NON-dividend stock = European call (BSM works). With dividends, early exercise can be optimal just before an ex-dividend date — use Black's approximation or a tree. American PUTS may be exercised early even with no dividends.

**Q10.** A company has 10 million shares outstanding and issues 2 million warrants. If a regular BSM call on the (post-announcement) stock is worth \$6, the approximate value of one warrant, accounting for dilution, is closest to:
- A. \$6.00
- B. \$5.00
- C. \$7.20
- D. \$1.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks: adjust a call value for the dilution caused by warrants.

When warrants are exercised, the company issues NEW shares, diluting existing holders. The warrant value is scaled by the dilution factor:
$$\text{Warrant value} \approx \frac{N}{N + M} \times c$$
where N = existing shares, M = warrants.

Step 1 — dilution factor:
$$\frac{N}{N+M} = \frac{10}{10 + 2} = \frac{10}{12} = 0.8333$$

Step 2 — multiply by the call value:
$$0.8333 \times 6 = \$5.00$$

- A (\$6.00) ignores dilution entirely — treats the warrant like a regular call.
- C (\$7.20) multiplies by $$12/10 = 1.2$$ — the dilution factor upside-down (makes it MORE valuable, which is backward).
- D (\$1.00) subtracts incorrectly.

Analogy: issuing new shares to warrant holders is like cutting a pizza into more slices — each existing slice (and each warrant's claim) is a bit smaller, so the warrant is worth less than a plain call.

📌 Exam tip: Warrant value ≈ $$\frac{N}{N+M} \times c$$ — the dilution factor is always less than 1, so warrants are worth LESS than otherwise-identical calls.
