**Q1.** A trader writes (sells) 500 put options, each with a delta of −0.38. To delta-hedge this position, the trader should:
- A. Buy 190 shares
- B. Sell 190 shares
- C. Buy 310 shares
- D. Sell 310 shares

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how do you delta-hedge a short put position?

Step 1 — find the position delta. The trader is SHORT 500 puts, and each put has delta −0.38. A short position flips the sign:
$$\text{Position delta} = -500 \times (-0.38) = +190$$

Step 2 — neutralize it. The position has +190 delta, so you need −190 delta from shares. Each share has delta +1, so SELL 190 shares.

- A buys instead of sells — that ADDS +190 more delta, doubling the exposure to +380.
- C and D use 310, which would come from $$500 \times 0.62$$ (using the wrong delta, $$1 - 0.38$$).

Analogy: a short put behaves like being long the stock (you gain if the stock rises). To offset that "long" tilt, you sell shares to flatten out.

📌 Exam tip: Position delta = (number of options) × (option delta), with a sign flip for short positions. Then offset with shares of the opposite sign. A short put → positive delta → sell shares.

**Q2.** Gamma is greatest for options that are:
- A. Deep in the money with long maturity
- B. At the money close to expiry
- C. Deep out of the money close to expiry
- D. At the money with long maturity

**Answer: B**

The question asks: where does gamma peak?

Gamma measures how fast delta changes. Delta flips most violently from near 0 to near 1 right across the strike when there is little time left — so gamma is largest for at-the-money options close to expiry.

- A: deep ITM options have delta near 1 that barely moves → low gamma.
- C: deep OTM options have delta near 0 that barely moves → low gamma.
- D describes where VEGA peaks (ATM, long maturity) — the opposite maturity pattern from gamma.

Analogy: an ATM option near expiry is like a coin balanced on its edge at the last second — the tiniest nudge tips delta dramatically. That sensitivity IS high gamma.

📌 Exam tip: Gamma peaks ATM, SHORT maturity (delta snaps across the strike near expiry). Vega peaks ATM, LONG maturity. Don't mix the two maturity patterns up.

**Q3.** A delta-neutral portfolio has a gamma of +8,000. The stock suddenly jumps \$3 (in either direction). Using the delta-gamma approximation, the approximate P&L is:
- A. +\$24,000
- B. −\$36,000
- C. +\$36,000
- D. Zero — the portfolio is delta-neutral

**Answer: C**

The question asks: estimate the P&L of a delta-neutral, long-gamma book after a \$3 move.

The delta-gamma approximation:
$$\Delta P \approx \delta \, \Delta S + \tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma (\Delta S)^2$$

Since the portfolio is delta-neutral, $$\delta = 0$$, so the first term vanishes:
$$\Delta P \approx \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8{,}000 \times (3)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8{,}000 \times 9 = +\$36{,}000$$

This is positive for a move in EITHER direction (because $$(\Delta S)^2$$ is positive either way) — the payoff of being long gamma.

- A (+\$24,000) is $$\Gamma \times \Delta S = 8{,}000 \times 3$$ — forgets both the ½ and the square.
- B (−\$36,000) gets the magnitude right but the wrong sign; long gamma GAINS on big moves.
- D ignores the gamma term — but gamma is exactly what produces P&L when delta is zero.

Analogy: long gamma is like a trampoline — whichever way you fall (up or down), the curvature bounces you to a gain.

📌 Exam tip: For a delta-neutral book, P&L ≈ ½Γ(ΔS)². Long gamma → gains on any large move; short gamma → losses. Don't forget the ½ and the SQUARE.

**Q4.** A dealer writes 1,000 calls with delta 0.62 and delta-hedges. The stock then rallies and the calls' delta rises to 0.70. To re-establish delta neutrality, the dealer must:
- A. Sell 80 shares
- B. Buy 80 shares
- C. Sell 620 shares
- D. Buy 700 shares from scratch

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how does the dealer rebalance after the delta rises?

Step 1 — initial hedge. Short 1,000 calls → position delta = $$-1{,}000 \times 0.62 = -620$$. The dealer buys 620 shares to reach zero.

Step 2 — after the rally, delta rises to 0.70. New option delta = $$-1{,}000 \times 0.70 = -700$$. Combined with the 620 shares already held: $$-700 + 620 = -80$$.

Step 3 — to flatten the −80, buy 80 more shares (bringing the total to 700).

- A sells, which would worsen the imbalance to −160.
- C and D ignore the shares already held (620) and re-buy the whole position.

Analogy: the dealer keeps topping up shares as the stock rises — "buy high." This buy-high, sell-low pattern of a short-gamma hedger is exactly the rebalancing cost the option premium was paid to cover.

📌 Exam tip: Rebalancing only adjusts for the CHANGE in delta. A short-call (short-gamma) hedger buys more as prices rise and sells as they fall — the costly "buy high, sell low" signature of negative gamma.

**Q5.** Which two Greeks are IDENTICAL for a European call and a European put with the same strike and expiration?
- A. Delta and theta
- B. Gamma and vega
- C. Delta and rho
- D. Theta and rho

**Answer: B**

The question asks: which Greeks match between a same-strike, same-expiry European call and put?

Differentiating put-call parity ($$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$$) shows that the $$S_0$$ and K terms drop out for the second derivative with respect to S (gamma) and the derivative with respect to σ (vega). So gamma and vega are identical for the call and put.

- A: delta differs ($$N(d_1)$$ vs $$N(d_1) - 1$$) and theta differs.
- C: delta and rho both differ between call and put.
- D: theta and rho both differ.

Analogy: a call and put on the same stock share the same "curvature" (gamma) and "volatility sensitivity" (vega) because those properties come from the shared underlying's wiggle, not from which direction you're betting.

📌 Exam tip: Gamma and vega are the SAME for a matched European call/put pair. Delta, theta, and rho all DIFFER. This comes straight from differentiating put-call parity.

**Q6.** For a delta-neutral portfolio, which Greek does theta most closely (and inversely) relate to?
- A. Delta
- B. Gamma
- C. Vega
- D. Rho

**Answer: B**

The question asks: which Greek does theta trade off against for a delta-neutral book?

For a delta-neutral portfolio, the BSM relationship reduces to:
$$\Theta + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi$$

This ties theta directly to gamma: large positive gamma forces large negative theta, and vice versa. You cannot own convexity (gamma) without paying time decay (theta) — theta is the "rent" on gamma.

- A: delta is zero by assumption, so it doesn't enter this trade-off.
- C and D (vega, rho) are not the variables linked by this delta-neutral relationship.

Analogy: gamma is the "good" curvature that pays off on big moves; theta is the daily "rent" you pay to keep it. They are two sides of the same coin.

📌 Exam tip: For a delta-neutral book, $$\Theta + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \Gamma = r\Pi$$. Big positive gamma ↔ big negative theta. Theta can serve as a rough proxy for gamma on such a book.

**Q7.** A book has delta −450 and gamma −6,000. A traded call has delta 0.55 and gamma 1.8. To make the book delta- AND gamma-neutral, the trader should FIRST:
- A. Sell shares to fix delta, then add options for gamma
- B. Buy 3,333 calls to fix gamma, then sell 1,383 shares to fix delta
- C. Buy 6,000 calls, then buy 450 shares
- D. Sell 3,333 calls, then buy 1,383 shares

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how do you build a delta-gamma-neutral book, and in what order?

Order matters: fix gamma FIRST with options (shares have zero gamma), THEN fix delta with shares (shares don't disturb gamma).

Step 1 — gamma. Need to offset −6,000 gamma using a call with gamma 1.8:
$$w = \frac{6{,}000}{1.8} = 3{,}333 \text{ calls (buy)}$$
Check: $$-6{,}000 + 3{,}333 \times 1.8 \approx 0$$.

Step 2 — delta. Buying 3,333 calls adds delta: $$3{,}333 \times 0.55 = 1{,}833$$. New delta = $$-450 + 1{,}833 = +1{,}383$$. To neutralize, SELL 1,383 shares (each share has delta 1).

- A reverses the order — fixing delta with shares first, then adding options, would wreck the delta hedge.
- C uses 6,000 directly as a contract count (ignores the option's gamma) and the wrong delta step.
- D sells calls (wrong sign — that worsens the negative gamma).

Analogy: build the foundation (gamma) before the walls (delta). If you put up walls first (delta hedge with shares), adding the foundation later (options) cracks them.

📌 Exam tip: Gamma BEFORE delta. Use options for gamma (shares have none), then shares for delta (shares don't change gamma). Watch the signs of any short positions in the book.

**Q8.** A trader holds a long call on a non-dividend stock. If the risk-free interest rate rises while everything else stays the same, the call's value will most likely:
- A. Fall, because higher rates push the stock price down
- B. Rise, because the present value of the strike paid at expiry falls
- C. Stay the same — interest rates do not affect option values
- D. Fall, because rho is negative for call options

**Answer: B**

The question asks how a long call reacts to a higher risk-free rate $$r$$ — that sensitivity is called **rho**.

When you own a call, you pay the strike $$K$$ only later, at expiry. Its cost today is the present value $$Ke^{-rT}$$. A higher $$r$$ **discounts** that future payment more heavily, so $$Ke^{-rT}$$ shrinks — paying later is cheaper in today's money. A cheaper effective cost makes the call **more** valuable. So a long call has **positive rho** and its value rises — answer **B**.

- A is wrong: BSM treats the stock price as given; rho works through the present value of the strike, not by moving the stock.
- C is wrong: rates do matter (that is exactly what rho measures); the effect is small but real.
- D is wrong: it states the sign backwards — rho is **positive** for long calls (it is negative for long puts).

📌 Exam tip: Long call → positive rho; long put → negative rho. The reason is always the discounted strike $$Ke^{-rT}$$: higher $$r$$ helps the call (pay less, later) and hurts the put (receive less, later).

**Q9.** Theta (time decay) is MOST negative for an option that is:
- A. Deep in the money with long maturity
- B. At the money close to expiry
- C. Deep out of the money with long maturity
- D. At the money with long maturity

**Answer: B**

The question asks: where is theta most negative (fastest time decay)?

Theta is most negative for at-the-money options close to expiry. Their value is almost entirely time value, which evaporates rapidly in the final days — mirroring where gamma peaks (ATM, near expiry), but with the opposite sign.

- A and C: deep ITM/OTM options have little time value to lose, so theta is small.
- D: ATM with LONG maturity decays slowly — there is plenty of time left, so daily decay is gentle (this is where vega peaks instead).

Analogy: an ATM option near expiry is like an ice cube on a hot day in its final minutes — it melts (loses value) fastest right at the end.

📌 Exam tip: Theta most negative = ATM, near expiry (it mirrors gamma's peak, opposite sign). Time decay accelerates as expiry approaches for at-the-money options.

**Q10.** Portfolio insurance via synthetic put replication involves:
- A. Buying more stock as the market falls and selling as it rises
- B. Selling stock (or index futures) as the market falls and buying as it rises
- C. Holding a fixed stock position regardless of market moves
- D. Buying actual put options on every stock in the portfolio

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how does synthetic put replication (portfolio insurance) work?

A protective put has a delta that becomes more negative as the market falls. To replicate it WITHOUT buying actual puts, you reduce equity exposure as the market drops (sell stock or index futures) and increase it as the market rises (buy back) — keeping your net exposure equal to the put-adjusted delta.

- A is backward — buying as it falls would amplify losses, the opposite of insurance.
- C describes no hedging at all.
- D describes buying REAL puts, which is protection but not SYNTHETIC replication (the whole point of synthetic insurance is to avoid buying puts directly).

Analogy: it's automatic "selling into a falling market" — like a thermostat that cuts heat as the room warms. The danger: everyone's programs sell at once when markets gap down (as in October 1987), amplifying the crash.

📌 Exam tip: Synthetic put = sell as the market falls, buy as it rises. Its key flaw is the assumption of continuous trading and stable volatility — exactly what fails in a crash, when liquidity disappears.
