**Q1.** $$X$$ and $$Y$$ have $$\sigma_X = 5$$, $$\sigma_Y = 8$$, and correlation $$\rho = -0.25$$. What is $$\mathrm{Var}(X + Y)$$?
- A. 49
- B. 69
- C. 89
- D. 109

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the variance of the sum of two correlated variables. First we need the covariance, then we plug into the variance-of-a-sum formula.

Step 1 — covariance from correlation:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \rho \, \sigma_X \, \sigma_Y = -0.25 \times 5 \times 8 = -10$$

Step 2 — variance of the sum:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)$$

$$= 5^2 + 8^2 + 2(-10) = 25 + 64 - 20 = 69$$

So the answer is 69 (option B). The negative covariance *reduces* the total variance — the two variables partly offset each other.

- A (49) subtracts too much, e.g., using $$25 + 64 - 40$$, double-counting the covariance term.
- C (89) just adds the variances ($$25 + 64$$) and ignores the covariance term entirely.
- D (109) adds the covariance instead of subtracting ($$25 + 64 + 20$$), using a positive sign for a negative covariance.

📌 Exam tip: $$\mathrm{Var}(X+Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Cov}$$. Get the covariance sign right — negative correlation lowers the sum's variance (diversification).

**Q2.** If $$U = 3X + 2$$ and $$V = -4Y + 1$$, and $$\rho(X, Y) = 0.5$$, then $$\rho(U, V)$$ equals:
- A. 0.0
- B. $$-0.5$$
- C. 0.5
- D. $$-6.0$$

**Answer: B**

The question asks how linear transformations of two variables affect their correlation.

Correlation is unit-free, so multiplying a variable by a positive number or adding a constant does NOT change its magnitude. The only thing that can change is the *sign*, and that depends on the signs of the two scale factors.

The scales here are $$a = 3$$ (for $$X$$) and $$c = -4$$ (for $$Y$$). The sign of their product is:

$$\mathrm{sign}(a \times c) = \mathrm{sign}(3 \times -4) = \mathrm{sign}(-12) = \text{negative}$$

So the correlation keeps its magnitude 0.5 but flips sign:

$$\rho(U, V) = \mathrm{sign}(ac) \times \rho(X,Y) = -0.5$$

So the answer is $$-0.5$$ (option B). The constants $$+2$$ and $$+1$$ have no effect at all.

- A (0.0) wrongly assumes the transformation destroys the relationship; it doesn't.
- C (0.5) keeps the original sign, ignoring that one scale ($$-4$$) is negative.
- D ($$-6.0$$) multiplies the correlation by the scale factors ($$0.5 \times 3 \times -4$$) — but correlation is unit-free and bounded in $$[-1, 1]$$, so it can never be $$-6$$.

📌 Exam tip: Linear transforms never change the *magnitude* of correlation; they only flip its sign when the two scale factors have opposite signs. Constants are irrelevant.

**Q3.** Zero correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$ guarantees that they are independent:
- A. Always
- B. Never
- C. Only if $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are jointly normally distributed
- D. Only if both have zero mean

**Answer: C**

The question tests the relationship between zero correlation and independence.

Correlation only measures *linear* co-movement. Two variables can be perfectly dependent in a nonlinear way yet have zero correlation — for example $$Y = X^2$$ with a symmetric $$X$$ has correlation 0 but $$Y$$ is completely determined by $$X$$. So in general, zero correlation does NOT imply independence.

The one important exception: if $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are *jointly normally distributed*, then zero correlation does imply independence. So option C is correct.

- A (Always) is the classic mistake — true only under joint normality, not in general.
- B (Never) is too strong; under joint normality it does hold.
- D (zero mean) is irrelevant; the means have nothing to do with the correlation-independence link.

📌 Exam tip: "Uncorrelated = independent" ONLY under joint normality. Otherwise zero correlation just means no *linear* relationship; nonlinear dependence can still exist.

**Q4.** Daily returns are iid with daily variance 0.0004. What is the standard deviation of the 25-day return?
- A. 0.01
- B. 0.02
- C. 0.05
- D. 0.10

**Answer: D**

The question asks us to scale risk over time using the iid square-root-of-time rule.

Step 1 — daily standard deviation:

$$\sigma_{\text{day}} = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$$

Step 2 — for iid returns, variance adds over time, so the variance over $$n$$ days is $$n \times \sigma_{\text{day}}^2$$, and the standard deviation scales by $$\sqrt{n}$$:

$$\sigma_{25} = \sigma_{\text{day}} \times \sqrt{n} = 0.02 \times \sqrt{25} = 0.02 \times 5 = 0.10$$

So the 25-day standard deviation is 0.10 (option D).

- A (0.01) divides instead of multiplying, as if risk shrinks over time.
- B (0.02) is just the daily standard deviation, forgetting to scale up for 25 days.
- C (0.05) multiplies the daily variance by something, or uses $$\sqrt{n}$$ on the variance instead of the standard deviation.

📌 Exam tip: Variance scales with time ($$\times t$$); standard deviation scales with $$\sqrt{t}$$. Convert variance to SD first, then multiply by $$\sqrt{t}$$. This requires iid returns.

**Q5.** Asset A has $$\sigma_A = 20\%$$, Asset B has $$\sigma_B = 12\%$$, and their correlation is $$\rho = 0.3$$. What is the covariance between A and B?
- A. 0.0072
- B. 0.0240
- C. 0.0720
- D. 0.7200

**Answer: A**

The question asks us to convert a correlation into a covariance.

The relationship is:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(A, B) = \rho \times \sigma_A \times \sigma_B$$

Convert the percentages to decimals: $$\sigma_A = 0.20$$, $$\sigma_B = 0.12$$. Then:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(A, B) = 0.3 \times 0.20 \times 0.12 = 0.3 \times 0.024 = 0.0072$$

So the covariance is 0.0072 (option A).

- B (0.0240) is $$\sigma_A \times \sigma_B = 0.20 \times 0.12$$, forgetting to multiply by the correlation 0.3.
- C (0.0720) forgets to convert one percentage (e.g., $$0.3 \times 0.20 \times 1.2$$ or similar decimal-place slip).
- D (0.7200) keeps everything in whole-number percentages ($$0.3 \times 20 \times 12 / 100$$ mis-scaled), inflating by 100.

📌 Exam tip: $$\mathrm{Cov} = \rho\,\sigma_A\,\sigma_B$$. Always convert % to decimals before multiplying, and watch the decimal places — covariance answers are usually small.

**Q6.** Asset A ($$\sigma_A = 20\%$$) and Asset B ($$\sigma_B = 12\%$$) have $$\rho = 0.3$$, so $$\mathrm{Cov}(A,B) = 0.0072$$. A portfolio holds 60% A and 40% B. What is the portfolio variance?
- A. 0.01670
- B. 0.02016
- C. 0.02822
- D. 0.16800

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the variance of a two-asset portfolio with weights $$a = 0.6$$ and $$b = 0.4$$.

The portfolio variance formula is:

$$\sigma_P^2 = a^2 \sigma_A^2 + b^2 \sigma_B^2 + 2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}(A, B)$$

Compute each piece (using decimals: $$\sigma_A^2 = 0.20^2 = 0.04$$, $$\sigma_B^2 = 0.12^2 = 0.0144$$):

- First term: $$0.6^2 \times 0.04 = 0.36 \times 0.04 = 0.0144$$
- Second term: $$0.4^2 \times 0.0144 = 0.16 \times 0.0144 = 0.002304$$
- Cross term: $$2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.0072 = 0.48 \times 0.0072 = 0.003456$$

Add them up:

$$\sigma_P^2 = 0.0144 + 0.002304 + 0.003456 = 0.02016$$

So the portfolio variance is 0.02016 (option B). (Its square root, about 14.2%, is below the 16.8% weighted average of the SDs — that's diversification.)

- A (0.01670) drops the cross (covariance) term entirely.
- C (0.02822) uses weights of 1 instead of squared weights, or double-counts the cross term.
- D (0.16800) is the weighted-average standard deviation ($$0.6 \times 20\% + 0.4 \times 12\% = 16.8\%$$), which is not the variance.

📌 Exam tip: Portfolio variance uses *squared* weights on the variance terms plus a $$2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}$$ cross term. Don't confuse it with the simple weighted average of standard deviations.

**Q7.** Independence between two random variables implies which of the following?
- A. Their covariance is zero.
- B. Their covariance equals 1.
- C. Their correlation is positive.
- D. Their variances are equal.

**Answer: A**

The question asks what *must* follow if two variables are independent.

If $$X$$ and $$Y$$ are independent, knowing one tells you nothing about the other, so they have no co-movement at all. This forces:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$$

So option A is correct. (Note the reverse is not guaranteed: zero covariance does not always imply independence — that only holds under joint normality.)

- B (covariance = 1) confuses covariance with a correlation bound; independent variables have covariance 0, not 1.
- C (positive correlation) is wrong; independence gives *zero* correlation, not positive.
- D (equal variances) is unrelated; independence says nothing about the sizes of the two variances.

📌 Exam tip: Independence ⟹ zero covariance (and zero correlation). The arrow only runs one way in general; zero covariance ⟹ independence only under joint normality.

**Q8.** For an iid sequence $$X_1, \dots, X_n$$ each with variance $$\sigma^2$$, what is the variance of the sum $$\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$$?
- A. $$\sigma^2$$
- B. $$\sigma^2 / n$$
- C. $$n\sigma^2$$
- D. $$\sigma\sqrt{n}$$

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the variance of a sum of $$n$$ iid variables.

Because iid variables are independent, all covariance terms are zero, so the variance of the sum is just the sum of the individual variances:

$$\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = n\sigma^2$$

So option C is correct. Variance of the *sum* grows linearly with $$n$$.

- A ($$\sigma^2$$) is the variance of a single observation, not the sum.
- B ($$\sigma^2 / n$$) is the variance of the *sample mean* (average), not the sum. The sum and the mean scale very differently.
- D ($$\sigma\sqrt{n}$$) is the standard *deviation* of the sum, not its variance.

📌 Exam tip: For iid sums: variance $$= n\sigma^2$$, SD $$= \sigma\sqrt{n}$$. For the sample mean: variance $$= \sigma^2/n$$, SD $$= \sigma/\sqrt{n}$$. Don't mix sum vs mean.

**Q9.** The square-root-of-time scaling rule for volatility requires that returns are:
- A. Normally distributed
- B. Independent and identically distributed (zero autocorrelation, constant variance)
- C. Positively autocorrelated
- D. Mean-reverting

**Answer: B**

The question asks under what condition $$\sigma_T = \sigma\sqrt{T}$$ is valid.

The $$\sqrt{t}$$ rule comes directly from the iid property: when returns are independent (zero autocorrelation) and identically distributed (constant variance), the variance of a multi-period return is the sum of single-period variances, $$T\sigma^2$$, so volatility scales as $$\sigma\sqrt{T}$$. So option B is correct.

- A (normal) is not required; iid is what matters. The CLT may make multi-period returns roughly normal, but the scaling rule itself only needs iid, not normality of each return.
- C (positive autocorrelation) actually *breaks* the rule: with positive autocorrelation, true multi-period risk *exceeds* $$\sigma\sqrt{T}$$.
- D (mean reversion) also breaks the rule, making true risk *below* $$\sigma\sqrt{T}$$.

📌 Exam tip: $$\sqrt{t}$$ scaling needs iid (no autocorrelation, constant variance) — not normality. Positive autocorrelation ⟹ understates risk; mean reversion ⟹ overstates risk.

**Q10.** From a joint probability table, $$P(Y=1, X=1) = 0.55$$ and the marginal $$P(X=1) = 0.70$$. What is the conditional probability $$P(Y=1 \mid X=1)$$?
- A. 0.385
- B. 0.550
- C. 0.700
- D. 0.786

**Answer: D**

The question asks for a conditional probability from a joint probability table.

The rule is: divide the joint probability by the marginal probability of the condition.

$$P(Y=1 \mid X=1) = \frac{P(Y=1, X=1)}{P(X=1)} = \frac{0.55}{0.70} = 0.7857 \approx 0.786$$

So the answer is 0.786 (option D). We are restricting attention to the world where $$X=1$$ (which has total probability 0.70) and asking what fraction of that also has $$Y=1$$.

- A (0.385) multiplies the two numbers ($$0.55 \times 0.70$$) instead of dividing.
- B (0.550) just reports the joint probability, forgetting to divide by the marginal.
- C (0.700) reports the marginal of $$X$$, the denominator, not the conditional.

📌 Exam tip: Conditional = joint ÷ marginal of the condition. The denominator is always the probability of what you're conditioning on (here $$P(X=1)$$).
