**Q1.** A bond has price \$100, modified duration 7.0, and convexity 60. Using the duration-plus-convexity estimate, the price change for a 100bp (1%) rise in yield is closest to:
- A. −\$7.00
- B. −\$6.70
- C. −\$7.30
- D. −\$6.40

**Answer: B**

The question asks: estimate the dollar price change for a 1% yield increase, including the convexity correction.

The second-order formula is:
$$\Delta P \approx -D \times P \times \Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2} \times C \times P \times (\Delta y)^2$$

Step 1 — duration term ($$\Delta y = +0.01$$):
$$-7.0 \times 100 \times 0.01 = -7.00$$

Step 2 — convexity term:
$$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 60 \times 100 \times (0.01)^2 = 30 \times 100 \times 0.0001 = 0.30$$

Step 3 — add them: $$-7.00 + 0.30 = -\$6.70$$.

- A (−\$7.00) uses duration only and forgets the convexity cushion. Convexity always makes the loss a little smaller.
- C (−\$7.30) is the answer for a 1% FALL in yield (both terms positive: +7.00 + 0.30), not a rise.
- D (−\$6.40) double-counts the convexity term (forgets the ½), subtracting 0.60 too much from the wrong direction.

Analogy: duration is the straight-line guess for how the price moves; convexity is the curve that bends the line in your favor — it softens losses when rates rise and boosts gains when rates fall.

📌 Exam tip: Convexity ALWAYS adds value (for option-free bonds), in both directions. Loss < duration estimate, gain > duration estimate. And never forget the ½ on the convexity term.

**Q2.** A bond has price \$98, modified duration 5.2, and convexity 40. Using the duration-plus-convexity estimate, the price change for a 150bp yield increase is closest to:
- A. −\$7.64
- B. −\$7.20
- C. −\$6.96
- D. −\$8.10

**Answer: B**

The question asks: estimate the dollar price change for a 1.5% yield rise.

Set $$\Delta y = +0.015$$.

Step 1 — duration term:
$$-5.2 \times 98 \times 0.015 = -7.644$$

Step 2 — convexity term:
$$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 40 \times 98 \times (0.015)^2 = 20 \times 98 \times 0.000225 = 0.441$$

Step 3 — add: $$-7.644 + 0.441 = -\$7.20$$.

- A (−\$7.64) is the duration-only estimate, leaving out the convexity cushion.
- C (−\$6.96) over-applies convexity (perhaps forgetting the ½ and so adding ~0.88 instead of 0.44).
- D (−\$8.10) goes the wrong way on convexity, subtracting it.

Analogy: same as a car's braking distance — duration is the rough estimate, and convexity is the small correction that makes the real number a touch less bad.

📌 Exam tip: Watch the units. $$\Delta y$$ must be a decimal (150bp = 0.015) and $$(\Delta y)^2 = 0.000225$$ is tiny — that is why convexity matters more for big moves.

**Q3.** A trader is long a bond portfolio with DV01 = \$12,500. Treasury note futures have a DV01 of \$85 per contract. To hedge the portfolio, the trader should:
- A. Buy 147 contracts
- B. Sell 147 contracts
- C. Sell 85 contracts
- D. Buy 12,500 contracts

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how many futures contracts neutralize the portfolio's interest-rate risk, and in which direction?

The hedge ratio is:
$$N = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{position}}{\text{DV01}_{hedge}} = -\frac{12{,}500}{85} = -147.06 \approx -147$$

The negative sign means take the OPPOSITE side. Since the trader is LONG the portfolio (loses when rates rise), the hedge must be SHORT — sell 147 contracts.

- A buys instead of sells — that doubles the risk rather than offsetting it.
- C confuses the per-contract DV01 (\$85) with the contract count.
- D treats the portfolio DV01 as a contract count — units error.

Analogy: if your boat tilts left, you lean right to balance it. The portfolio gains/loses one way, so the hedge must move the opposite way by an equal amount.

📌 Exam tip: Hedge contracts = portfolio DV01 ÷ hedge DV01, then take the opposite side. Long bonds → short the hedge. The minus sign is telling you the direction.

**Q4.** Which duration measure must be used for a callable bond, whose cash flows change when interest rates move?
- A. Macaulay duration
- B. Modified duration
- C. Effective duration
- D. Dollar duration

**Answer: C**

The question asks: which duration handles bonds whose cash flows depend on rates (like callables)?

Effective duration is computed by actually shifting the whole yield curve up and down and repricing the bond:
$$D_{Eff} = \frac{P_- - P_+}{2 \times P \times \Delta y}$$
Because it re-prices the bond at new rates, it automatically captures the fact that a call may be exercised when rates fall — the cash flows change. The yield-based formulas (Macaulay, modified) assume fixed cash flows and break down here.

- A (Macaulay) is the average time to cash flows, assuming they are fixed.
- B (Modified) is derived from Macaulay; it also assumes fixed cash flows, so it fails for callables.
- D (Dollar duration) is just modified duration scaled by price; same fixed-cash-flow problem.

Analogy: modified duration is like predicting a train's arrival assuming it never changes route. A callable bond CAN change route (get called early), so you need a method that re-checks the schedule at each rate level — that's effective duration.

📌 Exam tip: Embedded options (callable, putable, MBS) → effective duration. Option-free bonds → modified ≈ effective, either works.

**Q5.** A zero-coupon bond has a maturity of 8 years. Its Macaulay duration is:
- A. Less than 8 years
- B. Exactly 8 years
- C. Greater than 8 years
- D. Cannot be determined without the yield

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what is the Macaulay duration of an 8-year zero?

Macaulay duration is the value-weighted average time to receive the bond's cash flows. A zero-coupon bond has only ONE cash flow — the face value at maturity. So the "average time" to its single cash flow is exactly the maturity: 8 years.

- A is wrong; that would require some cash flows arriving earlier (coupons), which a zero has none of.
- C is impossible; you cannot have an average time later than the only payment date.
- D is wrong; the answer is 8 regardless of yield, because there is only one cash flow.

Analogy: if you get a single paycheck on one date, the "average payday" is just that date — no averaging needed.

📌 Exam tip: For a zero, Macaulay duration = maturity. A zero is the maximum-duration bond for its maturity, which is why lower-coupon bonds always have higher duration.

**Q6.** Holding maturity constant, which bond has the HIGHEST modified duration?
- A. A high-coupon bond at a high yield
- B. A low-coupon bond at a low yield
- C. A high-coupon bond at a low yield
- D. A low-coupon bond at a high yield

**Answer: B**

The question asks: which combination of coupon and yield maximizes duration (for a given maturity)?

Two rules push duration up:
- Lower coupon → higher duration (more value sits in the distant final payment).
- Lower yield → higher duration (distant cash flows are discounted less, so they weigh more).

The bond that is both low-coupon AND low-yield maximizes duration. So B wins on both counts.

- A is the opposite on both (high coupon, high yield) → lowest duration.
- C gets the yield right (low) but the coupon wrong (high).
- D gets the coupon right (low) but the yield wrong (high).

Analogy: duration is "how far out your money's center of gravity sits." Small coupons mean most of the weight is at the far end (maturity), and a low yield keeps that far-end weight heavy — both stretch the center of gravity outward.

📌 Exam tip: Duration up with: longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield. Memorize the three "levers."

**Q7.** A portfolio holds \$60M of Bond X (modified duration 4) and \$40M of Bond Y (modified duration 9). The portfolio's modified duration is closest to:
- A. 6.0
- B. 6.5
- C. 5.5
- D. 13.0

**Answer: A**

The question asks: combine two bonds' durations into a single portfolio duration.

Portfolio duration is the value-weighted average of the component durations. Total value = $$60 + 40 = \$100M$$.

Step 1 — weights: Bond X = $$60/100 = 0.6$$; Bond Y = $$40/100 = 0.4$$.

Step 2 — weighted average:
$$D_{port} = 0.6 \times 4 + 0.4 \times 9 = 2.4 + 3.6 = 6.0$$

- B (6.5) is the simple (unweighted) average of 4 and 9 — ignores that more money is in the shorter bond.
- C (5.5) miscomputes weights or mixes them up.
- D (13.0) adds the durations instead of averaging — durations average, they don't sum.

Analogy: portfolio duration is like a class's average test score — you weight each student's score by... no, simpler: weight each bond's duration by how many dollars are in it, then average.

📌 Exam tip: Portfolio duration and convexity are value-weighted averages. DV01s, on the other hand, simply add up. Don't sum the durations.

**Q8.** A barbell and a bullet portfolio have identical market value and identical duration. Following a large 200bp parallel downward shift in rates, the barbell will:
- A. Underperform, because of its longer cash flows
- B. Outperform, because it has greater convexity
- C. Match the bullet exactly, because durations are equal
- D. Outperform only if the curve also steepens

**Answer: B**

The question asks: with equal duration, which structure wins after a big parallel move?

Because durations are equal, the first-order (duration) P&L is the same for both. The difference is convexity: a barbell spreads cash flows across short and long maturities, so its cash flows are more dispersed, giving it MORE convexity than the bullet. Higher convexity adds extra value in any large parallel move — up or down. So the barbell outperforms.

- A is wrong; the longer leg is offset by the short leg to match duration, and the net effect of extra convexity is positive.
- C is true only for tiny moves; for a large 200bp move, convexity (the curvature) creates a gap.
- D is wrong; the barbell wins on a parallel move regardless of steepening. (Steepening would actually hurt the barbell's long leg — a separate risk.)

Analogy: two cars with the same speed (duration) but one has better suspension (convexity). On a smooth road they tie, but over a big bump (large rate move) the better-suspension car comes out ahead.

📌 Exam tip: "Same duration, big parallel move, which wins?" → barbell (more convexity), both directions. "Which has the higher yield?" → usually the bullet, because convexity isn't free — you pay for it with lower yield.

**Q9.** A portfolio has DV01 of \$9,000. A hedging bond has a DV01 of \$0.072 per \$100 of face value. The face amount of the hedging bond to short is closest to:
- A. \$1.25 million
- B. \$12.5 million
- C. \$6.48 million
- D. \$125 million

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how much face value of the hedge bond gives a matching \$9,000 DV01?

The hedge DV01 is quoted per \$100 face. So we need:
$$\text{Face} = \frac{\text{Portfolio DV01}}{\text{Hedge DV01 per \$100}} \times 100$$
$$= \frac{9{,}000}{0.072} \times 100 = 125{,}000 \times 100 = \$12{,}500{,}000 = \$12.5\text{M}$$

A cleaner way: \$0.072 of DV01 comes from each \$100 of face. To get \$9,000 of DV01 you need $$9{,}000 / 0.072 = 125{,}000$$ units of "\$100 face" = \$12.5M of face.

- A (\$1.25M) is off by a factor of 10 — a decimal slip.
- C (\$6.48M) multiplies rather than divides somewhere ($$9{,}000 \times 0.072 = 648$$ scaled).
- D (\$125M) forgets that the DV01 is per \$100, overshooting by 100×.

Analogy: if each \$100 brick adds \$0.072 of "weight," and you want \$9,000 of weight, you need 125,000 bricks — and 125,000 bricks of \$100 each is \$12.5M.

📌 Exam tip: When DV01 is quoted "per \$100 face," scale carefully. Face = (target DV01 / DV01-per-100) × 100.

**Q10.** DV01, duration, and convexity all rely on the same key assumption about how the yield curve moves. That assumption is:
- A. The curve steepens over time
- B. All rates along the curve move together by the same amount (a single-factor, parallel shift)
- C. Short rates are more volatile than long rates
- D. The curve is always flat

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what single assumption underlies DV01, duration, and convexity?

These are all "one-factor" measures: they assume the entire yield curve is driven by one factor, so when rates move, every point on the curve shifts by the same amount (a parallel shift). They answer "what if THE rate moves by Δy?" — and say nothing about the curve steepening, flattening, or twisting.

- A describes a non-parallel move, which these measures specifically cannot handle.
- C describes a curve-reshaping (volatility difference by maturity) — again outside the one-factor model.
- D is too strong; the curve need not be flat, it just needs to SHIFT in parallel.

Analogy: these tools assume the whole staircase rises or falls as one rigid block. They can't tell you what happens if the top steps rise while the bottom steps stay put (a twist).

📌 Exam tip: DV01/duration/convexity = one-factor, parallel-shift world. Non-parallel moves (steepeners, twists) require key-rate or partial '01 analysis — the topic of the next chapter.
