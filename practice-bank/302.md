**Q1.** Which life insurance product provides pure mortality protection for a fixed period only, with no investment/savings component, and is therefore the cheapest?
- A. Whole life
- B. Term life
- C. Variable life
- D. Endowment

**Answer: B**

The question asks which policy is "death-protection only" for a set time, with nothing saved up inside it.

Term life pays out only if the insured dies during a fixed term (say 10 years). If you survive the term, it pays nothing and there is no built-up cash value. Because it is pure protection with no savings component, it is the cheapest type.

Analogy: term life is like renting an umbrella for the monsoon season — you only get value if it rains during that period, and you don't keep anything afterward.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (whole life) covers the entire life and builds a cash surrender value (a savings component), so it is more expensive.
- C (variable life) is whole life where you choose how the cash value is invested — it has an investment component.
- D (endowment) pays at death OR at the end of the term even if you survive — so it always pays something, making it more than pure term cover.

📌 Exam tip: Term life = pure, temporary, cheapest, no cash value. Anything with "cash value" or that "pays even if you survive" is NOT term life.

**Q2.** Using a mortality table with $$q_{70} = 0.02$$ and $$q_{71} = 0.022$$, the probability that a person currently aged 70 dies during the SECOND year is closest to:
- A. 0.0220
- B. 0.0216
- C. 0.0200
- D. 0.0420

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what is the chance this 70-year-old dies in year 2 (i.e., between age 71 and 72)?

To die in the second year, two things must both happen:
1. Survive the first year (age 70 to 71). Probability of surviving = 1 − q70 = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
2. Then die during the second year (age 71). Probability = q71 = 0.022.

Because both must happen, we multiply:
$$P(\text{die in year 2}) = (1 - q_{70}) \times q_{71} = 0.98 \times 0.022 = 0.02156 \approx 0.0216$$

Analogy: to win a prize in round 2 of a game, you first have to survive round 1 AND then succeed in round 2. You multiply the two chances together.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (0.0220) just uses q71 alone, forgetting you must first survive year 1.
- C (0.0200) just uses q70, the wrong year entirely.
- D (0.0420) adds the two probabilities (0.020 + 0.022) instead of chaining them correctly with survival. You multiply, not add.

📌 Exam tip: To die in year n, multiply the survival probabilities of all earlier years by the death probability of year n. Survive-then-die = multiply.

**Q3.** Requiring a medical examination before issuing a life insurance policy primarily mitigates:
- A. Moral hazard
- B. Adverse selection
- C. Longevity risk
- D. Catastrophe risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks which problem a pre-contract medical exam fixes.

Adverse selection happens BEFORE the contract: sick or high-risk people are the most eager to buy insurance, so if everyone is charged the same price, the insurer attracts a worse-than-average pool. A medical exam lets the insurer screen applicants and price (or decline) high-risk people before issuing the policy — directly attacking adverse selection.

Moral hazard, by contrast, happens AFTER the contract (insured people behave more carelessly), and is fixed by deductibles, coinsurance, and limits — not by an upfront exam.

Analogy: the exam is like checking a player's fitness BEFORE letting them join your team and setting their fee accordingly. That's selecting who gets in (adverse selection). Watching whether they slack off AFTER joining is the moral-hazard problem.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (moral hazard) is post-contract behavior; exams don't change how you behave later.
- C (longevity risk) is about annuitants living too long; an exam doesn't address that.
- D (catastrophe risk) is a P&C concept (hurricanes, earthquakes), unrelated to individual life underwriting.

📌 Exam tip: Adverse selection = BEFORE the contract (who buys), fixed by underwriting/screening. Moral hazard = AFTER the contract (behavior), fixed by deductibles/coinsurance.

**Q4.** A P&C insurer reports: premiums $100M, losses $72M, expenses $28M, policyholder dividends $2M, and investment income $8M. What is its operating ratio?
- A. 94%
- B. 100%
- C. 102%
- D. 110%

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the operating ratio, which is built up step by step from simpler ratios (all as a percent of premiums).

Step 1 — Loss ratio = losses / premiums = 72 / 100 = 72%.
Step 2 — Expense ratio = expenses / premiums = 28 / 100 = 28%.
Step 3 — Combined ratio = loss ratio + expense ratio = 72% + 28% = 100%.
Step 4 — Combined ratio after dividends = combined ratio + dividends = 100% + 2% = 102%.
Step 5 — Operating ratio = combined ratio after dividends − investment income ratio = 102% − 8% = 94%.

So the operating ratio is 94%. Answer A.

Analogy: think of it as a running tally. Costs pile ON (losses, expenses, dividends raise the ratio), and the money the insurer earns by investing premiums comes OFF (lowers the ratio).

Why the others are wrong:
- B (100%) stops at the combined ratio and forgets dividends and investment income.
- C (102%) stops at "combined after dividends" and forgets to subtract investment income.
- D (110%) is just wrong arithmetic; nothing here totals 110%.

📌 Exam tip: Remember the signs: dividends are ADDED, investment income is SUBTRACTED. A combined ratio over 100% does NOT mean the insurer is losing money — check the operating ratio after investment income.

**Q5.** An insurer has a combined ratio of 103% but an operating ratio of 95%. The best interpretation is:
- A. The insurer is unprofitable because the combined ratio exceeds 100%
- B. Underwriting alone lost money, but investment income on the float makes the business profitable overall
- C. The insurer made an arithmetic error; the operating ratio cannot be below the combined ratio
- D. The insurer paid no claims this year

**Answer: B**

The question asks what it means when underwriting loses money but the operating ratio is still good.

A combined ratio of 103% means that, on insurance alone (claims + expenses + dividends), the insurer paid out more than the premiums it collected — underwriting lost money. But insurers also invest the premiums they hold before paying claims (called the "float"), earning investment income. Here investment income is worth about 103% − 95% = 8% of premiums, which more than offsets the 3% underwriting loss. So overall (operating ratio 95% < 100%), the insurer is profitable.

Analogy: a shop might sell goods at a tiny loss but still profit because it earns interest on the customers' deposits it holds in the meantime.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the classic trap — a combined ratio over 100% does NOT automatically mean unprofitable; you must factor in investment income.
- C is false — the operating ratio is below the combined ratio precisely because you subtract investment income; that's by design.
- D is unsupported and false — losses (claims) of 103% combined clearly mean claims were paid.

📌 Exam tip: Combined ratio > 100% = underwriting loss, but not necessarily an overall loss. The float and its investment income are what make P&C insurance work.

**Q6.** A catastrophe (CAT) bond is most attractive to investors mainly because:
- A. It guarantees both principal and a high coupon regardless of events
- B. Catastrophe losses have low correlation with financial markets, providing diversification
- C. It is insured by the FDIC
- D. It pays no coupon but offers tax-free principal

**Answer: B**

The question asks why investors like CAT bonds.

A CAT bond lets an insurer transfer extreme disaster risk (like a hurricane) to bond investors. The bond pays a high coupon, but if a defined catastrophe loss passes a trigger, the investors lose some interest and/or principal (that forgiven money helps the insurer pay claims). Investors like them because whether a hurricane hits has almost nothing to do with whether the stock or bond market goes up or down — so CAT bonds add valuable diversification to a portfolio.

Analogy: a stock portfolio and a bet on hurricanes move for totally different reasons, so combining them smooths your overall ride.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — principal/interest is precisely at risk if the catastrophe trigger is breached; nothing is guaranteed.
- C is false — CAT bonds are capital-market securities, not FDIC-insured deposits.
- D is false — CAT bonds pay a high coupon (that's the whole appeal) and are not tax-free instruments by definition.

📌 Exam tip: CAT bond = the insurer's alternative to reinsurance; investors get a high coupon and diversification because catastrophe risk is uncorrelated with markets, but they can lose principal if disaster strikes.

**Q7.** In the United States, insurance companies are primarily regulated by:
- A. The Federal Reserve
- B. The states (coordinated by the NAIC)
- C. The FDIC
- D. The Basel Committee

**Answer: B**

The question asks who regulates US insurers.

Unlike banks (which face federal oversight), US insurance companies are regulated at the STATE level. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) helps coordinate standards across states, but the actual regulators are the states themselves.

Analogy: think of insurance regulation like driver's licenses in the US — each state runs its own system, with a national body helping keep things roughly consistent.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (Federal Reserve) supervises banks and the banking system, not insurers as the primary regulator.
- C (FDIC) insures bank deposits; it has nothing to do with regulating insurance companies.
- D (Basel Committee) sets international BANK capital standards. The insurance analog in Europe is Solvency II — but that's Europe, and it's not a US regulator anyway.

📌 Exam tip: US insurers = STATE-regulated (NAIC coordinates). Europe = Solvency II (MCR and the higher SCR). Don't confuse the insurance guaranty system with bank deposit insurance.

**Q8.** How does the US insurance guaranty system differ from FDIC bank deposit insurance?
- A. The guaranty system is a permanent pre-funded federal fund; the FDIC charges levies only after a failure
- B. The guaranty system levies surviving insurers after an insolvency; the FDIC runs a permanent, pre-funded fund from ongoing premiums
- C. Both are identical pre-funded federal programs
- D. Neither protects customers; both only protect shareholders

**Answer: B**

The question asks how insurers' safety net differs from banks'.

For banks, the FDIC keeps a permanent fund, built up in advance from ongoing premiums paid by banks. If a bank fails, the money is already there.

For insurers, the state guaranty associations generally do NOT pre-fund. Instead, when an insurer fails, they levy the surviving insurers in that state to raise the money after the fact (post-insolvency assessments). This works because insurance failures are slower-moving — there are no sudden "runs" like with banks — so an after-the-fact collection is feasible.

Analogy: the FDIC is like a neighborhood that keeps an emergency fund saved up all year. The guaranty system is like neighbors passing the hat around only AFTER a house catches fire.

Why the others are wrong:
- A reverses the two systems.
- C is false — they are structured very differently (pre-funded vs. after-the-fact).
- D is false — both protect customers (depositors / policyholders), up to limits.

📌 Exam tip: FDIC = permanent, pre-funded. Insurance guaranty = after-the-fact assessments on surviving insurers. The reason it works for insurers is the absence of fast "runs."

**Q9.** In a defined contribution (DC) pension plan, the investment risk and the longevity risk are borne by:
- A. The employer
- B. The employee
- C. The state guaranty association
- D. Shared equally by formula

**Answer: B**

The question asks who carries the risk in a DC plan.

In a DC plan, the employer only promises to put in contributions (e.g., a percentage of salary). Once those go into the employee's account, the employer's obligation is done. The final retirement pot equals the contributions plus whatever the investments earned — so if markets do poorly (investment risk) the employee has less, and if the employee lives a very long time (longevity risk) the savings might run out. Both risks sit with the EMPLOYEE.

Contrast: in a defined benefit (DB) plan, the employer promises a specific income for life, so the EMPLOYER bears investment and longevity risk and must fund any shortfall.

Analogy: DC is like your employer giving you seeds to plant in your own garden — how much food you end up with, and whether it lasts, is on you. DB is like the employer promising you a fixed basket of food every month for life no matter what.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (employer) bears these risks in a DB plan, not DC.
- C (guaranty association) relates to insurance failures, not pension investment/longevity risk.
- D is false — the risk is squarely on the employee, not shared by formula.

📌 Exam tip: DC = employee bears investment AND longevity risk (and it's portable). DB = employer bears them. The world is shifting toward DC.

**Q10.** What is the key difference between a mutual insurance company and a stock insurance company?
- A. A mutual company is owned by its policyholders; a stock company is owned by shareholders
- B. A mutual company is owned by shareholders; a stock company is owned by policyholders
- C. Only stock companies are allowed to sell life insurance
- D. Mutual companies cannot earn investment income

**Answer: A**

The question asks who owns each type of insurer.

A mutual insurance company is owned by its policyholders. There are no outside shareholders, so profits flow back to policyholders (as dividends or lower premiums). There is no owner-vs-policyholder conflict, but it is harder to raise capital.

A stock insurance company is owned by shareholders. Profits go to shareholders, and it can raise capital more easily by issuing equity — but there can be a conflict between shareholders and policyholders. Many mutuals have "demutualized" to become stock companies and tap equity markets.

Analogy: a mutual is like a member-owned cooperative store (the customers own it). A stock company is like a regular corporation with outside investors.

Why the others are wrong:
- B reverses the definitions.
- C is false — both mutual and stock insurers sell life insurance.
- D is false — mutuals invest premiums and earn investment income just like any insurer.

📌 Exam tip: Mutual = policyholder-owned (no shareholder conflict, harder to raise capital). Stock = shareholder-owned (easier capital, possible conflict). "Demutualization" = mutual converting to stock form.
