**Q1.** Which of the following is the best explanation for why a sovereign might default on its local-currency (domestic) debt?
- A. It is impossible — local-currency debt is always risk-free
- B. Printing money to repay may be judged more costly (inflation, currency collapse) than restructuring
- C. Local-currency debt always has the shortest maturity
- D. Rating agencies require periodic local-currency defaults

**Answer: B**

The question asks why a government that can print its own money would still default on debt in that currency.

A sovereign can in principle print local currency to repay, but doing so can trigger severe inflation and a currency collapse — costs that may exceed those of restructuring the debt. Some sovereigns also face institutional constraints (an independent central bank, a currency board, or euro membership — Greece could not print euros). So default can be a choice. B is correct.

- A is a false statement GARP likes to plant: local-currency default has happened (e.g., Russia 1998).
- C is wrong: maturity structure does not force a local-currency default.
- D is nonsense — agencies do not require defaults.

Analogy: you could pay a debt by burning down your own house for the insurance money, but if that's more damaging than negotiating with the lender, you negotiate instead.

📌 Exam tip: "A sovereign that prints its own currency cannot default" is FALSE. Default is partly a choice when monetization's inflation cost beats restructuring — or when printing is institutionally barred.

**Q2.** A sovereign's 5-year CDS trades at 300 basis points. Assuming a loss given default (LGD) of 75%, the implied (risk-neutral) annual default probability is closest to:
- A. 2.25%
- B. 4.00%
- C. 3.00%
- D. 0.75%

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to back out a default probability from a CDS spread.

For small spreads the approximation is $s \approx PD \times LGD$, so:

$$PD \approx \frac{s}{LGD}$$

Convert 300 bp to a decimal: $300 \text{ bp} = 0.03$. Then:

$$PD \approx \frac{0.03}{0.75} = 0.04 = 4\%$$

So B is correct.

- A (2.25%) multiplies instead of divides ($0.03 \times 0.75$) — the classic trap.
- C (3.00%) just reads the spread as the PD, ignoring LGD.
- D (0.75%) confuses the LGD value with the answer.

Analogy: the CDS premium covers your expected loss = how often × how much. To recover "how often" (PD), divide the premium by "how much" (LGD).

📌 Exam tip: PD ≈ spread ÷ LGD (divide, never multiply). And this is a risk-neutral PD — it sits above the real-world default frequency because it includes risk premia.

**Q3.** Compared with sovereign credit ratings, sovereign CDS spreads are generally:
- A. Slower to react and less noisy
- B. Faster to react but noisier
- C. Identical in information content
- D. Updated less frequently than ratings

**Answer: B**

The question contrasts market-based CDS spreads with agency ratings.

CDS spreads are continuous, forward-looking market prices, so they react quickly and tend to lead rating changes. But they are noisier — contaminated by liquidity premia, counterparty effects, and shifting risk appetite. B is correct.

- A is reversed: ratings are the slow, smooth measure; CDS are fast and noisy.
- C is wrong: they carry different information (market pricing vs ordinal opinion).
- D is wrong: CDS update continuously, far more often than episodic ratings.

Analogy: ratings are like a doctor's annual checkup (thorough but infrequent); CDS spreads are like a fitness tracker (constant readings, but jumpy and sometimes misleading).

📌 Exam tip: Market measures lead ratings but carry liquidity/risk-premium noise. Best practice: ratings for the level, market spreads for direction and early warning.

**Q4.** Local-currency sovereign ratings are usually:
- A. Lower than foreign-currency ratings
- B. Equal to or higher than foreign-currency ratings
- C. Not assigned by major agencies
- D. Set equal to the rating of the country's largest bank

**Answer: B**

The question asks how local- and foreign-currency sovereign ratings compare.

A sovereign has more capacity to service local-currency debt (it can print the currency and tax domestic holders), so local-currency ratings are typically equal to or one to two notches higher than foreign-currency ratings. B is correct.

- A is reversed: foreign-currency ratings are usually the same or lower, not higher.
- C is wrong: agencies assign both kinds of ratings separately.
- D is nonsense — the sovereign rating is not pegged to a bank's rating (if anything, the sovereign ceiling drags banks down).

Analogy: it's easier to pay a debt in a currency you control (you have the printing press) than in someone else's currency you must earn or borrow — so your "score" for the home-currency debt is at least as good.

📌 Exam tip: Local-currency ratings ≥ foreign-currency ratings (printing press + domestic taxation). Most sovereign defaults historically are on foreign-currency debt.

**Q5.** Which of the following is a recognized source of country risk?
- A. Only the level of stock-market prices
- B. Economic structure, political risk, the legal system, and economic maturity
- C. Solely the level of a country's central-bank interest rate
- D. The number of listed companies

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to identify the core determinants of country risk.

Country risk is driven by economic structure (growth stage, commodity/sector concentration), political risk (continuity vs rupture, corruption, expropriation, war), the legal system (property rights and contract enforcement), and economic maturity (early-stage economies are more volatile). B is correct.

- A is far too narrow — stock prices are not a core determinant by themselves.
- C is too narrow — interest rates alone don't define country risk.
- D is irrelevant to the standard framework.

Analogy: judging a country's safety for investment is like inspecting a house: the foundation (economy), the neighborhood politics, the enforceable property deed (legal system), and how settled the area is (maturity) all matter — not just the paint color.

📌 Exam tip: Remember the four buckets: economic structure, political risk, legal system, economic maturity. Country risk is broader than sovereign default risk — it covers any investment in the country.

**Q6.** Which statement about the history of sovereign defaults is most accurate?
- A. Sovereign defaults are randomly scattered with no pattern
- B. Defaults have clustered in waves tied to global economic cycles
- C. Only emerging markets have ever defaulted
- D. Local-currency defaults have never occurred

**Answer: B**

The question tests the historical pattern of sovereign defaults.

Sovereign defaults have come in waves linked to global cycles: 1820s–30s Latin America, the 1930s Depression, the 1980s Latin American debt crisis, Russia 1998, Argentina 2001, Greece 2012, and recent cases (Sri Lanka, Zambia, Ghana). B is correct.

- A is wrong: the clustering is precisely the pattern.
- C is wrong: even developed sovereigns (e.g., Greece) have defaulted/restructured.
- D is wrong: local-currency defaults have occurred (e.g., Russia 1998).

Analogy: defaults are like power outages during big storms — they cluster when the broader system is stressed, not at random.

📌 Exam tip: Defaults come in cycle-linked waves. Most are foreign-currency, but local-currency defaults do happen — don't fall for "never."

**Q7.** A sovereign default most directly tends to cause which of the following domestic consequences?
- A. Permanent, total exclusion from all capital markets forever
- B. Output losses, banking-system stress, and a period of costlier or lost market access
- C. An immediate, guaranteed economic boom
- D. No domestic effect whatsoever

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the consequences of sovereign default.

Default typically brings output losses and recessions, banking crises (domestic banks hold government bonds and take losses), and a period of lost or more expensive market access, plus trade/capital-flow disruption and political fallout. B is correct.

- A overstates it: reputational exclusion is real but often shorter-lived than feared.
- C is the opposite of reality.
- D is wrong — domestic economic damage is usually the largest cost.

Analogy: defaulting is like declaring personal bankruptcy — it doesn't lock you out of all credit forever, but it hurts your finances now and makes future borrowing pricier for a while.

📌 Exam tip: Biggest cost is usually the domestic damage (recession + banking stress), not permanent market exclusion. The banking feedback loop is a key channel.

**Q8.** Why should a CDS-implied default probability not be treated as a real-world (historical) probability of default?
- A. CDS spreads are always too low
- B. CDS spreads embed risk premia and liquidity effects, so risk-neutral PDs exceed historical default frequencies
- C. CDS markets do not exist for sovereigns
- D. Real-world PDs are always higher than risk-neutral PDs

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the gap between risk-neutral and real-world default probabilities.

CDS spreads price not just expected loss but also investors' risk aversion and liquidity/counterparty effects. As a result, the risk-neutral PD backed out from spreads systematically exceeds the actual historical default frequency — most visibly for investment-grade sovereigns. B is correct.

- A is wrong: spreads tend to be too high relative to pure expected loss, not too low.
- C is wrong: sovereign CDS markets exist.
- D is reversed: risk-neutral PDs are higher than real-world PDs.

Analogy: an insurance premium is more than your true expected loss — it also pays the insurer to bear risk and stay in business. The CDS spread is the same: it overstates the "true" default odds.

📌 Exam tip: Risk-neutral PD (from CDS) > real-world PD, because spreads include risk premia and liquidity. Don't equate the two on the exam.

**Q9.** A common criticism of sovereign credit ratings is that they:
- A. Change too frequently, creating excessive noise
- B. Lag market signals and exhibit clustered, pro-cyclical multi-notch downgrades in crises
- C. Are based purely on real-time CDS spreads
- D. Never affect the cost of borrowing for the sovereign

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a recognized criticism of sovereign ratings.

Ratings tend to lag markets (downgrades follow spread widening) and show herd/pro-cyclical behavior — clustered, multi-notch downgrades during crises (Asia 1997, eurozone 2010–12). Upgrades are slow and incremental, while downgrades cluster and cascade through sovereign ceilings. B is correct.

- A is the opposite — ratings are too smooth/stable, not too frequent.
- C is wrong: ratings are agency credit opinions, not derived purely from CDS.
- D is wrong: ratings strongly affect borrowing costs and trigger collateral/mandate effects.

Analogy: ratings are like a slow weather forecaster who announces "storm" only after the rain has started, and then panics with several warnings at once.

📌 Exam tip: Sovereign-rating criticisms: lag markets, pro-cyclical clustered downgrades, sovereign-ceiling cascades, and the same issuer-pays conflicts as corporates.

**Q10.** A sovereign's USD-denominated bond yields 6.2% while a matched-maturity U.S. Treasury yields 4.2%. The sovereign default spread is:
- A. 10.4%
- B. 2.0%
- C. 1.48%
- D. 4.2%

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to compute a sovereign default spread.

The default spread is the sovereign's bond yield minus the matched-maturity risk-free (Treasury) yield:

$$\text{spread} = 6.2\% - 4.2\% = 2.0\%$$

So B is correct.

- A (10.4%) adds the two yields instead of subtracting.
- C (1.48%) divides the yields (6.2/4.2 − 1) rather than subtracting.
- D (4.2%) is just the Treasury yield, not the spread.

Analogy: the spread is the "extra rent" investors charge the riskier borrower above the safe benchmark — simply the difference between the two yields.

📌 Exam tip: Sovereign default spread = sovereign (USD/EUR) bond yield − matched-maturity risk-free yield. It's a subtraction, and it must be in the same currency to isolate default (not currency) risk.
