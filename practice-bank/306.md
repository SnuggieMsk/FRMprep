**Q1.** When a trade is accepted for central clearing through novation, what happens to the original bilateral contract?

- A. It stays in place, but a CCP guarantees it
- B. It is replaced by two new contracts, with the CCP as buyer to the seller and seller to the buyer
- C. It is canceled and the parties must re-trade directly
- D. It becomes a futures contract on an exchange

**Answer: B**

The question asks what "novation" does to a trade when it goes into central clearing.

Novation means the single original contract between two parties is torn up and replaced by two new contracts. The central counterparty (CCP) steps into the middle: it becomes the buyer to the original seller and the seller to the original buyer. Think of it like a trusted middleman at a school swap meet: instead of two kids trading directly and worrying whether the other will keep their promise, the teacher (the CCP) buys from one and sells to the other, so each kid only has to trust the teacher.

Why B is correct: novation literally creates two contracts with the CCP in the middle, so members only worry about the CCP's creditworthiness, not each other's.

Why A is wrong: the original contract does not survive. Novation replaces it; a guarantee on top of the old contract would be a different mechanism.
Why C is wrong: nothing is re-traded; the economic terms are kept, only the counterparty changes.
Why D is wrong: clearing is separate from exchange trading. An OTC trade can be cleared without becoming an exchange-listed futures contract.

📌 Exam tip: "Novation = one contract becomes two, CCP in the middle." This is the single most testable definition in the chapter.

**Q2.** Three dealers have these obligations: A owes B $50M, B owes C $70M, and C owes A $60M. If all positions move to a CCP, what is B's net position?

- A. B receives $20M from the CCP
- B. B pays $20M to the CCP
- C. B pays $70M to the CCP
- D. B receives $50M from the CCP

**Answer: B**

The question asks for B's single net position against the CCP after multilateral netting.

With a CCP, each dealer's many obligations collapse into one net amount. List what B pays and what B receives:
- B receives $50M (because A owes B $50M).
- B pays $70M (because B owes C $70M).

Net for B = receives 50 − pays 70 = −$20M. A negative number means B is a net payer, so B pays $20M to the CCP.

Why B is correct: $50M in, $70M out, so B owes the difference of $20M.

Why A is wrong: it flips the sign. B pays more than it receives, so it cannot be a net receiver.
Why C is wrong: $70M is only the gross amount B owes C; netting cancels the $50M B is owed by A.
Why D is wrong: $50M is only the amount B is owed; it ignores the larger $70M B must pay.

📌 Exam tip: For netting, always write down "receives" and "pays" for the named party only, then subtract. Ignore amounts between the other two parties.

**Q3.** After a clearing member defaults, its initial margin and its default fund contribution are both used up. According to the CCP default waterfall, what is consumed NEXT?

- A. Surviving members' initial margin
- B. The CCP's own capital ("skin in the game")
- C. Surviving members' default fund contributions
- D. Variation margin gains haircutting

**Answer: B**

The question tests the exact order of the CCP loss waterfall.

The waterfall order is:
1. Defaulter's initial margin
2. Defaulter's default fund contribution
3. CCP's own capital ("skin in the game")
4. Surviving members' default fund contributions
5. Further assessments / recovery tools (extra cash calls, VM gains haircutting, tear-ups)

After steps 1 and 2 (the defaulter's own money) are gone, step 3 is the CCP's own equity tranche.

Why B is correct: the CCP must put its own capital at risk before tapping the surviving members.

Why A is wrong: surviving members' initial margin is NEVER used to cover another member's losses — it is their own protection and is returned to them.
Why C is wrong: surviving members' default fund contributions come AFTER the CCP's skin in the game, not before.
Why D is wrong: VM gains haircutting is a last-resort recovery tool, far down the waterfall.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize "defaulter pays first (twice), then the CCP, then everyone else." The CCP's own capital sits between the defaulter's money and the survivors' money.

**Q4.** Which statement about loss mutualization at a CCP is correct?

- A. Non-defaulting members' initial margin can be used to absorb a defaulter's losses
- B. Only the defaulter's resources can ever be used, so survivors never bear losses
- C. Survivors' default fund contributions, and possibly further assessments, can absorb a defaulter's losses
- D. The CCP's skin in the game is always the very last resource used

**Answer: C**

The question asks how losses are shared ("mutualized") among surviving members.

Loss mutualization means surviving members collectively share extreme losses that exceed the defaulter's own resources and the CCP's capital. This sharing happens through the survivors' default fund contributions and, if needed, additional assessments (cash calls). It does NOT touch their initial margin.

Why C is correct: the default fund plus assessment rights are exactly the mutualized layers.

Why A is wrong: a non-defaulting member's initial margin is protected and returned; it can never be used to cover someone else's default. This is a classic trap.
Why B is wrong: survivors CAN bear losses in extreme cases — that is the whole point of mutualization.
Why D is wrong: skin in the game is used relatively early (step 3), before survivors' default fund; the truly last resources are assessments and recovery tools.

📌 Exam tip: Initial margin = "mine alone." Default fund = "shared." Only the default fund (plus assessments) is mutualized.

**Q5.** Procyclicality of CCP initial margin refers to the risk that:

- A. Margin requirements fall in volatile markets, leaving the CCP underprotected
- B. Margin requirements rise in volatile markets, draining members' liquidity exactly when funding is scarce
- C. Margin is collected only once per business cycle
- D. The default fund automatically shrinks as trading volume grows

**Answer: B**

The question asks what "procyclical" margins means as a disadvantage of central clearing.

Initial margin models size the buffer based on volatility. When markets get stressed, volatility spikes, so the models demand MORE margin. But stressed markets are exactly when cash is hardest to find. So the CCP pulls liquidity from members at the worst possible moment, amplifying the stress. "Procyclical" means moving in the same direction as the market cycle — making bad times worse.

Why B is correct: higher volatility leads to higher margin calls, draining liquidity in a crisis.

Why A is wrong: it reverses the effect. Margins rise, not fall, in volatile markets.
Why C is wrong: margin (VM) is collected daily, not once per cycle; this misreads the word.
Why D is wrong: the default fund relating to volume has nothing to do with procyclicality.

📌 Exam tip: "Procyclical" = amplifies the cycle. For margin, that means more cash demanded right when cash is scarce.

**Q6.** Compared with traditional bilateral (ISDA/CSA) collateral arrangements, CCP margining is best described as:

- A. One-way, with high thresholds, and collected only at maturity
- B. Two-way, zero-threshold, marked daily, and includes initial margin
- C. Optional and negotiated trade by trade
- D. Limited to variation margin only, with no initial margin

**Answer: B**

The question compares CCP margin practices with older bilateral practices.

A CCP requires both sides to post margin (two-way), with no threshold below which collateral is skipped (zero-threshold), marks positions to market every day, and collects initial margin on top of variation margin. Historically this was stricter than typical bilateral CSA collateral, which often allowed thresholds and was one-sided.

Why B is correct: it lists exactly the four CCP features — two-way, zero-threshold, daily, plus IM.

Why A is wrong: that describes a weak bilateral arrangement, not a CCP. CCPs settle daily, not only at maturity.
Why C is wrong: CCP margining is mandatory and standardized, not negotiated per trade.
Why D is wrong: CCPs require BOTH variation margin and initial margin; dropping IM is incorrect.

📌 Exam tip: CCP margin = "two-way, zero-threshold, daily, IM + VM." Post-crisis rules now force IM/VM on bilateral trades too, narrowing the old gap.

**Q7.** When a CCP manages a defaulting member, transferring that member's CLIENT positions and their collateral to a surviving clearing member is called:

- A. Novation
- B. Porting
- C. Multilateral netting
- D. Juniorization

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the name of moving a defaulted member's clients to a healthy member.

Porting means carrying the clients (and their segregated collateral) across to a willing surviving clearing member, so the clients are not dragged down by their broker's failure. Porting works best when client margin is segregated and a backup member is ready to take them. Think of it like a school bus breaking down: the kids (clients) and their backpacks (collateral) are moved onto another working bus (surviving member).

Why B is correct: porting is precisely the transfer of client positions and collateral on a member default.

Why A is wrong: novation is the original CCP-in-the-middle mechanism at trade acceptance, not the default-management transfer.
Why C is wrong: multilateral netting is the compression of many exposures into one; it is not about transferring clients.
Why D is wrong: juniorization is making a poor-bidding member's default-fund contribution rank lower (an auction incentive), not a client transfer.

📌 Exam tip: On default management, "porting" = move the clients to safety. Segregated margin makes porting possible.

**Q8.** Which of the following is a genuine DISADVANTAGE or risk of central clearing of OTC derivatives?

- A. It eliminates multilateral netting benefits
- B. It removes all transparency from the market
- C. The CCP becomes a systemically critical single point of failure ("too big to fail")
- D. It increases counterparty credit risk between members

**Answer: C**

The question asks for a true downside of central clearing.

By concentrating huge volumes of contracts in one institution, the CCP itself becomes critically important. If it ever failed, the damage would ripple across the whole system. So central clearing trades many small bilateral risks for one giant concentrated risk — the CCP is "too big to fail" by design.

Why C is correct: concentration of risk in a single critical institution is a recognized disadvantage.

Why A is wrong: central clearing CREATES multilateral netting; it does not eliminate it (though splitting products across CCPs can reduce some netting benefits).
Why B is wrong: clearing INCREASES transparency — the CCP sees positions across all members.
Why D is wrong: clearing REDUCES counterparty credit risk between members through novation and margining.

📌 Exam tip: The headline downside of a CCP is concentration — one super-important node whose failure would be catastrophic. Most other CCP features are advantages.

**Q9.** In the agency (US FCM) client clearing model, the relationship is best described as:

- A. The client's contract is with the CCP, and the clearing member acts as agent guaranteeing the client's performance
- B. The client faces the clearing member, which holds a mirror trade with the CCP as principal
- C. The client faces the CCP with no member involvement at all
- D. The CCP faces the client directly and bears all client default losses itself

**Answer: A**

The question contrasts the two client clearing models, specifically the agency (US) model.

In the agency / FCM model, the client's contract is directly with the CCP, but the clearing member acts as the client's agent and guarantees the client's performance to the CCP. In the principal-to-principal (European) model, the client faces the member, and the member faces the CCP with a back-to-back mirror trade — being principal to both.

Why A is correct: that is exactly the agency model — client-to-CCP contract, member as guaranteeing agent.

Why B is wrong: that describes the principal-to-principal (European) model, not the agency model.
Why C is wrong: the member is always involved (as agent and guarantor) in the agency model.
Why D is wrong: the member guarantees the client, so the CCP does not simply absorb all client default losses alone.

📌 Exam tip: Agency = US/FCM, client-to-CCP, member guarantees. Principal-to-principal = European, member sits in the middle with two mirror trades.

**Q10.** A CCP runs a "matched book." What does this mean, and what risk does the CCP primarily face as a result?

- A. Every long is offset by a short, so the CCP has no market risk but faces counterparty risk if a member defaults
- B. The CCP takes large directional bets, so it faces mainly market risk
- C. The CCP holds only cash, so it faces no risk of any kind
- D. The CCP matches buyers to sellers but never becomes a counterparty, so it faces only operational risk

**Answer: A**

The question asks what a matched book is and what risk it leaves behind.

Because the CCP is buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, every long position it holds is offset by an equal short position. Net, its market exposure is zero — if prices move, gains on one side exactly cancel losses on the other. So the CCP has essentially no market risk. Its real worry is counterparty risk: if a member defaults, that side of the book is no longer matched, and the CCP must cover the gap.

Why A is correct: matched book = offsetting longs and shorts = no market risk, but counterparty risk on default.

Why B is wrong: a matched book is the opposite of a directional bet; the CCP does not take market positions.
Why C is wrong: the CCP holds open offsetting contracts, not just cash, and clearly faces counterparty risk.
Why D is wrong: the CCP DOES become a counterparty through novation; saying it never does is incorrect.

📌 Exam tip: Matched book → CCP has no market risk, only counterparty (default) risk. A member default breaks the match and is what the margin stack and waterfall exist to handle.
