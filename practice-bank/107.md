**Q1.** Which performance measure divides excess return by the portfolio's TOTAL risk (standard deviation)?
- A. Treynor ratio
- B. Jensen's alpha
- C. Sharpe ratio
- D. Information ratio

**Answer: C**

The question is simply asking: which ratio uses total risk (the whole standard deviation, $$\sigma$$) on the bottom?

The Sharpe ratio formula is:

$$S_p = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\sigma_p}$$

The top is the extra return above the risk-free rate, and the bottom is $$\sigma_p$$, which measures ALL of the portfolio's ups and downs (both the market-driven part and the company-specific part). So Sharpe uses total risk. That is exactly what the question describes, so C is correct.

Think of it like grading a runner's speed against the total bumpiness of the road they ran on, not just the part of the bumpiness caused by traffic.

- A is wrong: the Treynor ratio divides by beta ($$\beta$$), which is only the systematic (market-driven) part of risk, not total risk.
- B is wrong: Jensen's alpha is not a "divide by risk" ratio at all; it is a single percentage showing return above the CAPM-required return.
- D is wrong: the information ratio divides active return by tracking error (risk versus a benchmark), not by total standard deviation.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the denominators: Sharpe = total risk ($$\sigma$$), Treynor = systematic risk ($$\beta$$). This is the single most-tested trap in Book 1.

**Q2.** A portfolio returns 10%, the risk-free rate is 2%, and the portfolio's standard deviation is 16%. What is its Sharpe ratio?
- A. 0.50
- B. 0.625
- C. 0.80
- D. 1.25

**Answer: A**

The question asks us to plug numbers into the Sharpe ratio formula and compute.

Formula:

$$S_p = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\sigma_p}$$

Plug in the numbers:

$$S_p = \frac{10\% - 2\%}{16\%}$$

Step 1 — do the top first: $$10\% - 2\% = 8\%$$.

Step 2 — divide by the bottom: $$\frac{8}{16} = 0.50$$.

So the Sharpe ratio is 0.50, which is option A.

It is like asking "how much reward did I get for each unit of bumpiness?" — 8 units of extra return spread over 16 units of risk gives 0.5 reward per unit.

- B (0.625) is wrong: this is $$10/16$$ — the trap of forgetting to subtract the risk-free rate from the top.
- C (0.80) is wrong: this is $$8/10$$ — dividing by the return instead of by the standard deviation.
- D (1.25) is wrong: this is $$10/8$$ — flipping the numbers upside down.

📌 Exam tip: Always subtract the risk-free rate first to get "excess return," THEN divide by risk. Forgetting to subtract $$R_f$$ is the most common slip.

**Q3.** An investor holds a single fund as their entire wealth. Which measure is most appropriate for judging that fund?
- A. Treynor ratio
- B. Jensen's alpha
- C. Sharpe ratio
- D. Information ratio

**Answer: C**

The question is asking: when a fund is someone's whole portfolio (not one piece among many), which yardstick fits?

When a fund is your ENTIRE wealth, you are exposed to all of its risk — both the market part AND the company-specific (idiosyncratic) part, because you have nothing else to cancel that company-specific risk out. So you must measure against total risk, which is the standard deviation. The Sharpe ratio uses total risk, so C is correct.

Analogy: if you own only one stock, every wobble in that stock hits your wealth. If you owned 100 stocks, the individual wobbles would mostly cancel out.

- A is wrong: Treynor uses only beta (systematic risk). That is right when the fund is one slice of a big diversified portfolio, because the company-specific risk has already been diversified away — but not when it is your only holding.
- B is wrong: Jensen's alpha also focuses on systematic risk (beta) and suits diversified sub-portfolios, not a stand-alone whole.
- D is wrong: the information ratio is for judging an active manager against a benchmark mandate, not for a stand-alone total portfolio.

📌 Exam tip: "Entire portfolio / only holding / undiversified" = Sharpe. "One of many funds / a sub-portfolio" = Treynor or alpha.

**Q4.** A portfolio returns 11%, has a beta of 1.2, and the risk-free rate is 3%. What is its Treynor ratio?
- A. 6.67%
- B. 9.17%
- C. 8.00%
- D. 13.20%

**Answer: A**

The question asks us to compute the Treynor ratio, which is excess return per unit of systematic risk (beta).

Formula:

$$T_p = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\beta_p}$$

Plug in the numbers:

$$T_p = \frac{11\% - 3\%}{1.2}$$

Step 1 — top: $$11\% - 3\% = 8\%$$.

Step 2 — divide by beta: $$\frac{8\%}{1.2} = 6.67\%$$.

So the Treynor ratio is 6.67%, option A.

- B (9.17%) is wrong: this is $$11/1.2$$ — forgetting to subtract the risk-free rate.
- C (8.00%) is wrong: this is just the excess return ($$11 - 3$$) without dividing by beta at all.
- D (13.20%) is wrong: this is $$11 \times 1.2$$ — multiplying by beta instead of dividing.

📌 Exam tip: Treynor's denominator is beta. Keep the units straight: a Treynor result is a percentage return per unit of beta.

**Q5.** A portfolio returns 12%, has a beta of 0.9, the market returns 10%, and the risk-free rate is 2%. What is Jensen's alpha?
- A. +2.0%
- B. +2.8%
- C. +3.0%
- D. +10.0%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for Jensen's alpha — how much the portfolio beat (or missed) the return that CAPM says it "should" have earned for its level of beta.

Formula:

$$\alpha_p = E[R_p] - \left[R_f + \beta_p\left(E[R_m] - R_f\right)\right]$$

Step 1 — find the market risk premium (market return minus risk-free): $$10\% - 2\% = 8\%$$.

Step 2 — multiply by beta: $$0.9 \times 8\% = 7.2\%$$.

Step 3 — add the risk-free rate to get the CAPM-required return: $$2\% + 7.2\% = 9.2\%$$.

Step 4 — subtract that from the actual return: $$12\% - 9.2\% = +2.8\%$$.

So alpha is +2.8%, option B. A positive alpha means the manager earned more than CAPM required — a sign of skill.

- A (+2.0%) is wrong: comes from forgetting to multiply by beta (using $$12 - (2+8)$$).
- C (+3.0%) is wrong: comes from using $$12 - 0.9 \times 10 = 3$$, mishandling the formula by multiplying beta by the full market return instead of the market premium.
- D (+10.0%) is wrong: this just compares the portfolio return to the risk-free rate, ignoring beta and the market entirely.

📌 Exam tip: The bracket is the "required return." Always build it as $$R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f)$$, using the market PREMIUM ($$R_m - R_f$$), not the raw market return.

**Q6.** A manager returns 12%, the benchmark returns 9%, and the tracking error is 6%. What is the information ratio?
- A. 0.33
- B. 0.50
- C. 1.50
- D. 2.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the information ratio, which measures average active return (the manager's return above the benchmark) per unit of tracking error.

Formula:

$$IR = \frac{E[R_p] - E[R_b]}{TE}$$

Step 1 — find active return (manager minus benchmark): $$12\% - 9\% = 3\%$$.

Step 2 — divide by tracking error: $$\frac{3\%}{6\%} = 0.50$$.

So the information ratio is 0.50, option B. An IR sustained around 0.5 is considered strong active management.

Think of it as: "for every unit of extra wobbliness the manager created by straying from the benchmark, how much extra return did they deliver?"

- A (0.33) is wrong: this is $$3/9$$ — dividing by the benchmark return instead of the tracking error.
- C (1.50) is wrong: this is $$9/6$$ — using the benchmark return on top instead of the active return.
- D (2.00) is wrong: this is $$12/6$$ — using the manager's total return on top instead of the active (excess-over-benchmark) return.

📌 Exam tip: Information ratio = (active return) / (tracking error). Both pieces are measured RELATIVE to the benchmark, not relative to the risk-free rate.

**Q7.** The Sortino ratio differs from the Sharpe ratio mainly because the Sortino ratio:
- A. Uses beta instead of standard deviation
- B. Penalizes only downside deviation below a minimum acceptable return
- C. Ignores the risk-free rate completely
- D. Can only be applied to bond portfolios

**Answer: B**

The question asks what makes the Sortino ratio special compared with the Sharpe ratio.

The Sharpe ratio uses total standard deviation, which counts ALL ups and downs as "risk" — even the good surprises (returns above target). The Sortino ratio is smarter for lopsided (asymmetric) returns: it only counts the BAD moves, those falling below a chosen minimum acceptable return ($$R_{min}$$). Its denominator is the downside deviation:

$$\sigma_{down} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{R_t < R_{min}} (R_t - R_{min})^2}$$

So B is correct: it only punishes downside.

Analogy: Sharpe scolds you both for falling AND for jumping unexpectedly high; Sortino only scolds you for falling, which seems fairer.

- A is wrong: Sortino does not use beta; it uses downside deviation. Beta belongs to Treynor and Jensen's alpha.
- C is wrong: Sortino still uses a hurdle in the numerator (a minimum acceptable return, often the risk-free rate or zero); it does not ignore it.
- D is wrong: Sortino is general-purpose and is especially useful for options and hedge fund strategies with skewed returns, not just bonds.

📌 Exam tip: When a question mentions "asymmetric returns," "skewed payoffs," or "options/hedge fund strategies," the intended answer is usually the Sortino ratio.

**Q8.** In the RAROC formula, expected losses are treated as:
- A. Added back because they are non-cash
- B. Ignored because economic capital covers them
- C. Deducted as a cost of doing business
- D. Multiplied by the firm's beta

**Answer: C**

The question asks how expected losses appear in the RAROC numerator.

RAROC stands for Risk-Adjusted Return on Capital. Its numerator looks like:

$$\text{revenues} - \text{costs} - \text{expected losses} - \text{taxes} + \text{return on economic capital}$$

Expected losses are losses the firm KNOWS, on average, it will suffer (for example, a predictable fraction of loans that default). Because they are expected, they are simply priced in and subtracted like any other regular business cost. So C is correct.

Analogy: a grocery store knows some fruit will spoil every week. That expected spoilage is just a normal cost it builds into prices — not a disaster it keeps emergency cash for.

- A is wrong: expected losses are not added back; they are real anticipated costs, so they are subtracted.
- B is wrong: economic capital is the buffer for UNEXPECTED losses (the nasty surprises), not for expected ones. Expected losses are handled in income, not capital.
- D is wrong: there is no step that multiplies expected losses by beta; beta enters only in the separate ARAROC adjustment.

📌 Exam tip: Remember the split — EXPECTED losses are a priced cost (deducted from income); UNEXPECTED losses are covered by economic capital.

**Q9.** A business unit has a RAROC of 14%. Its equity beta is 1.5, the expected market return is 9%, and the risk-free rate is 3%. Using the ARAROC decision rule, the project should be:
- A. Rejected, since ARAROC = 5% < 9%
- B. Accepted, since ARAROC = 5% > 3%
- C. Rejected, since ARAROC = 0%
- D. Accepted, since RAROC = 14% > 9%

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to compute ARAROC (Adjusted RAROC) and apply its accept/reject rule.

Step 1 — find the market risk premium: $$E[R_m] - R_f = 9\% - 3\% = 6\%$$.

Step 2 — use the ARAROC formula:

$$ARAROC = RAROC - \beta_E\left(E[R_m] - R_f\right)$$

Plug in: $$ARAROC = 14\% - 1.5 \times 6\%$$.

Step 3 — multiply: $$1.5 \times 6\% = 9\%$$.

Step 4 — subtract: $$14\% - 9\% = 5\%$$.

Step 5 — apply the decision rule: accept if $$ARAROC > R_f$$. Here $$5\% > 3\%$$, so accept. That is option B.

The adjustment charges the business extra for the systematic risk it carries; a high-beta unit must clear a higher bar before we credit it with creating value.

- A is wrong: the rule compares ARAROC to the risk-free rate (3%), not to the market return (9%).
- C is wrong: ARAROC is 5%, not 0%; this comes from an arithmetic error.
- D is wrong: the plain RAROC-vs-market-return comparison is not the ARAROC rule; the ARAROC rule compares the adjusted figure to $$R_f$$.

📌 Exam tip: ARAROC rule: subtract $$\beta_E(R_m - R_f)$$ from RAROC, then accept only if the result beats the RISK-FREE rate.

**Q10.** A bank's lending unit has revenue of $60 million, operating costs of $20 million, and expected losses of $12 million. Its economic capital is $50 million, invested at the risk-free rate of 4%. Ignoring taxes and transfer prices, what is its RAROC?
- A. 56%
- B. 60%
- C. 80%
- D. 64%

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to compute RAROC by building the numerator step by step, then dividing by economic capital.

Formula (one period, ignoring taxes and transfer prices):

$$RAROC = \frac{\text{revenues} - \text{costs} - \text{expected losses} + \text{return on economic capital}}{\text{economic capital}}$$

Step 1 — return on economic capital (the capital is invested safely and earns the risk-free rate): $$0.04 \times 50 = \$2 \text{ million}$$.

Step 2 — build the numerator carefully, one piece at a time:
- Start with revenue: $$60$$.
- Subtract costs: $$60 - 20 = 40$$.
- Subtract expected losses: $$40 - 12 = 28$$.
- Add the return on economic capital: $$28 + 2 = 30$$.

So the numerator is $30 million.

Step 3 — divide by economic capital: $$\frac{30}{50} = 0.60 = 60\%$$.

So RAROC is 60%, option B. This is the risk-adjusted profit the unit earns for each dollar of economic capital it ties up.

- A (56%) is wrong: this comes from forgetting to add back the $2 million return on capital ($$28/50 = 56\%$$).
- C (80%) is wrong: this comes from forgetting to subtract expected losses ($$60 - 20 + 2 = 42$$, then mishandled), an easy slip.
- D (64%) is wrong: this comes from adding expected losses instead of subtracting, or other sign errors in the numerator.

📌 Exam tip: Build the RAROC numerator in order — revenues, minus costs, minus expected losses, plus the risk-free return earned on economic capital — then divide by economic capital. Watch the signs: costs and expected losses are subtracted, the capital return is added.
