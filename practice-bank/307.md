**Q1.** A trader posts $5,000 of initial margin; the maintenance margin is $3,750. After some losses the account balance falls to $3,200. What is the size of the margin call?

- A. $550
- B. $1,250
- C. $1,800
- D. $3,200

**Answer: C**

The question asks how much cash the trader must add after the balance drops below the maintenance margin.

First, check whether a call is triggered: the balance ($3,200) is below the maintenance margin ($3,750), so yes, a call happens.

The rule: when a call is triggered, the trader must top the account back up to the INITIAL margin, not just to the maintenance level.

Margin call = initial margin − current balance = $5,000 − $3,200 = $1,800.

Why C is correct: you restore to the initial margin of $5,000, and you are short by $1,800.

Why A is wrong: $550 = $3,750 − $3,200, which restores only to maintenance. That is the classic trap.
Why B is wrong: $1,250 = $5,000 − $3,750, the gap between initial and maintenance, which is not the call.
Why D is wrong: $3,200 is just the current balance, not the amount owed.

📌 Exam tip: Margin call = Initial margin − current balance. You always top up to INITIAL, never just to maintenance.

**Q2.** An investor goes long 2 gold futures (100 oz each) at $1,800/oz. Initial margin is $12,000 and maintenance margin is $9,000. The next day the settlement price falls to $1,780/oz. What is the new margin balance, and is there a margin call?

- A. Balance $8,000; margin call of $4,000
- B. Balance $8,000; no margin call
- C. Balance $11,600; no margin call
- D. Balance $12,000; margin call of $4,000

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the new balance after a daily loss and whether a call is triggered.

Total ounces = 2 contracts × 100 oz = 200 oz.
Price change = $1,780 − $1,800 = −$20 per oz (a loss because the investor is long and price fell).
Daily loss = $20 × 200 oz = $4,000.
New balance = $12,000 − $4,000 = $8,000.

Is there a call? The balance ($8,000) is below the maintenance margin ($9,000), so yes. The call restores to initial: $12,000 − $8,000 = $4,000.

Why A is correct: balance is $8,000 and it is below maintenance, so a $4,000 call brings it back to $12,000.

Why B is wrong: the balance IS below maintenance ($9,000), so a call is required.
Why C is wrong: $11,600 would come from a $2 (not $20) move; misreading the price change.
Why D is wrong: $12,000 is the balance AFTER paying the call, not before; the question asks for the balance reached by the loss.

📌 Exam tip: Daily gain/loss = price change × total units. For a long, a price drop is a loss. Then compare the new balance to maintenance.

**Q3.** A trade occurs in which the buyer is opening a new long position and the seller is closing an existing short position. The effect on open interest is to:

- A. Increase by the trade size
- B. Decrease by the trade size
- C. Remain unchanged
- D. Increase by twice the trade size

**Answer: C**

The question asks how open interest moves when one side opens and the other closes.

Open interest counts the number of contracts currently outstanding. A contract is created (open interest up) when BOTH sides are opening new positions, and destroyed (open interest down) when BOTH sides are closing. If one side opens and the other closes, the position simply transfers from the old holder to the new one — the total number outstanding does not change.

Here: buyer opens (new long), seller closes (closing a short). One opens, one closes, so open interest is unchanged. Volume still rises by the trade size.

Why C is correct: one new and one closing trade leaves total contracts outstanding the same.

Why A is wrong: open interest rises only when both sides open new positions.
Why B is wrong: open interest falls only when both sides close.
Why D is wrong: open interest never jumps by twice the trade size; that double-counts.

📌 Exam tip: Both open → OI up. Both close → OI down. One of each → OI flat. Volume always rises regardless.

**Q4.** Open interest starts the day at 1,000 contracts. Then: (1) a new long buys 50 from a new short; (2) someone closing a long sells 30 to a new long; (3) someone closing a long sells 20 to someone closing a short. What is the end-of-day open interest and total volume?

- A. Open interest 1,030; volume 100
- B. Open interest 1,000; volume 100
- C. Open interest 1,050; volume 70
- D. Open interest 1,030; volume 70

**Answer: A**

The question tests tracking open interest and volume across several trades.

Start: open interest = 1,000.
- Trade 1: both open (new long, new short) → open interest +50. Volume +50.
- Trade 2: one closes, one opens → open interest unchanged. Volume +30.
- Trade 3: both close (closing a long, closing a short) → open interest −20. Volume +20.

End open interest = 1,000 + 50 + 0 − 20 = 1,030.
Total volume = 50 + 30 + 20 = 100.

Why A is correct: open interest = 1,030 and volume = 100.

Why B is wrong: it ignores the net +30 change to open interest.
Why C is wrong: it mishandles trade 3 (it actually lowers OI) and undercounts volume.
Why D is wrong: open interest is right but volume must include all three trades (100, not 70).

📌 Exam tip: Volume is just the sum of all contracts traded. Open interest changes only on trades where both sides open (+) or both close (−).

**Q5.** As a futures contract approaches expiration, the futures price converges to the spot price. The reason is that:

- A. Exchanges legally fix the two prices to be equal
- B. Otherwise a riskless arbitrage would be available between the futures and the spot market
- C. Daily settlement forces them together
- D. Open interest always falls to zero

**Answer: B**

The question asks WHY futures and spot prices converge at expiration.

At the moment of delivery, a futures contract is essentially a promise to buy or sell the asset right now. So its price must equal today's spot price. If it did not — say the futures were higher than spot — a trader could buy the cheap spot asset, sell the expensive futures, deliver immediately, and pocket a riskless profit. Traders rushing to do this push the prices back together. Convergence is enforced by arbitrage, just like in pricing models.

Why B is correct: any gap at expiration creates riskless arbitrage, which traders close instantly.

Why A is wrong: exchanges do not legally fix prices equal; markets do it through arbitrage.
Why C is wrong: daily settlement marks positions to the settlement price but does not cause convergence; arbitrage does.
Why D is wrong: open interest declining is a consequence of positions closing, not the cause of price convergence.

📌 Exam tip: "Convergence at expiry" is enforced by arbitrage. At expiry the futures IS effectively spot, so any difference is free money.

**Q6.** An investor is long futures at a price of 250 and wants to limit losses. The investor accepts that the execution price is uncertain in a fast market but demands that the order fire automatically if the price falls to 240. The appropriate order is a:

- A. Limit sell at 240
- B. Stop (stop-loss) sell at 240
- C. Market-if-touched sell at 240
- D. Fill-or-kill sell at 240

**Answer: B**

The question asks which order type protects a long position by triggering at a worse price and accepting execution uncertainty.

A stop (stop-loss) order becomes a MARKET order once the stop price is reached. It guarantees the order fires when price hits 240, but it does NOT guarantee the exact execution price in a fast-moving market. That matches exactly what the investor wants: automatic firing at 240, accepting price uncertainty.

Why B is correct: a stop sell at 240 triggers automatically and becomes a market order, capping losses while accepting price slippage.

Why A is wrong: a limit sell at 240 only fills at 240 or better. In a fast falling market the price may blow through 240, and the order might never fill — exactly the protection the investor does NOT get.
Why C is wrong: market-if-touched is used to ENTER at a favorable price (it triggers on the opposite side), not to cap losses on a long.
Why D is wrong: fill-or-kill demands an immediate full fill or cancellation; it is not a loss-limiting trigger order.

📌 Exam tip: Stop → becomes a market order (fires for sure, price not guaranteed). Stop-limit → becomes a limit order (price guaranteed, fill not guaranteed). To cap losses on a long, use a stop sell below the market.

**Q7.** In an inverted futures market:

- A. Futures prices increase with time to maturity
- B. Futures prices decrease with time to maturity
- C. The futures price equals the spot price at every maturity
- D. Daily settlement is suspended

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the definition of an inverted futures market.

In an inverted market, longer-dated futures are priced LOWER than nearer-dated ones — prices fall as maturity gets further out. This usually happens when there is a high convenience yield (people value having the physical asset now, e.g., tight oil supply) or the market expects prices to decline. A normal market is the opposite: prices rise with maturity.

Why B is correct: inverted = prices decrease as time to maturity lengthens.

Why A is wrong: that describes a normal market, the opposite of inverted.
Why C is wrong: equal prices at all maturities is neither normal nor inverted.
Why D is wrong: daily settlement always continues; it is unrelated to the term-structure shape.

📌 Exam tip: Normal market = prices rise with maturity (carry costs dominate). Inverted market = prices fall with maturity (high convenience yield / expected declines).

**Q8.** Which of the following is true about margin requirements for hedgers versus speculators on a futures exchange?

- A. Hedgers face higher margins because they trade larger sizes
- B. Hedgers face lower margins because their futures losses are offset by gains on the underlying exposure
- C. Speculators and hedgers always face identical margins
- D. Hedgers post no margin at all

**Answer: B**

The question asks how margin requirements differ for bona fide hedgers versus speculators.

Exchanges set LOWER initial margin for bona fide hedgers (and often for day trades and spreads). The logic: a hedger holds an offsetting position in the underlying, so a loss on the futures is offset by a gain on the physical exposure. Total risk is lower, so less margin is needed. A speculator has no offset, so the exchange demands more cushion.

Why B is correct: the hedger's underlying offset lowers total risk, justifying a lower margin.

Why A is wrong: margin is set by risk, not trade size; hedgers actually get a discount.
Why C is wrong: they are not identical; hedgers get preferential (lower) treatment.
Why D is wrong: hedgers still post margin, just at a reduced level — not zero.

📌 Exam tip: Lower margin for hedgers because their futures position is offset by a real exposure, so net risk is smaller.

**Q9.** Which statement correctly contrasts forward and futures contracts?

- A. Futures are customized OTC contracts; forwards are standardized exchange contracts
- B. Forwards settle daily; futures settle once at maturity
- C. Futures are exchange-traded and marked to market daily; forwards are OTC and settled once at maturity
- D. Forwards carry minimal counterparty risk; futures carry high counterparty risk

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the correct distinction between forwards and futures.

Futures: traded on an exchange, standardized terms, marked to market daily (gains and losses settle every day), and counterparty risk is minimal because of the clearing house and margining. Forwards: traded over the counter (OTC), customized, settled once at maturity, and carry counterparty credit risk because they rely on the dealer's promise.

Why C is correct: it correctly pairs futures with exchange/daily settlement and forwards with OTC/single settlement.

Why A is wrong: it reverses the two — futures are standardized and exchange-traded; forwards are customized OTC.
Why B is wrong: it reverses settlement — futures settle daily, forwards settle once.
Why D is wrong: it reverses counterparty risk — forwards carry credit risk; futures have minimal risk via clearing.

📌 Exam tip: Futures = Exchange, standardized, daily mark-to-market, minimal credit risk. Forwards = OTC, customized, one settlement at maturity, dealer credit risk.

**Q10.** A short trader files a "notice of intention to deliver" during the delivery period of a physically settled futures contract. Who chooses the delivery timing, grade, and location, and to whom is the notice typically assigned?

- A. The long chooses everything; the notice goes to the newest short
- B. The short chooses within contract terms; the notice is assigned to the oldest outstanding long
- C. The exchange chooses everything randomly
- D. The clearing house delivers the asset itself

**Answer: B**

The question asks who controls the delivery decisions and how the notice is assigned.

In a physically delivered futures contract, the SHORT (the seller) has the choice — within the contract's allowed terms — of when in the delivery period to deliver, which acceptable grade, and which location. The short files a notice of intention to deliver with the exchange, which then assigns it to a long, typically the OLDEST outstanding long position.

Why B is correct: the short chooses (timing, grade, location within terms), and the notice goes to the oldest long.

Why A is wrong: the short, not the long, makes the delivery choices; and assignment goes to the oldest long, not the newest short.
Why C is wrong: the exchange does not choose the delivery details randomly; the short decides within contract rules.
Why D is wrong: the clearing house facilitates and guarantees but does not itself produce and deliver the physical asset.

📌 Exam tip: In physical delivery the SHORT holds the options (timing/grade/location); the exchange assigns to the OLDEST long. Fewer than ~2% of contracts ever reach delivery anyway.
