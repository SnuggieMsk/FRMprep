**Q1.** On S&P's rating scale, the lowest rating that is still considered investment grade is:
- A. BB+
- B. BBB−
- C. A−
- D. BBB+

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the investment-grade/high-yield boundary on S&P's scale.

The investment-grade floor is BBB− (equivalently Baa3 at Moody's). Anything at BB+ or below is speculative grade ("junk" / high yield). B is correct.

- A (BB+) is the top of high yield, just below the boundary — the trap of being one notch off.
- C (A−) is comfortably investment grade but not the lowest one.
- D (BBB+) is investment grade but two notches above the floor.

Analogy: think of a ladder where BBB− is the lowest rung you can still stand on and be called "investment grade." Step down to BB+ and you've fallen into junk.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the boundary cold: BBB− / Baa3 = lowest IG; BB+ / Ba1 = highest HY. A downgrade across it creates a "fallen angel" and can force selling.

**Q2.** Compared with point-in-time (PIT) measures, through-the-cycle (TTC) agency ratings are generally:
- A. More volatile and better short-term default predictors
- B. More stable but slower to reflect current conditions
- C. Based mainly on equity prices
- D. Updated daily

**Answer: B**

The question contrasts TTC and PIT rating philosophies.

TTC ratings condition on average or stressed conditions over a full cycle, so they are smoothed and stable — agencies act only when a change is unlikely to be reversed. The trade-off is timeliness: they are slower to reflect current conditions. B is correct.

- A is reversed: TTC ratings are more stable and worse (not better) short-term predictors.
- C describes market-based PIT measures (e.g., KMV EDFs from equity prices), not TTC ratings.
- D is wrong: TTC ratings change episodically, not daily.

Analogy: TTC ratings are like a person's overall fitness level (stable, slow to change), while PIT measures are like today's heart rate (jumps around with the moment).

📌 Exam tip: TTC = stable, smoothed, low procyclicality, lower short-term accuracy. PIT = volatile, timely, procyclical. Agencies use TTC.

**Q3.** A one-year transition matrix gives a BB issuer: stay BB 80%, up to BBB 8%, down to B 10%, default 2%. One-year default probabilities are 6% for B and 0.5% for BBB. Assuming the Markov property, the two-year cumulative default probability for the BB issuer is closest to:
- A. 2.00%
- B. 4.24%
- C. 6.00%
- D. 4.00%

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to chain a transition matrix over two years.

A BB issuer can default in year 1, or survive year 1 in some state and then default in year 2. Add up all those paths:

- Default in year 1: $0.02$
- Stay BB then default: $0.80 \times 0.02 = 0.016$
- Move to B then default: $0.10 \times 0.06 = 0.006$
- Move to BBB then default: $0.08 \times 0.005 = 0.0004$

Sum:

$$0.02 + 0.016 + 0.006 + 0.0004 = 0.0424 = 4.24\%$$

So B is correct.

- A (2.00%) counts only the year-1 default and ignores year 2.
- C (6.00%) is just B's one-year PD, irrelevant to the BB chain.
- D (4.00%) wrongly takes 2 × the year-1 PD (2% × 2), skipping the proper path conditioning.

Analogy: to find the chance of failing over two years, you must trace every route — fail now, or move to a new "room" first and fail from there.

📌 Exam tip: Multi-year default probability ≠ annual PD × years. Walk the matrix: condition on each surviving state, then default from it.

**Q4.** A B-rated issuer has cumulative default probabilities of 5% by year 1 and 11% by year 2. The marginal (conditional) default probability in year 2, given survival through year 1, is closest to:
- A. 6.00%
- B. 6.32%
- C. 11.00%
- D. 5.50%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the conditional PD in year 2 given the issuer survived year 1.

The extra default probability accumulated in year 2 is $11\% - 5\% = 6\%$. But this is "out of" the issuers who survived year 1 (95%), so divide by the survival probability:

$$PD_{2|1} = \frac{0.11 - 0.05}{1 - 0.05} = \frac{0.06}{0.95} = 0.0632 = 6.32\%$$

So B is correct.

- A (6.00%) is the unconditional increase in cumulative PD, forgetting to divide by the survival probability.
- C (11.00%) is the cumulative two-year PD, not the marginal year-2 figure.
- D (5.50%) is an arbitrary mid-value with no correct derivation.

Analogy: "6% of everyone" defaulted in year 2, but the question asks "what fraction of the survivors?" Since fewer remain (95%), the rate among them is a bit higher (6.32%).

📌 Exam tip: Marginal PD in year n = (cumulative_n − cumulative_{n−1}) ÷ survival through year n−1. The trap answer skips dividing by survival.

**Q5.** A rating transition matrix typically shows which of the following properties?
- A. Rows sum to less than 100% because defaults are excluded
- B. A heavy diagonal, with default probability rising as the rating falls
- C. Equal probability of moving to every other rating
- D. Higher-rated issuers default more often than lower-rated ones

**Answer: B**

The question tests the basic features of a transition matrix.

Ratings are sticky, so the largest probabilities sit on the diagonal (most issuers keep their rating). As you move to lower starting ratings, the default-column probability rises monotonically. B is correct.

- A is wrong: rows sum to 100% and include a default column.
- C is wrong: migrations are concentrated near the current rating, not spread equally.
- D is reversed: lower-rated issuers default more, not higher-rated ones.

Analogy: most people stay roughly where they are year to year (heavy diagonal), and the closer you start to the edge of a cliff (low rating), the more likely you fall off (default).

📌 Exam tip: Heavy diagonal + monotonic default column + rows summing to 100%. Empirically there is also "rating momentum" (recently downgraded issuers tend to be downgraded again), which violates the strict Markov assumption.

**Q6.** Empirically, sovereign and corporate recovery rates tend to:
- A. Stay constant across the economic cycle
- B. Rise when default rates rise
- C. Fall when default rates rise
- D. Depend only on the issuer's credit rating

**Answer: C**

The question asks about the relationship between recoveries and defaults.

Recovery rates are negatively correlated with default rates: both are driven by the cycle, so recoveries fall exactly when defaults spike (in recessions). This worsens tail losses — you lose more per default precisely when there are more defaults. C is correct.

- A is wrong: recoveries are strongly cyclical.
- B is reversed.
- D is wrong: recovery depends mainly on seniority and collateral, not the issuer's rating alone.

Analogy: a fire sale — when many assets are dumped at once (high defaults), prices crater, so recovered value per asset is lower.

📌 Exam tip: Recovery and default rates move oppositely. Seniority drives recovery: senior secured loans recover most, subordinated bonds least.

**Q7.** Altman's Z-score model for credit assessment is best described as:
- A. A market-based measure using equity option prices
- B. A discriminant function combining weighted accounting ratios into a default signal
- C. A through-the-cycle agency rating
- D. A model that outputs an exact probability of default with no zones

**Answer: B**

The question tests what the Altman Z-score is.

The Z-score is a statistical discriminant function that combines weighted accounting ratios (working capital/assets, retained earnings/assets, EBIT/assets, market equity/book liabilities, sales/assets) into a single score, with rough zones: above 3.0 safe, 1.8–3.0 gray, below 1.8 distress. B is correct.

- A describes Merton/KMV market-based models, not Altman's accounting model.
- C is wrong: it is a statistical scoring model, not an agency rating.
- D is wrong: the classic Z-score maps to zones, not an exact PD (modern logit/ML successors output PDs).

Analogy: it's like a credit health-score formula that mixes several lab tests (financial ratios) with fixed weights to flag whether a patient (company) is healthy, borderline, or sick.

📌 Exam tip: Know the idea — weighted accounting ratios → a default signal with safe/gray/distress zones. Don't memorize the exact coefficients for Part I.

**Q8.** Internal credit rating systems at banks differ from external agency ratings primarily because internal ratings:
- A. Are always identical to agency ratings
- B. Cover borrowers without public ratings and often separate obligor (PD) from facility (LGD/EAD) ratings
- C. Cannot be used for regulatory capital
- D. Are always through-the-cycle

**Answer: B**

The question asks how internal ratings differ from agency ratings.

Banks rate borrowers internally to cover names with no public rating (SMEs, retail) and often use a two-dimensional scheme: an obligor rating (PD) plus a facility rating (LGD/EAD). They may be PIT, TTC, or hybrid, and under Basel's IRB approach they feed regulatory capital. B is correct.

- A is wrong: internal ratings are independent and need not match agencies.
- C is wrong: under the IRB approach they are used for regulatory capital (PD, and under advanced IRB also LGD and EAD).
- D is wrong: internal ratings can be PIT, TTC, or hybrid — not always TTC.

Analogy: an agency rating is a public report card; an internal rating is the bank's own detailed file, covering even people the agencies never grade and splitting "how likely to fail" from "how much we'd lose."

📌 Exam tip: Internal ratings = wider coverage + obligor/facility split + feed IRB capital. Watch for rating-inflation pressure from relationship managers; governance and backtesting are essential.

**Q9.** The "issuer-pays" model used by major rating agencies is criticized mainly because it:
- A. Makes ratings too volatile
- B. Creates a conflict of interest, pressuring agencies to rate generously
- C. Prevents agencies from rating sovereigns
- D. Requires investors to pay for every rating

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the central conflict of interest in credit ratings.

Under issuer-pays, the entity being rated pays the agency, creating an incentive to assign favorable ratings to win and keep business. This became acute in structured finance before 2008, where agencies rated CDO tranches AAA using flawed correlation assumptions while earning large fees and advising on structuring ("rating shopping"). B is correct.

- A is wrong: the conflict is about generosity/bias, not volatility (ratings are actually quite stable).
- C is wrong: agencies do rate sovereigns.
- D describes the older "investor-pays" model, the opposite arrangement.

Analogy: it's like a student paying the teacher who grades their exam — there's an obvious temptation to inflate the mark.

📌 Exam tip: Issuer-pays → generosity bias, worst in structured products pre-2008. Post-crisis reforms: agency regulation (SEC/ESMA), less hard-wiring of ratings into rules, methodology disclosure.

**Q10.** A one-year matrix shows an A-rated issuer stays A with 92%, falls to BBB with 6%, and defaults with 0.1%; a BBB issuer's one-year PD is 0.3%. Ignoring other paths, the two-year cumulative default probability for the A issuer is closest to:
- A. 0.11%
- B. 0.21%
- C. 0.29%
- D. 0.40%

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to chain the matrix two years for a high-grade issuer.

Add the relevant default paths:

- Default in year 1: $0.001 = 0.1\%$
- Stay A then default in year 2: $0.92 \times 0.001 = 0.00092 = 0.092\%$
- Move to BBB then default in year 2: $0.06 \times 0.003 = 0.00018 = 0.018\%$

Sum:

$$0.1\% + 0.092\% + 0.018\% = 0.21\%$$

So B is correct.

- A (0.11%) forgets the survive-then-default paths, counting only year-1 default plus a fragment.
- C (0.29%) over-counts a path or mis-multiplies.
- D (0.40%) naively doubles a misread 0.2% annual figure.

Analogy: same idea as before — to fail over two years, you either fail in year 1 or survive (in A or BBB) and then fail. Trace each route and add.

📌 Exam tip: Even for tiny PDs, do the path chaining: year-1 default + (survive in each state × that state's year-2 PD). Don't just double the annual number.
