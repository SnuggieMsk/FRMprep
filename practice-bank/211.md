**Q1.** A time series is described by $$Y_t = Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$, where $$\epsilon_t$$ is white noise with variance $$\sigma^2$$. Starting from a known value $$Y_0$$, what is the variance of $$Y_t$$?
- A. $$\sigma^2$$
- B. $$t\,\sigma^2$$
- C. $$t^2\,\sigma^2$$
- D. $$\sigma^2 / t$$

**Answer: B**

The question describes a random walk (no drift) and asks how uncertain the value becomes after $$t$$ steps.

A random walk just keeps adding a new random shock each period:
$$Y_t = Y_0 + \epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2 + \cdots + \epsilon_t$$

Since $$Y_0$$ is a known number, its variance is 0. Each shock has variance $$\sigma^2$$, and the shocks are independent, so variances simply add up:
$$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + \cdots + \sigma^2 = t\,\sigma^2$$

So the answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. $$\sigma^2$$ would be the variance of just one shock. But the shocks pile up; they do not stay at one period's worth.
- C. $$t^2\sigma^2$$ would happen if the standard deviation (not the variance) grew with $$t$$. Here the variance grows with $$t$$, so standard deviation grows with $$\sqrt{t}$$.
- D. $$\sigma^2/t$$ shrinks over time — that is the opposite of what a random walk does. A random walk gets more uncertain, not less.

Think of a coin-flip walk: every step you take could be left or right at random. The longer you walk, the more spread out you could end up — uncertainty grows.

📌 Exam tip: For a random walk, variance grows linearly with horizon ($$t\sigma^2$$), so the confidence band widens with $$\sqrt{t}$$ — this is why long-horizon forecasts of random walks are so wide.

**Q2.** Which of the following is the defining feature that makes a series with a deterministic linear time trend non-stationary?
- A. Its variance grows without bound over time.
- B. Its mean changes over time.
- C. Its shocks are permanent and never decay.
- D. It contains a unit root.

**Answer: B**

The question asks why a series like $$Y_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1 t + \epsilon_t$$ is not stationary.

A stationary series must have a constant mean, constant variance, and a stable autocovariance over time. In a linear-trend model, the term $$\delta_1 t$$ makes the expected value rise (or fall) every period:
$$E[Y_t] = \delta_0 + \delta_1 t$$

Because the mean keeps moving with $$t$$, the constant-mean requirement is broken, so the series is non-stationary. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. Growing variance describes a unit-root / random-walk series, not a deterministic-trend series. A trend-stationary series has constant variance (the shocks $$\epsilon_t$$ have fixed variance).
- C. Permanent shocks describe a unit root. In a trend-stationary series shocks die out and the series returns to its trend line.
- D. A deterministic trend is not a unit root. You fix a trend by detrending, but you fix a unit root by differencing — different problems.

Picture a straight escalator carrying you upward: your height keeps changing (mean moves), but you are not bouncing around more and more (variance is steady).

📌 Exam tip: Deterministic trend = mean moves (fix by detrending). Unit root = variance explodes and shocks are permanent (fix by differencing). Don't mix them up.

**Q3.** A driftless random walk for a stock price has daily shock volatility $$\sigma = \$3$$ and today's price is \$80. What is the best forecast of the price 25 trading days from now?
- A. \$80
- B. \$95
- C. \$155
- D. \$65

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the point forecast of a random walk several days ahead.

For a random walk without drift, the best (expected) forecast at any future horizon is simply the most recent value, because future shocks have an expected value of zero:
$$E[Y_{t+h} \mid Y_t] = Y_t = \$80$$

So no matter how many days ahead, the forecast stays at today's price, \$80. The answer is A.

Why the others are wrong:
- B and C. These assume some growth or drift is added each day. But this walk has no drift, so the expected change is zero.
- D. This assumes a decline, again implying a non-zero expected drift downward, which is not present.

It is like predicting where a leaf floating on still water will be tomorrow with no current pushing it: your best guess is right where it is now.

📌 Exam tip: For a driftless random walk, the forecast is flat at the current value for every horizon. Only the uncertainty (variance = $$h\sigma^2$$) grows.

**Q4.** An analyst regresses one company's stock price level on an unrelated company's stock price level (both behave like random walks) and finds $$R^2 = 0.88$$ with a t-statistic of 14 on the slope. The most likely explanation is:
- A. The two stocks have a strong genuine economic relationship.
- B. This is a spurious regression caused by regressing two unit-root series on each other.
- C. The residuals are white noise, confirming a good model.
- D. The square-root-of-time rule has been violated.

**Answer: B**

The question describes a very high $$R^2$$ and a huge t-stat from regressing two trending price levels — and asks what is really going on.

When you regress one independent random walk on another, you routinely get a high $$R^2$$ and a significant-looking t-statistic even though there is no true relationship. This is the classic spurious regression problem. The standard errors are wrong because the residuals are strongly autocorrelated, which fools the usual tests. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. A "strong genuine relationship" is exactly the false conclusion the spurious-regression trap tempts you into. High $$R^2$$ between two unit-root series proves nothing.
- C. In a spurious regression the residuals are highly autocorrelated (very low Durbin–Watson), not white noise. This is the telltale warning sign.
- D. The square-root-of-time rule is about scaling volatility across horizons; it has nothing to do with this regression setup.

Imagine two people walking randomly on the same street; both happen to drift north over the day, so a chart makes them look "linked," but neither is following the other.

📌 Exam tip: High $$R^2$$ + high t-stat + very low Durbin–Watson on regressions of price levels = spurious regression. Work with returns/differences, or test for cointegration, first.

**Q5.** The Dickey–Fuller test is run by estimating $$\Delta Y_t = \delta + \gamma Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$. What is the null hypothesis?
- A. $$\gamma < 0$$ — the series is stationary.
- B. $$\gamma = 0$$ — the series contains a unit root.
- C. $$\delta = 0$$ — the series has no drift.
- D. $$\gamma = 1$$ — the series is explosive.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what the Dickey–Fuller test assumes under its null hypothesis.

The Dickey–Fuller setup rewrites an AR(1) so that $$\gamma = \phi - 1$$. A unit root means $$\phi = 1$$, which is the same as $$\gamma = 0$$. The test puts the unit root in the null:
- $$H_0: \gamma = 0$$ (unit root present, non-stationary)
- $$H_1: \gamma < 0$$ (stationary)

So the null is "there is a unit root," making the answer B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. $$\gamma < 0$$ (stationary) is the alternative hypothesis, not the null.
- C. Whether there is drift ($$\delta$$) is a separate matter; the Dickey–Fuller null is about $$\gamma$$, not $$\delta$$.
- D. $$\gamma = 1$$ would mean $$\phi = 2$$, an explosive series — that is not what the test is checking, and it is not the null.

Think of a courtroom: the test starts by assuming "guilty of having a unit root" and only frees the series (declares it stationary) if the evidence ($$\gamma$$ clearly negative) is strong enough.

📌 Exam tip: Dickey–Fuller null = unit root ($$\gamma = 0$$). It is a one-sided, lower-tail test; you reject only when $$\hat\gamma/SE$$ is very negative.

**Q6.** An augmented Dickey–Fuller regression on an index level ($$T = 240$$) gives $$\hat\gamma = -0.018$$ with $$SE(\hat\gamma) = 0.012$$. The 5% Dickey–Fuller critical value (with constant) is about $$-2.86$$. What is the correct conclusion?
- A. Reject the unit root; the series is stationary.
- B. Fail to reject the unit root; treat the series as a random walk and model its returns.
- C. The test is invalid because the t-statistic is negative.
- D. Reject the unit root because $$-1.5$$ is less than $$-1.645$$.

**Answer: B**

The question gives the numbers and asks for the test decision.

First compute the Dickey–Fuller statistic:
$$DF = \frac{\hat\gamma}{SE(\hat\gamma)} = \frac{-0.018}{0.012} = -1.50$$

Now compare with the 5% Dickey–Fuller critical value of about $$-2.86$$. To reject the unit root (a lower-tail test), the statistic must be MORE negative than the critical value. Here:
$$-1.50 > -2.86$$

Since $$-1.50$$ is not negative enough, we fail to reject the null of a unit root. We treat the series as a random walk, so we model its differences (returns) rather than its level. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. We did not reject, so we cannot call it stationary.
- C. A negative DF statistic is completely normal and expected — that is the whole point of a lower-tail test. The test is valid.
- D. This wrongly uses the ordinary normal critical value ($$-1.645$$). Dickey–Fuller statistics do NOT follow the t/normal distribution; you must use the special, more-negative DF critical values. Using $$-1.645$$ would falsely reject.

It is like a high jump bar: the bar (critical value) is set at $$-2.86$$. Your jump only reached $$-1.50$$, so you did not clear it — you fail to reject.

📌 Exam tip: Reject only if DF statistic < critical value (more negative). Always use Dickey–Fuller critical values (~$$-2.86$$ at 5% with constant), never the ordinary $$-1.645$$.

**Q7.** For quarterly data, an analyst wants to model seasonality with dummy variables while also including an intercept. To avoid the dummy variable trap, how many seasonal dummies should be included?
- A. 4 dummies plus the intercept.
- B. 3 dummies plus the intercept.
- C. 5 dummies plus the intercept.
- D. 1 dummy plus the intercept.

**Answer: B**

The question asks how many seasonal dummies to use for quarterly data when an intercept is already in the model.

There are 4 quarters. If you include a dummy for every quarter AND an intercept, the dummies add up to a column of ones that exactly equals the intercept — this perfect collinearity is the "dummy variable trap," and the regression cannot be estimated. To avoid it, drop one category: use $$4 - 1 = 3$$ dummies plus the intercept. The intercept then represents the left-out quarter, and each dummy measures the difference from that base quarter. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. 4 dummies plus an intercept is exactly the trap (perfect collinearity). The valid all-dummy version is 4 dummies and NO intercept.
- C. 5 dummies makes no sense — there are only 4 quarters.
- D. 1 dummy captures only one quarter's effect, leaving the other quarters unmodeled. You need 3 to capture all seasonal differences.

Think of 4 light switches and a master switch (the intercept). If all 4 switches plus the master can produce the same "on" state, the computer can't tell which one did it — so you remove one switch.

📌 Exam tip: With $$s$$ seasons, use either ($$s-1$$) dummies + intercept, OR $$s$$ dummies + no intercept. Never $$s$$ dummies + intercept — that's the dummy trap.

**Q8.** A series follows a deterministic linear trend with stationary (mean-reverting) shocks around that trend. What is the correct way to make it stationary?
- A. First-difference the series.
- B. Detrend it by regressing on time and using the residuals.
- C. Apply the Dickey–Fuller test repeatedly.
- D. Take seasonal differences.

**Answer: B**

The question asks how to handle a trend-stationary series.

A trend-stationary series is stationary once you remove the deterministic trend. You estimate $$Y_t = \delta_0 + \delta_1 t + \epsilon_t$$, then subtract the fitted trend; the leftover residuals are stationary. This is detrending. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. First-differencing is the cure for a unit root, not a deterministic trend. Differencing a trend-stationary series over-differences it and creates a misspecified model (it introduces an artificial moving-average error).
- C. Dickey–Fuller is a test, not a fix; it tells you whether a unit root is present but does not make the series stationary.
- D. Seasonal differencing removes recurring calendar patterns, not a steady linear trend.

It is like correcting for a ramp you are walking up: to study your normal up-and-down bobbing, subtract the ramp's steady slope (detrend) — don't try to remove it by comparing each step to the previous one.

📌 Exam tip: Match the fix to the cause. Deterministic trend → detrend. Unit root → difference. Seasonality → dummies or seasonal differencing. Using the wrong fix creates a misspecified model.

**Q9.** A random walk has drift $$\delta = 0.20$$ per day and daily shock volatility $$\sigma = \$2$$. Today's price is \$50. What is the best forecast 10 days ahead, and how is the variance affected by the drift?
- A. \$52; the drift reduces the variance.
- B. \$52; the variance is unchanged by the drift and equals $$10\sigma^2 = \$40$$.
- C. \$50; the drift increases the variance.
- D. \$70; the variance becomes $$\delta \times 10$$.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the forecast and the variance of a random walk with drift.

The model is $$Y_t = \delta + Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$, which gives:
$$E[Y_{t+h} \mid Y_t] = Y_t + \delta \times h$$

Plug in $$Y_t = \$50$$, $$\delta = 0.20$$, $$h = 10$$:
$$E[Y_{t+10}] = 50 + 0.20 \times 10 = 50 + 2 = \$52$$

The drift is a deterministic tilt — a known amount added each day — so it shifts the mean but adds no randomness. The variance still comes only from the random shocks:
$$\mathrm{Var}(Y_{t+10}) = h\sigma^2 = 10 \times (2)^2 = 10 \times 4 = \$40$$

So the forecast is \$52 and the variance is unchanged by the drift. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. Drift does not reduce variance — it has no effect on variance at all (it is a constant).
- C. The forecast must include the drift, so it is \$52, not \$50; and drift does not raise variance.
- D. \$70 wildly overstates the drift effect, and variance is $$h\sigma^2$$, not $$\delta \times h$$.

Think of an escalator (the drift) carrying you steadily upward while you also randomly sway side to side (the shocks). The escalator changes where you end up on average, but your random swaying is the only thing that makes your final spot uncertain.

📌 Exam tip: Drift moves the mean forecast ($$+\delta h$$) but never changes the variance ($$h\sigma^2$$). The randomness lives entirely in the shocks.

**Q10.** Which statement about the power of the Dickey–Fuller (unit-root) test is correct?
- A. Failing to reject the null proves the series has a unit root.
- B. The test has low power against near-unit-root alternatives such as $$\phi = 0.98$$, so failing to reject does not prove a unit root.
- C. The test always rejects when the true $$\phi = 0.5$$.
- D. The test statistic follows the standard t-distribution under the null.

**Answer: B**

The question asks about a known limitation of the Dickey–Fuller test.

A hypothesis test that fails to reject the null does not "prove" the null — it just means there was not enough evidence against it. The Dickey–Fuller test is especially weak (low power) when the true process is stationary but very close to a unit root, like $$\phi = 0.98$$. In that case the test often fails to reject even though the series is technically stationary. So "fail to reject" should never be read as "proof of a unit root." The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. Failing to reject never proves a null hypothesis — a basic statistics principle. This is precisely the trap.
- C. With $$\phi = 0.5$$ the series is clearly stationary and far from a unit root, so the test usually rejects — but "always" is too strong; no test rejects with certainty.
- D. Under the null the statistic follows the special Dickey–Fuller distribution, NOT the standard t-distribution. That is the whole reason DF critical values exist.

It is like a metal detector that struggles to tell apart "no metal" from "a tiny bit of metal" — when something is borderline ($$\phi = 0.98$$), the detector often stays silent even though metal is technically there.

📌 Exam tip: Low power near $$\phi = 1$$ means a non-rejection is weak evidence. Never say the DF test "proves" a unit root — it only fails to find enough evidence against one.
