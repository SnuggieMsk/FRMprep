**Q1.** A bank wants to hedge the default risk on a large loan to a key client without selling the loan or alerting the client. The most direct instrument is to:
- A. Enter a total return swap paying the loan's total return
- B. Buy a single-name credit default swap (CDS) on the borrower
- C. Issue a credit-linked note (CLN)
- D. Securitize its entire loan book

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the cleanest, most targeted way to shed *default* risk on one borrower while keeping the loan and the relationship quiet. A single-name CDS does exactly that: the bank pays a periodic spread and, if the borrower has a credit event, the protection seller pays par minus recovery. The loan stays on the books and the client is never notified.

It is like buying insurance on a friend's loan you co-signed — you're protected if they default, but you didn't have to sell or cancel anything.

- A is wrong: a total return swap would transfer *both* credit and market risk and changes the loan's economics — more than needed to hedge default.
- C is wrong: issuing a CLN is heavier machinery (a funded note structure), not the most direct hedge for one loan.
- D is wrong: securitizing the whole book is a large, complex transaction far beyond hedging a single client's default risk.

📌 Exam tip: To discreetly hedge ONE name's default risk while keeping the loan, use a single-name CDS. A TRS adds market risk transfer; CLN/securitization are heavier funded structures.

**Q2.** Which credit risk transfer instrument leaves the protection *buyer* with no counterparty risk on the protection provider?
- A. CDS
- B. Total return swap
- C. Credit-linked note (CLN)
- D. Bank guarantee

**Answer: C**

The question asks which structure removes the worry that the protection seller might fail to pay. A credit-linked note is *funded*: the investor (protection seller) pays cash upfront to buy the note, and that cash is held against the reference exposure. Because the protection buyer already holds the money, there is no counterparty risk on the protection.

Think of asking a friend to cover a possible loss: a CDS is a verbal promise (they might not pay), while a CLN is your friend handing you the cash in advance to hold — no chance they default on the promise.

- A is wrong: a CDS is unfunded, so the protection buyer still relies on the seller to pay (AIG-style counterparty risk).
- B is wrong: a TRS is also unfunded and carries counterparty risk on the other party.
- D is wrong: a bank guarantee leaves you holding the guarantor's credit risk (and wrong-way risk if correlated).

📌 Exam tip: Funded structures (CLN, CDO tranche) eliminate counterparty risk to the buyer because cash is posted upfront. Unfunded ones (CDS, TRS, guarantee) leave counterparty risk on the provider.

**Q3.** In a typical securitization, the first losses on the asset pool are absorbed by the:
- A. Senior tranche
- B. Mezzanine tranche
- C. Equity (first-loss) tranche
- D. Originating bank's depositors

**Answer: C**

The question tests the loss waterfall. In a securitization, losses flow bottom-up: the equity (first-loss) tranche takes the first hit, then the mezzanine, and only after those are wiped out does the senior tranche suffer. (Cash flows go the other way — top-down to senior first.)

Picture a stadium that floods from the bottom: the lowest seats (equity) get wet first, the middle rows (mezzanine) next, and the top rows (senior) only if it floods badly.

- A is wrong: the senior tranche has the *first claim* on cash and is the *last* to take losses — that's why it has the highest rating and lowest yield.
- B is wrong: the mezzanine tranche takes losses *after* equity, not first.
- D is wrong: a true sale to a bankruptcy-remote SPV isolates the assets, so the originating bank's depositors are not in the loss line for the pool.

📌 Exam tip: Losses flow bottom-up (equity → mezzanine → senior); cash flows top-down (senior first). Equity = first-loss, highest yield; senior = last-loss, highest rating.

**Q4.** The originate-to-distribute (OTD) business model primarily weakens:
- A. The originator's incentive to screen and monitor borrowers
- B. The SPV's bankruptcy remoteness
- C. Investors' access to credit risk
- D. The waterfall priority of senior tranches

**Answer: A**

The question asks what goes wrong with incentives under OTD. When a bank originates loans only to sell (distribute) them to investors, it no longer bears the losses if those loans go bad. That removes its "skin in the game," so its incentive to carefully screen and monitor borrowers weakens, and underwriting becomes volume-driven.

It is like a used-car dealer who sells cars and never has to honor a warranty — they'll be far less careful about quality than one who must repair every breakdown.

- B is wrong: bankruptcy remoteness is a legal feature of the SPV (true sale), unaffected by the originator's incentives.
- C is wrong: OTD actually *expands* investors' access to credit risk, it doesn't reduce it.
- D is wrong: the waterfall priority of senior tranches is a structural feature, not something OTD erodes.

📌 Exam tip: OTD's core flaw is weakened underwriting incentives (no skin in the game) — a key cause of the subprime crisis. Post-crisis "risk retention" rules force originators to keep a slice (e.g., 5%).

**Q5.** A protection seller writes CDS protection on subprime mortgage CDOs, and that seller's own financial health is highly correlated with the subprime market it is insuring. This situation is best described as:
- A. Basis risk
- B. Wrong-way risk
- C. Funding liquidity risk
- D. Translation risk

**Answer: B**

The question describes a hedge that is most likely to fail precisely when you need it. Wrong-way risk arises when the protection seller's ability to pay deteriorates *at the same time* as the reference credit — so when defaults spike, the seller (e.g., AIG, monolines) is also collapsing and cannot pay.

Imagine buying flood insurance from a company located in the same flood zone as your house: when your house floods, the insurer is underwater too and can't honor the claim.

- A is wrong: basis risk is when a hedge doesn't perfectly track the exposure (different grade/maturity/index), not about correlated counterparty failure.
- C is wrong: funding liquidity risk is about raising cash to meet obligations, not the correlation between seller and reference credit.
- D is wrong: translation risk is an FX accounting concept, unrelated.

📌 Exam tip: Wrong-way risk = your counterparty is likely to default exactly when the protection is needed (their health is correlated with the reference credit). The hedge fails at the worst moment — see AIG and the monolines.

**Q6.** The key purpose of selling securitized assets to a bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle (SPV) is to:
- A. Increase the originator's leverage on its own balance sheet
- B. Isolate the pooled assets from the originator's insolvency through a true sale
- C. Guarantee the senior tranche against all losses
- D. Eliminate the need for credit ratings

**Answer: B**

The question asks why securitizations use an SPV. By making a *true sale* of the asset pool to a bankruptcy-remote SPV, the assets are legally separated from the originator. So even if the originating bank goes bankrupt, the SPV's investors still have claim on the pool's cash flows.

It is like putting money in a trust for your children: even if you go bankrupt, your creditors can't reach what's already legally in the trust.

- A is wrong: securitization typically provides balance-sheet/capital *relief*, not more leverage on the originator's own books.
- C is wrong: the SPV structure does not guarantee the senior tranche against *all* losses; credit enhancement reduces but doesn't eliminate risk.
- D is wrong: tranches are still rated; the SPV does not remove the role of credit ratings.

📌 Exam tip: SPV + true sale = bankruptcy remoteness, isolating the assets from the originator's insolvency. Credit enhancement (subordination, overcollateralization, excess spread, reserves) protects senior tranches but doesn't eliminate risk.

**Q7.** Which credit derivative transfers *both* the credit risk and the market (price) risk of a reference asset?
- A. Single-name CDS
- B. Total return swap (TRS)
- C. First-to-default basket
- D. Index CDS

**Answer: B**

The question asks which instrument passes along the *full* economics of an asset. In a total return swap, one party pays the total return (coupons plus price appreciation, minus depreciation) of the reference asset and receives a floating rate plus a spread. The receiver of total return gains everything — both credit and market/price risk — without funding the asset on its balance sheet.

- A is wrong: a single-name CDS transfers default (credit) risk *only*, not price moves.
- C is wrong: a first-to-default basket covers only the first default among a small basket — still credit risk, not total return.
- D is wrong: an index CDS transfers credit risk on a standardized basket of names, not market/price risk.

📌 Exam tip: TRS = transfers total return (credit + market risk). CDS = transfers default (credit) risk only. This distinction is a favorite GARP trap.

**Q8.** After the 2007–2009 crisis, which of the following changes occurred in the credit derivatives market?
- A. Standardized CDS moved toward central clearing through CCPs
- B. Margin requirements for non-cleared derivatives were abolished
- C. Trade reporting to repositories was discontinued to protect privacy
- D. Single-name CDS liquidity dramatically increased

**Answer: A**

The question asks about post-crisis reforms in credit derivatives. To reduce the opaque, tangled web of bilateral exposures that amplified panic after Lehman, regulators pushed standardized CDS toward central clearing through central counterparties (CCPs), along with trade reporting and standardization (the "Big Bang" protocol).

- B is wrong: margin requirements for non-cleared derivatives were *introduced/strengthened*, not abolished.
- C is wrong: trade reporting to repositories was *increased* for transparency, not discontinued.
- D is wrong: the market shrank and concentrated in index products; single-name CDS liquidity actually *declined*.

📌 Exam tip: Post-crisis CDS changes — central clearing (CCPs), trade reporting (transparency), standardization (Big Bang: fixed coupons, auction settlement, determinations committees), margin for non-cleared trades, and CVA capital charges.

**Q9.** A traditional credit risk mitigant in which counterparties offset their mutual obligations so that only the net amount is at risk on default (under an ISDA master agreement) is called:
- A. Collateralization
- B. Netting
- C. Marking to market
- D. Securitization

**Answer: B**

The question describes reducing exposure by canceling out what two parties owe each other, leaving only the net. That is netting (specifically close-out netting under an ISDA master agreement): instead of each gross obligation being at risk, only the single net figure is exposed if the counterparty defaults.

If you owe a friend $100 and they owe you $70, netting means only $30 is really at risk between you, not the full $100.

- A is wrong: collateralization means posting assets (margin) against an exposure — a different mechanism.
- C is wrong: marking to market periodically settles gains/losses to reset exposure toward zero — related but not the offsetting of mutual obligations.
- D is wrong: securitization is pooling and tranching assets, not offsetting mutual obligations.

📌 Exam tip: Netting (close-out under ISDA) leaves only the net amount at risk — but watch the key risk: legal enforceability across jurisdictions. Don't confuse it with collateralization or marking to market.

**Q10.** Compared with the buy-and-hold model, a distinctive risk of the originate-to-distribute model is:
- A. Heavy long-term balance-sheet usage
- B. Strong incentives to monitor borrowers to maturity
- C. Pipeline/warehouse risk if markets close before loans can be distributed
- D. Complete elimination of reputational and legal risk

**Answer: C**

The question asks for a risk that is specific to OTD. Because OTD relies on selling loans onward, the originator faces pipeline (warehouse) risk: if the securitization market freezes, loans the bank intended to distribute get stuck on its balance sheet, exposing it to losses it never planned to hold.

It is like a fruit reseller who buys produce only to flip it the same day — if buyers vanish, the fruit piles up and rots in the warehouse.

- A is wrong: heavy long-term balance-sheet usage describes the *buy-and-hold* model, not OTD (which is capital-light).
- B is wrong: strong monitoring incentives belong to buy-and-hold; OTD *weakens* them.
- D is wrong: OTD actually *raises* reputational and legal risk from bad originations, it doesn't eliminate them.

📌 Exam tip: OTD risks = pipeline/warehouse risk (loans stuck when markets close), moral hazard (weak underwriting), and reputational/legal risk from bad originations. Buy-and-hold risks = concentration and the credit cycle, with heavy balance-sheet use.
