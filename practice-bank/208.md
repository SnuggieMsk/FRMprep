**Q1.** A multiple regression with $$n = 30$$ observations and $$k = 4$$ slope coefficients has $$R^2 = 0.52$$. The adjusted $$R^2$$ is closest to:
- A. 0.52
- B. 0.44
- C. 0.48
- D. 0.40

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to penalize $$R^2$$ for the number of regressors using the adjusted-$$R^2$$ formula.

Step 1 — Write the formula:
$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1}$$

Step 2 — Find each piece:
$$1 - R^2 = 1 - 0.52 = 0.48$$
$$n - 1 = 30 - 1 = 29$$
$$n - k - 1 = 30 - 4 - 1 = 25$$

Step 3 — Compute the adjustment ratio:
$$\frac{29}{25} = 1.16$$

Step 4 — Combine:
$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - 0.48 \times 1.16 = 1 - 0.5568 = 0.4432 \approx 0.44$$

The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.52): this is the plain $$R^2$$ with no penalty applied.
- C (0.48): this is $$1 - R^2$$, an intermediate quantity, not the adjusted $$R^2$$.
- D (0.40): too large a penalty — likely from using a wrong denominator like $$n - k$$ or $$n - k - 2$$.

Analogy: $$R^2$$ always brags that adding variables helps. Adjusted $$R^2$$ is the skeptical accountant who charges "rent" for every extra regressor, so it comes out lower.

📌 Exam tip: $$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - (1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}$$. The denominator is $$n-k-1$$ (subtract the slopes AND the intercept).

**Q2.** In a wage regression that already includes an intercept, the analyst adds dummy variables for ALL four regions. This causes:
- A. Omitted variable bias.
- B. Heteroskedasticity.
- C. Perfect multicollinearity.
- D. Autocorrelation.

**Answer: C**

The question describes the classic "dummy variable trap."

If you include a dummy for every category PLUS an intercept, the four region dummies always add up to 1 for every observation — which is exactly what the intercept (a column of 1s) already represents. So one regressor is an exact linear combination of the others. OLS then cannot separate their effects: this is perfect multicollinearity. The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: omitted variable bias comes from LEAVING OUT a relevant variable, not from including too many dummies.
- B: heteroskedasticity is about the error variance changing with $$X$$ — unrelated to dummy setup.
- D: autocorrelation is about errors being correlated across time/observations — also unrelated.

Analogy: imagine four light switches that together must always equal "on" — if you also have a master switch that says "on," one of the switches is redundant. The computer cannot tell which switch did the work.

📌 Exam tip: With an intercept, always drop ONE category as the base. Coefficients on the remaining dummies are differences relative to that base.

**Q3.** A regression shows $$R^2 = 0.85$$ and a highly significant overall F-statistic, but NO individual t-statistic is significant. The most likely explanation is:
- A. The model is useless.
- B. Multicollinearity among the regressors.
- C. Heteroskedastic errors.
- D. The sample is too large.

**Answer: B**

The question describes a very specific symptom: the regressors matter as a GROUP (significant F, high $$R^2$$) but no single one stands out (all t-stats insignificant).

This is the signature of multicollinearity. When regressors are highly correlated with each other, OLS cannot tell which one deserves credit, so each individual standard error balloons and each t-stat shrinks. But collectively they still explain $$Y$$ well, so the F-test and $$R^2$$ stay strong. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: a model with $$R^2 = 0.85$$ and a significant F is clearly NOT useless — it explains a lot of variation.
- C: heteroskedasticity corrupts standard errors but does not produce this exact "F-significant, all-t-insignificant" pattern.
- D: a large sample usually makes t-stats LARGER (more significant), not smaller — the opposite of what we see.

Analogy: two nearly identical twins do a job together perfectly (the team works), but you cannot tell which twin actually did each task (no individual credit). That is collinear regressors.

📌 Exam tip: Significant F + insignificant individual t's = multicollinearity. The group matters; the individuals can't be separated.

**Q4.** A model with $$k = 3$$ regressors on $$n = 44$$ observations has $$R^2 = 0.30$$. The overall F-statistic for $$H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0$$ is closest to (critical $$F_{3,40} \approx 2.84$$):
- A. 5.71; reject — the regressors are jointly significant.
- B. 0.30; fail to reject.
- C. 1.43; fail to reject.
- D. 2.84; reject.

**Answer: A**

The question asks whether all the slopes together are jointly zero, using the overall F-test.

Step 1 — Use the $$R^2$$ form of the overall F-statistic:
$$F = \frac{R^2 / k}{(1 - R^2)/(n - k - 1)}$$

Step 2 — Compute the numerator:
$$\frac{R^2}{k} = \frac{0.30}{3} = 0.10$$

Step 3 — Compute the denominator. First $$n - k - 1 = 44 - 3 - 1 = 40$$, then:
$$\frac{1 - R^2}{n - k - 1} = \frac{0.70}{40} = 0.0175$$

Step 4 — Divide:
$$F = \frac{0.10}{0.0175} = 5.71$$

Step 5 — Compare to the critical value 2.84. Since $$5.71 > 2.84$$, reject $$H_0$$. The regressors are jointly significant. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B (0.30): this just reports $$R^2$$, not the F-statistic.
- C (1.43): this comes from mishandling the formula (e.g., dividing 0.30 by 0.21 wrongly).
- D (2.84): this copies the critical value instead of computing the statistic.

Analogy: the F-test asks "does the whole team of regressors explain meaningfully more than nothing?" An F of 5.71, well above the 2.84 bar, says yes.

📌 Exam tip: Overall F (all slopes zero) = $$\frac{R^2/k}{(1-R^2)/(n-k-1)}$$. It is always an upper-tail test — reject for LARGE F.

**Q5.** An unrestricted model ($$k_u = 4$$, $$n = 60$$) has $$RSS_u = 270$$. Dropping two regressors gives a restricted model with $$RSS_r = 306$$. The F-statistic for testing whether the two dropped coefficients are jointly zero ($$q = 2$$) is closest to (critical $$F_{2,55} \approx 3.16$$):
- A. 3.67; reject — the pair belongs in the model.
- B. 0.13; fail to reject.
- C. 7.33; reject.
- D. 1.83; fail to reject.

**Answer: A**

The question asks whether two omitted regressors jointly add value, using the restricted-vs-unrestricted F-test. We compare how much the residual sum of squares (RSS) went UP when we dropped them.

Step 1 — Write the formula:
$$F = \frac{(RSS_r - RSS_u)/q}{RSS_u/(n - k_u - 1)}$$

Step 2 — Numerator: how much worse the fit got, per restriction.
$$RSS_r - RSS_u = 306 - 270 = 36$$
$$\frac{36}{q} = \frac{36}{2} = 18$$

Step 3 — Denominator: the unrestricted RSS spread over its degrees of freedom. $$n - k_u - 1 = 60 - 4 - 1 = 55$$:
$$\frac{270}{55} = 4.909$$

Step 4 — Divide:
$$F = \frac{18}{4.909} = 3.667$$

Step 5 — Compare to 3.16. Since $$3.67 > 3.16$$, reject — the two regressors jointly belong in the model. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B (0.13): this would come from flipping the ratio or dividing 36 by 270 wrongly.
- C (7.33): this forgets to divide the numerator by $$q = 2$$ (using 36 instead of 18).
- D (1.83): this uses 9 in the numerator (over-dividing) or another arithmetic slip.

Analogy: dropping the two variables made the model fit worse (RSS rose from 270 to 306). The F-test asks whether that worsening is big enough to say the variables genuinely mattered. Here it is.

📌 Exam tip: Restricted-vs-unrestricted F: numerator is $$(RSS_r - RSS_u)/q$$, denominator is $$RSS_u/(n - k_u - 1)$$. The restricted RSS is always the larger one.

**Q6.** In a multiple regression of bond spread on rating dummies and leverage, the coefficient on leverage is $$-0.6$$. The correct interpretation is the expected change in spread per one-unit increase in leverage:
- A. Ignoring all other variables.
- B. Holding the other included regressors constant.
- C. Only when all the other variables equal zero.
- D. Only for the omitted rating category.

**Answer: B**

The question tests how to interpret a coefficient in a MULTIPLE regression.

In multiple regression, each slope is a PARTIAL (ceteris paribus) effect: it is the change in $$Y$$ for a one-unit change in that regressor while holding all the OTHER regressors fixed. So $$-0.6$$ means: with the rating dummies held constant, a one-unit rise in leverage is associated with a 0.6 decrease in spread. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: "ignoring all other variables" describes a SIMPLE (single-variable) regression slope, which can differ from the partial effect when regressors are correlated.
- C: the partial effect applies generally (holding others fixed at ANY values), not only when the others are zero.
- D: the coefficient on leverage is the same regardless of rating category; rating effects are captured separately by the dummies.

Analogy: a multiple-regression coefficient is like asking "how much does an extra hour of study raise the test score, comparing students of the SAME age and SAME sleep?" You hold the other factors steady to isolate the one you care about.

📌 Exam tip: Multiple-regression coefficients are partial effects — "holding the other included regressors constant." This differs from a simple regression slope when regressors overlap.

**Q7.** Compared with adding a regressor that is genuinely useless, the $$R^2$$ of a multiple regression will:
- A. Always decrease.
- B. Stay exactly the same or rise — it never falls.
- C. Become negative.
- D. Fall below the adjusted $$R^2$$.

**Answer: B**

The question asks how plain $$R^2$$ behaves when you add ANY new regressor, even a useless one.

Key fact: $$R^2 = 1 - RSS/TSS$$ never falls when you add a variable. Adding a regressor can only keep the residual sum of squares the same or shrink it (OLS can always set the new coefficient to zero if it does not help), so $$R^2$$ can only stay flat or rise. This is exactly why $$R^2$$ cannot be used to compare models of different sizes. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: $$R^2$$ never decreases when adding a regressor — the opposite of "always decrease."
- C: plain $$R^2$$ is always between 0 and 1; it cannot go negative. (Adjusted $$R^2$$ CAN go negative, but that is a different measure.)
- D: $$R^2$$ is always greater than or equal to the adjusted $$R^2$$, never below it.

Analogy: $$R^2$$ is like a scoreboard that can only tick up or stay the same when you add players — even a player who sits on the bench never lowers the score. That is why you need adjusted $$R^2$$ to charge "rent" for extra players.

📌 Exam tip: Plain $$R^2$$ never falls with more regressors → useless for model comparison. Use adjusted $$R^2$$, which can fall and can go negative.

**Q8.** A regression has $$n = 66$$ and $$k = 5$$ with $$R^2 = 0.40$$, giving adjusted $$R^2 = 0.35$$. Adding a 6th regressor raises $$R^2$$ to 0.41. The new adjusted $$R^2$$ is closest to:
- A. 0.41
- B. 0.35
- C. 0.30
- D. 0.45

**Answer: B**

The question asks whether the 6th regressor "earns its keep" by improving the adjusted $$R^2$$.

Step 1 — Formula with the new $$k = 6$$:
$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1}$$

Step 2 — Pieces:
$$1 - R^2 = 1 - 0.41 = 0.59$$
$$n - 1 = 66 - 1 = 65$$
$$n - k - 1 = 66 - 6 - 1 = 59$$

Step 3 — Ratio:
$$\frac{65}{59} = 1.1017$$

Step 4 — Combine:
$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - 0.59 \times 1.1017 = 1 - 0.650 = 0.350 \approx 0.35$$

The adjusted $$R^2$$ is essentially unchanged at 0.35 — the new variable did not earn its degree of freedom. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.41): this is the new plain $$R^2$$, ignoring the penalty.
- C (0.30): too large a drop — wrong degrees of freedom.
- D (0.45): adjusted $$R^2$$ cannot exceed plain $$R^2$$ (0.41), so 0.45 is impossible.

Analogy: the 6th regressor added a tiny bit of explained variation (0.40 → 0.41) but consumed a degree of freedom in rent. The two cancel out, so adjusted $$R^2$$ stands still — a sign the variable is not pulling its weight.

📌 Exam tip: Adjusted $$R^2$$ rises only if the new variable's |t| > 1 — a much lower bar than 5% significance (|t| > 1.96). "Adjusted $$R^2$$ went up" does NOT mean significant.

**Q9.** The single NEW assumption that multiple regression adds beyond single-variable regression is:
- A. Homoskedastic errors.
- B. No perfect multicollinearity among the regressors.
- C. Zero mean of the errors.
- D. Random sampling of the data.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what extra requirement appears once you have more than one explanatory variable.

With several regressors, a new problem can arise: one regressor could be an exact linear combination of the others. If so, OLS cannot separate their effects and cannot even be computed. So multiple regression adds the requirement of NO perfect multicollinearity. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (homoskedasticity): this assumption already exists in single-variable regression — it is not new.
- C (zero-mean errors / exogeneity): this is a core assumption carried over from single regression, not newly introduced.
- D (random sampling): also a carried-over assumption from single regression.

Analogy: with one regressor there is nothing for it to be collinear WITH. Once you have a team of regressors, you must make sure no two of them are perfect duplicates, or the computer cannot assign individual credit.

📌 Exam tip: All single-regression assumptions carry over; multiple regression just ADDS "no perfect multicollinearity." Perfect collinearity = OLS can't be computed at all.

**Q10.** Which statement correctly contrasts $$R^2$$ and adjusted $$R^2$$?
- A. Adjusted $$R^2$$ is always greater than $$R^2$$.
- B. $$R^2$$ can decrease when adding a regressor, but adjusted $$R^2$$ cannot.
- C. Adjusted $$R^2$$ can decrease when a weak regressor is added and can even be negative, while $$R^2$$ cannot.
- D. A high $$R^2$$ proves the model is correctly specified and causal.

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the true relationship between the two goodness-of-fit measures.

The facts:
- Plain $$R^2$$ never falls when you add a regressor and stays between 0 and 1.
- Adjusted $$R^2$$ penalizes extra parameters: it CAN fall when a weak regressor is added, and it CAN even go negative if the fit is very poor relative to the penalty.

So the correct contrast is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: backwards — $$\bar{R}^2 \le R^2$$ always, so adjusted is never greater.
- B: this swaps the two measures. It is $$R^2$$ that never decreases; adjusted $$R^2$$ is the one that can decrease.
- D: a high $$R^2$$ proves NEITHER causality NOR correct specification — a notorious trap. Correlation/fit is not causation.

Analogy: $$R^2$$ is the optimistic salesperson who never reports a loss from adding features. Adjusted $$R^2$$ is the honest accountant who will show a loss if a feature does not pay for itself — and can even report a "net negative."

📌 Exam tip: $$\bar{R}^2 \le R^2$$ always; only adjusted $$R^2$$ can fall or go negative. High $$R^2$$ ≠ causation and ≠ correct specification.
