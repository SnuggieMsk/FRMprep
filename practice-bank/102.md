**Q1.** An airline is heavily exposed to jet fuel prices — a risk in which it has no special informational edge — and deep, liquid futures and options markets for fuel exist. The most appropriate risk management strategy is to:
- A. Accept the risk and hold capital against it
- B. Avoid flying to reduce fuel exposure
- C. Transfer (hedge) the risk using fuel derivatives
- D. Increase fuel exposure to earn the risk premium

**Answer: C**

The question asks which of the four strategies fits a risk that is non-core, uncompensated, and easy to lay off in a deep market. When a firm has no edge in bearing a risk but a liquid market exists to shift it, the right move is to transfer (hedge) it.

Think of a baker who must buy flour: the baker is paid for baking, not for guessing flour prices, so locking in the flour cost with a forward lets them focus on baking.

- A is wrong: accepting/holding capital fits *core* risks the firm is paid to bear — fuel price speculation is not the airline's edge.
- B is wrong: avoiding the activity means not flying, which destroys the business; you avoid risks only when the whole activity is outside appetite.
- D is wrong: deliberately taking *more* of an uncompensated risk is speculation, not risk management.

📌 Exam tip: Match the strategy to the risk: Accept core risks you're paid for; Avoid risks outside appetite/expertise with no reward; Mitigate with cost-effective controls; Transfer non-core risks when a deep market exists.

**Q2.** In a frictionless Modigliani–Miller world (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs), what is the strongest *theoretical* argument that corporate hedging adds shareholder value?
- A. Shareholders cannot diversify firm-specific risk themselves
- B. Hedging reduces expected financial distress and bankruptcy costs
- C. Hedging always increases the firm's expected cash flows
- D. Hedging removes systematic risk from equity returns

**Answer: B**

The question asks where hedging value really comes from. In a perfect M&M world, hedging adds nothing because shareholders can diversify on their own. Hedging creates value only through real-world *frictions* — and reducing the probability and cost of financial distress/bankruptcy is the classic one.

Analogy: insurance on your house doesn't make the house worth more in a perfect world, but in the real world it stops a fire from forcing a panicked fire-sale — that avoided distress is the value.

- A is wrong: it is actually backwards — shareholders generally *can* diversify firm-specific risk cheaply, which is precisely why hedging it adds no value in theory.
- C is wrong: hedging does not reliably raise expected cash flows; it mainly reduces their volatility.
- D is wrong: a firm cannot remove systematic (market) risk from equity returns by hedging its operating exposures, and investors don't pay extra for that.

📌 Exam tip: Hedging adds value through frictions: distress costs, convex (progressive) taxes, and avoiding underinvestment when internal cash is scarce. "Shareholders can hedge themselves" is the M&M argument *against* corporate hedging.

**Q3.** A firm hedges a 10-year obligation to deliver oil by continually rolling short-dated futures (a "stack-and-roll"). Oil prices fall sharply. What is the firm's most immediate danger?
- A. Basis risk between different crude grades
- B. Counterparty default on the exchange-traded futures
- C. Margin calls on the futures exceeding its available cash
- D. Loss of upside on the physical commitment

**Answer: C**

The question points to the famous Metallgesellschaft trap. The futures are marked to market daily, so when prices move against the short-dated position, the firm must post cash variation margin *now* — but the offsetting gain on the long-dated physical obligation only arrives years later. The immediate threat is running out of cash to meet margin calls.

Imagine your future paycheck will cover a loss, but the lender demands cash today; even though you'll be fine later, you can be squeezed now.

- A is wrong: basis risk (grade mismatch) is a real but secondary concern; it doesn't create the urgent cash crisis described.
- B is wrong: exchange-traded futures are margined and cleared, so counterparty default risk is minimal.
- D is wrong: losing upside on the physical commitment is the normal trade-off of any forward-type hedge, not an immediate danger.

📌 Exam tip: Rolling short-dated hedges for a long-dated exposure creates a *funding liquidity* (margin call) timing mismatch — the gains come later, the cash drains now. Remember Metallgesellschaft.

**Q4.** A board states it is "willing to tolerate up to a 20% decline in annual earnings in pursuit of its growth strategy." This statement is best described as part of the firm's:
- A. Risk capacity
- B. Risk appetite
- C. Risk profile
- D. Limit framework

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to label a statement of how much risk the firm is *willing* to take to reach its goals. That is exactly the definition of risk appetite — the amount and type of risk the firm chooses to accept in pursuit of its objectives, set by the board.

- A is wrong: risk capacity is the *maximum* risk the firm *could* absorb given its capital and liquidity — a ceiling, not a chosen willingness.
- C is wrong: risk profile is the risk the firm is *actually* taking right now, not its stated willingness.
- D is wrong: the limit framework is the set of granular desk/business limits that translate appetite into enforceable constraints — more detailed than this high-level statement.

📌 Exam tip: Appetite = willing to take (board's choice). Capacity = maximum can absorb (the ceiling). Profile = actually taking now. Appetite must sit inside capacity.

**Q5.** Which of the four risk management strategies involves reducing the likelihood or severity of a risk while still staying in the activity?
- A. Accept
- B. Avoid
- C. Mitigate
- D. Transfer

**Answer: C**

The question asks which strategy means "stay in the game but make the risk smaller." That is mitigate — using controls, diversification, collateral, or limits to cut the chance or the size of a loss while continuing the activity.

Think of driving but wearing a seatbelt and slowing down in rain: you still drive (stay in the activity) but reduce the harm if something goes wrong.

- A is wrong: accept means keeping the risk as-is and holding capital against it, not actively shrinking it.
- B is wrong: avoid means exiting or never entering — the opposite of staying in the activity.
- D is wrong: transfer means handing the risk to a third party (insurance, derivatives), not reducing it yourself while keeping it.

📌 Exam tip: Mitigate = stay in, but shrink likelihood/severity with controls and diversification. Don't confuse it with Transfer (hand it off) or Avoid (exit entirely).

**Q6.** A company with a known U.S.-dollar payment due in three months from a euro-based operation hedges using an FX forward. This is an example of managing which type of foreign currency exposure?
- A. Translation risk
- B. Transaction risk
- C. Economic risk
- D. Basis risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks which FX exposure involves a *known, specific future cash flow* in a foreign currency. That is transaction risk — the risk that the exchange rate moves before a contracted payment or receipt settles. It is naturally hedged with FX forwards, futures, money-market hedges, or options.

- A is wrong: translation risk is the accounting risk from *consolidating* foreign subsidiaries' financial statements, not from a specific cash payment.
- C is wrong: economic risk is the long-run competitive exposure to currency levels (managed structurally, e.g., where you locate production), not a single dated cash flow.
- D is wrong: basis risk is a hedging imperfection (the hedge instrument not tracking the exposure), not a category of FX exposure.

📌 Exam tip: Transaction = known future FX cash flow (hedge with forwards/options). Translation = accounting consolidation of subsidiaries. Economic = long-run competitive FX exposure (managed structurally).

**Q7.** A firm enters a sound economic hedge with a derivative. The derivative is marked to market through earnings, while the hedged underlying is recorded on an accrual basis. A likely result is that:
- A. The economic hedge has clearly failed
- B. Reported earnings become more volatile even though economic risk fell
- C. The firm has eliminated all of its market risk
- D. The hedge has introduced new counterparty default risk on an exchange

**Answer: B**

The question highlights a classic accounting-vs-economics trap. The derivative's value swings show up in earnings each period, but the underlying item's offsetting change may not be recognized at the same time. So even a good hedge can make *reported* earnings look bumpier, while the true economic risk has actually gone down.

It is like weighing yourself right after drinking water: the scale (accounting) jumps around even though your real body (economics) hasn't changed much.

- A is wrong: more volatile reported earnings does not mean the hedge failed — the economics can be perfectly sound.
- C is wrong: hedges reduce specific exposures; they do not eliminate "all market risk," and that is not what the scenario claims.
- D is wrong: the scenario is about accounting timing, not counterparty risk; exchange-traded derivatives have minimal counterparty risk anyway.

📌 Exam tip: Don't equate "earnings got more volatile" with "the hedge failed." Mark-to-market on the derivative vs. accrual on the underlying creates *reported* volatility even when the economic hedge is correct.

**Q8.** A bank wants to convert its floating-rate interest payments into fixed payments to stabilize its borrowing cost. The most direct instrument is:
- A. An interest rate cap
- B. An interest rate swap
- C. A foreign exchange forward
- D. A credit default swap

**Answer: B**

The question asks which tool transforms floating-rate cash flows into fixed-rate cash flows. An interest rate swap does exactly this: the firm agrees to pay a fixed rate and receive a floating rate (or vice versa), converting the profile of its interest payments over multiple periods.

- A is wrong: a cap only *limits* (puts a ceiling on) a floating rate for an upfront premium; it doesn't fix the rate, and it keeps the benefit if rates fall.
- C is wrong: an FX forward hedges currency exposure, not interest rate type.
- D is wrong: a credit default swap hedges default risk of a reference entity, not interest rate exposure.

📌 Exam tip: Swap = transform floating↔fixed cash flows over many periods (symmetric, no upfront cost). Cap = pay a premium to limit a floating rate while keeping the downside benefit (asymmetric).

**Q9.** Compared with a forward/futures hedge, the key advantage of using an option (such as a cap) to hedge a price exposure is that an option:
- A. Has no upfront cost
- B. Provides a linear, symmetric payoff
- C. Protects against adverse moves while keeping the favorable side, for a premium
- D. Removes all counterparty risk automatically

**Answer: C**

The question contrasts options with forwards as hedging tools. A forward locks in a price, removing both downside *and* upside. An option, by contrast, protects you against bad moves but lets you still benefit from good moves — and you pay an upfront premium for that asymmetric protection.

It is like insurance: you pay a premium, you're covered if disaster strikes, but if nothing bad happens you simply keep your good outcome (minus the premium).

- A is wrong: options require paying a premium upfront — that is precisely their cost; forwards have no upfront cost.
- B is wrong: a linear, symmetric payoff describes forwards/futures and swaps, not options (which are asymmetric).
- D is wrong: an option does not "automatically" remove counterparty risk; OTC options carry counterparty risk, and it is exchange clearing (not the option feature) that mitigates it.

📌 Exam tip: Forward = lock the price (gives up upside, no upfront cost). Option = insure the downside, keep the upside (asymmetric, costs a premium). That premium is the price of keeping the good side.

**Q10.** Which statement about risk limits is most accurate?
- A. A limit that is routinely waived still functions as an effective limit
- B. Risk limits translate the firm's risk appetite into enforceable constraints with monitoring and escalation
- C. Risk limits are only useful for credit risk, not market risk
- D. Limits should be set so that they are never approached, ensuring zero breaches

**Answer: B**

The question tests what risk limits are for. Limits (notional, stop-loss, VaR, concentration, tenor) are the practical bridge that turns the board's high-level risk appetite into concrete, enforceable rules at the desk level — complete with ownership, monitoring, and escalation when breached.

Think of speed limits on a road: they turn the vague goal "drive safely" into an enforceable number that police can monitor.

- A is wrong: a limit that is routinely waived is not a real limit at all — enforcement and escalation are what make it meaningful.
- C is wrong: limits apply across many risk types (market, credit, concentration, tenor), not just credit.
- D is wrong: limits are meant to be used and operated near deliberately, not set absurdly high so they are never relevant; the point is to constrain, not to be cosmetic.

📌 Exam tip: Limits convert appetite into enforceable constraints. Good limit regimes have clear ownership, monitoring, and escalation. "A limit that is routinely waived is not a limit."
