**Q1.** Which of the following is a core function that derivatives exchanges provide?
- A. Fully customized, bilaterally negotiated contract terms
- B. Standardization of contracts, which concentrates trading and creates liquidity
- C. Guaranteed elimination of all market (price) risk
- D. Private prices visible only to the two trading parties

**Answer: B**

The question asks what an exchange fundamentally does for traders.

Exchanges fix the contract terms — size, quality of the underlying, delivery dates and locations — so the only thing left to negotiate is price. This standardization funnels everyone into a handful of identical contracts, which gathers many buyers and sellers in one place and creates liquidity (you can easily get in and out). Exchanges also provide transparent public prices and manage counterparty risk through a clearing house.

Analogy: standardized shoe sizes mean millions of people can buy and resell the same size easily. If every shoe were custom-made, there'd be no easy resale market — standardization creates a liquid market.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (customization) describes the OTC market, the opposite of an exchange.
- C is false — exchanges manage COUNTERPARTY risk, not market/price risk; prices still move and you can still lose money.
- D is false — exchange prices are PUBLIC and transparent, not private.

📌 Exam tip: Exchange benefits = standardization → liquidity, price transparency, counterparty risk management. It does NOT remove market risk. Customization belongs to OTC.

**Q2.** Under novation by an exchange clearing house, what happens to a trade between an original buyer and seller?
- A. The original counterparties remain liable to each other, with the clearing house only guaranteeing
- B. The original contract is replaced by two contracts, each with the clearing house as the counterparty
- C. Contracts are netted multilaterally among clearing-ring members
- D. The buyer's obligations are transferred to a special purpose vehicle

**Answer: B**

The question asks what novation does in central clearing.

Novation means the clearing house steps into the middle and the original contract is legally torn up and replaced by TWO new contracts: the clearing house becomes the buyer to the original seller, and the seller to the original buyer. After this, neither original party faces the other anymore — each faces only the clearing house. That's how a CCP concentrates and manages counterparty risk.

Analogy: instead of you lending money directly to a stranger, a trusted bank steps in: the bank now owes you, and the stranger now owes the bank. You never deal with the stranger again.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes a mere guarantee, but novation goes further — the original contract is REPLACED, not just guaranteed.
- C describes clearing RINGS, an earlier stage of clearing, not novation by a CCP.
- D describes SPV usage in OTC markets, not central-clearing novation.

📌 Exam tip: Novation = original contract replaced by two contracts, CCP in the middle (buyer to every seller, seller to every buyer). Members then face only the clearing house.

**Q3.** Which statement best describes the role of variation margin in exchange clearing?
- A. It is posted once when the position opens and never adjusted
- B. It is the daily settlement of mark-to-market gains and losses, resetting each contract's value to zero
- C. It is extra collateral posted regardless of price moves
- D. It is a fee paid to the exchange for trade execution

**Answer: B**

The question asks what variation margin (VM) is.

Each day, positions are marked to market (revalued at current prices). Whoever lost value that day pays variation margin; whoever gained receives it. This daily cash settlement of gains and losses resets each contract's value back to zero every day, so losses can't quietly pile up and create a big unpaid exposure.

Analogy: it's like settling up a running tab with friends every single night instead of letting it grow into a huge bill — small daily settlements stop a dangerous balance from building.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes INITIAL margin (posted at the start, sized for the close-out period), not variation margin.
- C describes an "independent amount" / extra cushion, not variation margin.
- D is false — VM is the settlement of P&L, not an execution fee.

📌 Exam tip: Initial margin = posted upfront, covers potential close-out losses (driven by volatility). Variation margin = daily settlement of mark-to-market P&L, resets value to zero. Don't mix them up.

**Q4.** A bank has four trades with one counterparty worth (to the bank) +$8M, −$5M, +$3M, and −$2M. Its current credit exposure WITH and WITHOUT close-out netting is closest to:
- A. $4M with netting; $11M without
- B. $11M with netting; $4M without
- C. $4M with netting; $18M without
- D. $11M with netting; $11M without

**Answer: A**

The question asks how netting changes credit exposure if the counterparty defaults.

Without netting: if the counterparty defaults, the bank still loses the positive-value trades (the money owed TO it) but cannot escape paying the negative ones it owes. So exposure counts only the positives:
$$8 + 3 = 11 \text{ (million)}$$

With netting (close-out netting under an ISDA master agreement): all trades are combined into one net figure, and exposure is that net amount if positive (you can't have negative exposure):
$$\max(8 - 5 + 3 - 2,\, 0) = \max(4, 0) = 4 \text{ (million)}$$

So $4M with netting, $11M without. Answer A.

Analogy: without netting, you must pay every debt you owe but can only hope to collect what others owe you. With netting, everything is added into one balance — your debts cancel against what you're owed, leaving a single smaller figure.

Why the others are wrong:
- B reverses the two values.
- C ($18M without) wrongly adds the absolute values of ALL trades (8+5+3+2); only POSITIVE-value trades count as exposure without netting.
- D ignores netting's benefit entirely.

📌 Exam tip: Without netting, exposure = sum of POSITIVE values only. With netting, exposure = max(sum of all values, 0). Netting reduces exposure by letting negatives offset positives.

**Q5.** In an OTC Credit Support Annex (CSA), the "threshold" refers to:
- A. The minimum size below which a collateral call is not made
- B. The amount of uncollateralized exposure allowed before collateral must be posted
- C. The discount applied to non-cash collateral
- D. Extra collateral posted regardless of mark-to-market value

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the meaning of the CSA "threshold."

A threshold is the amount of exposure a party is willing to leave UNCOLLATERALIZED before requiring collateral. For example, with a $5M threshold against a $12M exposure, only $12M − $5M = $7M of collateral must be posted. A zero threshold means full collateralization.

Analogy: a threshold is like a credit limit you extend to a friend before asking for any deposit — you let the tab run up to a set amount before requiring security.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes the "minimum transfer amount" (small calls aren't made), a different CSA term.
- C describes a "haircut" (discount on non-cash collateral), not the threshold.
- D describes the "independent amount" (extra collateral like initial margin), not the threshold.

📌 Exam tip: CSA terms — Threshold = uncollateralized exposure allowed; Independent amount = extra cushion (like IM); Haircut = discount on non-cash collateral; Minimum transfer amount = ignore tiny calls. Keep these four straight.

**Q6.** Why did monoline insurers fail as derivative counterparties during the 2008 crisis?
- A. They posted too much collateral, draining their liquidity
- B. Their exposures were fully netted away under ISDA agreements
- C. Downgrades triggered collateral calls they could not meet, after years of posting little or no collateral
- D. Central clearing mandates forced them out of business

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the mechanism behind the monoline failures.

Monolines were highly rated (AAA) insurers that sold credit protection (like guaranteeing CDS). Because of their top ratings, they posted little or no collateral. But their contracts had ratings-based triggers: if they were downgraded, they'd suddenly have to post huge amounts of collateral. In 2008, losses on structured products caused downgrades, the downgrades triggered massive collateral calls, and they couldn't meet them — a death spiral. Protection buyers found their "hedge" was worthless exactly when they needed it most.

Analogy: imagine a guard who promised to step in only if his fitness rating ever dropped — but the moment his rating dropped (the worst possible moment), he was suddenly required to do far more than he could handle, and collapsed.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is backwards — they posted TOO LITTLE collateral while rated AAA, then faced sudden huge calls.
- B is false — netting didn't save them; the problem was the collateral cliff from downgrades.
- D is false — central clearing mandates came AFTER the crisis as a reform; they didn't cause monoline failures.

📌 Exam tip: Monoline lesson = ratings-based collateral triggers create wrong-way, cliff-edge risk. The counterparty must post a flood of collateral precisely when downgraded and least able to pay — so the protection fails when it's most needed.

**Q7.** A special purpose vehicle (SPV) used in the OTC derivatives market is best described as:
- A. A central counterparty that novates all exchange trades
- B. A bankruptcy-remote entity that holds collateral/transactions so one counterparty gets priority over the sponsor's other creditors
- C. A government fund that insures derivative losses
- D. An interdealer broker that anonymously matches dealers

**Answer: B**

The question asks what an SPV is in OTC markets.

An SPV is a separate, "bankruptcy-remote" legal entity. By routing transactions or holding collateral through the SPV, a counterparty can get priority over the sponsor's other creditors if things go wrong — improving its position. The catch: this converts counterparty risk into LEGAL risk, because courts might "consolidate" the SPV back with the sponsor, undoing the protection.

Analogy: it's like putting valuables in a separate locked safe deposit box (in a separate legal name) so that if the main household goes bankrupt, those valuables are protected for a specific person. But a court might still rule the box belongs to the household.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes a central counterparty (CCP), not an SPV.
- C is false — no government fund insures derivative losses through SPVs.
- D describes an interdealer broker, a different OTC participant.

📌 Exam tip: SPV = bankruptcy-remote entity giving a counterparty priority; its weakness is legal risk (possible court consolidation with the sponsor). Don't confuse SPVs, DPCs, monolines, and CCPs.

**Q8.** Which of the following was NOT one of the post-crisis G20 reforms of OTC derivatives markets?
- A. Mandatory central clearing of standardized OTC derivatives through CCPs
- B. Reporting of all OTC derivatives to trade repositories
- C. A requirement that ALL OTC derivatives be converted into exchange-traded futures
- D. Higher capital and bilateral margin requirements for non-centrally cleared derivatives

**Answer: C**

The question asks which item was NOT a real reform.

After 2008, the G20 (implemented via Dodd-Frank in the US and EMIR in the EU) required: mandatory central clearing of STANDARDIZED OTC derivatives, reporting of all OTC trades to repositories (transparency), trading standardized contracts on electronic platforms (SEFs) where appropriate, and higher capital/margin for derivatives that remain non-centrally cleared. Crucially, bespoke (custom) OTC contracts STILL EXIST — nothing forced everything to convert into exchange-traded futures.

Analogy: the reforms made the OTC market behave MORE like an exchange for the common, standardized products, but they didn't ban custom-tailored contracts for special needs.

Why the others are wrong:
- A, B, and D are all genuine G20 reforms.
- C is the false statement — custom OTC contracts survive; only standardized ones must be cleared and (where appropriate) traded on platforms.

📌 Exam tip: G20 OTC reforms = central clearing of standardized contracts, trade reporting, electronic platforms (SEFs), higher margin/capital for non-cleared. Bespoke OTC trades remain — they were NOT all converted to futures.

**Q9.** Compared with exchange margining, traditional OTC collateralization under a CSA generally:
- A. Eliminates counterparty risk entirely, just like a clearing house
- B. Mitigates but does not eliminate counterparty risk, being often one-way, subject to thresholds, and transferred less frequently
- C. Requires daily variation margin enforced by a central counterparty
- D. Is identical to exchange margining in every respect

**Answer: B**

The question asks how OTC CSA collateral compares to exchange margining.

Exchange margining is strict: daily (or intraday) variation margin, enforced by a clearing house, that effectively resets exposure to zero. Traditional CSA collateral is looser: it was often one-way (only one side posts), subject to thresholds (some exposure left uncollateralized), sometimes disputed, and transferred less frequently. So it REDUCES counterparty risk but does not fully eliminate it the way an exchange clearing house does.

Analogy: exchange margining is like a strict daily settling-up enforced by a referee. A CSA is more like a casual agreement between two people to put up some security sometimes — helpful, but with gaps.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — only an exchange/CCP comes close to eliminating counterparty risk; a CSA leaves residual risk.
- C is false — there's no central counterparty in traditional bilateral CSA collateral, and transfers were less frequent than exchange VM.
- D is false — they differ in frequency, enforcement, thresholds, and directionality.

📌 Exam tip: Exchange margin = daily, CCP-enforced, resets exposure to zero. Traditional CSA = often one-way, thresholds, less frequent, disputable → mitigates but doesn't eliminate counterparty risk.

**Q10.** Which stage of clearing development allows a trader to exit a position by "ringing out" against any member of a group, rather than only the original counterparty?
- A. Direct clearing
- B. Clearing rings
- C. Complete clearing through a central counterparty
- D. Bilateral netting under an ISDA agreement

**Answer: B**

The question asks which clearing stage lets you offset against any ring member.

Clearing developed in three stages. Clearing rings are the middle stage: a group of members agrees to net offsetting positions among themselves, so a trader can close out (exit) by "ringing out" against ANY member of the ring, not just the specific person they originally traded with. This is more flexible than direct clearing but less complete than a central counterparty.

Analogy: clearing rings are like a group of friends who agree that any of them can settle a debt on behalf of the others — you don't have to track down the exact person you originally owed.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (direct clearing) settles bilaterally; you can only net with the SAME original counterparty, not any member.
- C (complete clearing / CCP) goes further — through novation, everyone faces the clearing house itself; it's the modern, most complete stage, not the ring stage.
- D (ISDA bilateral netting) is an OTC counterparty-risk tool, not a stage of exchange-clearing development.

📌 Exam tip: Three clearing stages — Direct (same counterparty only) → Clearing rings (offset against any ring member) → Complete clearing/CCP (novation; everyone faces the clearing house). The ring stage is the "any member" middle step.
