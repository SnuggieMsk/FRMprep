**Q1.** A rate of 8% with semiannual compounding is equivalent to which continuously compounded rate?
- A. 8.16%
- B. 7.844%
- C. 8.00%
- D. 7.87%

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to convert a semiannually compounded rate into a continuously compounded one.

The conversion formula (from m-times compounding to continuous) is:

$$r_c = m \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right)$$

Here m = 2 (semiannual) and $$r_m = 0.08$$.

Step by step:
- $$\frac{r_m}{m} = \frac{0.08}{2} = 0.04$$
- $$1 + 0.04 = 1.04$$
- $$\ln(1.04) = 0.039221$$
- $$r_c = 2 \times 0.039221 = 0.078442 = 7.844\%$$

So the continuous equivalent is 7.844%.

Why it is LOWER than 8%: more frequent compounding (continuous is the most frequent) needs a smaller quoted rate to reach the same growth, because the interest compounds more often.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (8.16%): That's the EFFECTIVE ANNUAL rate ((1.04)^2 - 1), not the continuous rate.
- C (8.00%): That's the original semiannual rate, unchanged.
- D (7.87%): That's the MONTHLY-compounded equivalent, not continuous.

📌 Exam tip: To go to continuous, use r_c = m x ln(1 + r_m/m). The continuous rate is always the LOWEST for the same growth.

**Q2.** A rate of 6% with monthly compounding is equivalent to a continuously compounded rate closest to:
- A. 6.00%
- B. 5.985%
- C. 6.17%
- D. 5.84%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the continuous equivalent of a monthly-compounded 6%.

Use the same conversion with m = 12:

$$r_c = m \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right) = 12 \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)$$

Step by step:
- $$\frac{0.06}{12} = 0.005$$
- $$1 + 0.005 = 1.005$$
- $$\ln(1.005) = 0.0049875$$
- $$r_c = 12 \times 0.0049875 = 0.059850 = 5.985\%$$

So the continuous equivalent is 5.985%.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (6.00%): That's the original monthly rate unchanged - continuous must be slightly lower.
- C (6.17%): That's roughly the effective ANNUAL rate ((1.005)^12 - 1 = 6.17%), not continuous.
- D (5.84%): Too low - this might come from using m = 2 instead of m = 12.

📌 Exam tip: More compounding -> lower quoted rate for the same outcome. Continuous (the extreme) sits just below the monthly rate, which sits below the annual effective rate.

**Q3.** $100 is invested for 2 years at a 5% rate with continuous compounding. The terminal value is closest to:
- A. $110.25
- B. $110.52
- C. $110.00
- D. $110.38

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the future value under continuous compounding.

The continuous-compounding growth formula is:

$$FV = P \cdot e^{rT}$$

with P = $100, r = 0.05, T = 2.

Step by step:
- $$rT = 0.05 \times 2 = 0.10$$
- $$e^{0.10} = 1.10517$$
- $$FV = 100 \times 1.10517 = 110.52$$

So the terminal value is $110.52.

Analogy: Continuous compounding is like interest being added every instant rather than once a year, so it grows a touch faster than annual compounding.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($110.25): That's ANNUAL compounding: 100 x (1.05)^2 = 110.25 - thin compared with continuous.
- C ($110.00): Simple interest, no compounding (100 + 5 + 5).
- D ($110.38): That's SEMIANNUAL compounding: 100 x (1.025)^4 = 110.38.

📌 Exam tip: Continuous compounding uses e^(rT). It always gives the LARGEST future value among compounding conventions for the same quoted rate.

**Q4.** Order the following rates from highest to lowest, given they all produce the SAME terminal wealth: annual, semiannual, monthly, and continuous compounding.
- A. Continuous > monthly > semiannual > annual
- B. Annual > semiannual > monthly > continuous
- C. Semiannual > annual > continuous > monthly
- D. They are all equal

**Answer: B**

The question asks how quoted rates rank when they produce identical growth.

The key idea: the MORE often interest compounds, the LESS the quoted rate needs to be to reach the same final wealth. Annual compounding compounds least often, so it needs the highest quoted rate. Continuous compounds most often, so it needs the lowest quoted rate.

So the ordering is:

$$r_{annual} > r_{semi} > r_{monthly} > r_{cont}$$

We saw this with 8% semiannual: annual effective 8.16% > semi 8.00% > monthly 7.87% > continuous 7.844%.

Analogy: To fill a bucket to the same level, a faster-dripping tap (more frequent compounding) can run at a lower flow rate. More frequent = lower rate needed.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Exactly reversed - continuous should be lowest, not highest.
- C: Jumbled and incorrect ordering.
- D: They are NOT equal as quoted rates; they only produce the same WEALTH.

📌 Exam tip: For equal terminal wealth: annual > semiannual > monthly > continuous (quoted rates). More compounding -> lower quoted rate.

**Q5.** The 1-year spot rate is 3.00% and the 2-year spot rate is 3.50% (both annually compounded). The 1-year forward rate covering year 2 is closest to:
- A. 3.25%
- B. 4.00%
- C. 3.50%
- D. 2.50%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the forward rate for the second year, given two spot rates.

No-arbitrage says: investing for 2 years directly must equal investing for 1 year then rolling at the forward rate:

$$(1 + z_2)^2 = (1 + z_1)^1 \times (1 + f)$$

Solve for f:

$$1 + f = \frac{(1 + z_2)^2}{1 + z_1} = \frac{(1.035)^2}{1.03}$$

Step by step:
- $$(1.035)^2 = 1.071225$$
- $$\frac{1.071225}{1.03} = 1.040024$$
- $$f = 1.040024 - 1 = 0.040024 = 4.00\%$$

So the forward rate for year 2 is 4.00%.

It sits ABOVE both spot rates (3.00% and 3.50%), which makes sense for an upward-sloping curve - the forward is the "marginal" rate for stretching maturity by one more year.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (3.25%): That's the simple average of the two spots - forwards are not simple averages.
- C (3.50%): That's the 2-year spot, not the forward.
- D (2.50%): Below both spots - wrong direction for a rising curve.

📌 Exam tip: Forward = ((1+z2)^2 / (1+z1)) - 1 for this case. On a rising curve, forward > spot. The forward amplifies the curve's slope.

**Q6.** The 1-year spot rate is 2.0% and the 18-month (1.5-year) spot rate is 2.4%, both continuously compounded. The 6-month forward rate starting at year 1 is:
- A. 2.2%
- B. 2.8%
- C. 3.2%
- D. 4.0%

**Answer: C**

The question asks for a forward rate using continuous compounding, which has a clean shortcut formula:

$$f(t_1, t_2) = \frac{z_2 t_2 - z_1 t_1}{t_2 - t_1}$$

Here $$t_1 = 1$$, $$t_2 = 1.5$$, $$z_1 = 0.02$$, $$z_2 = 0.024$$.

Step by step:
- $$z_2 t_2 = 0.024 \times 1.5 = 0.036$$
- $$z_1 t_1 = 0.02 \times 1 = 0.020$$
- Numerator: $$0.036 - 0.020 = 0.016$$
- Denominator: $$1.5 - 1 = 0.5$$
- $$f = \frac{0.016}{0.5} = 0.032 = 3.2\%$$

So the 6-month forward starting at year 1 is 3.2%.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (2.2%): Looks like a simple average of the two spots - not the forward.
- B (2.8%): Comes from a miscalculation of the numerator or denominator.
- D (4.0%): Forgot to divide by (t2 - t1) = 0.5 properly, doubling the result.

📌 Exam tip: With continuous compounding, forward = (z2 x t2 - z1 x t1) / (t2 - t1). It's just the "extra" return needed over the extra time period. Don't forget to divide by the time gap.

**Q7.** The spot rate z(t) relates to the discount factor d(t). Under continuous compounding, the discount factor is:
- A. $$d(t) = e^{z(t)\,t}$$
- B. $$d(t) = e^{-z(t)\,t}$$
- C. $$d(t) = 1 + z(t)\,t$$
- D. $$d(t) = z(t) \times t$$

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the discount factor formula under continuous compounding.

A discount factor is the value TODAY of $1 received at time t. To discount, you shrink a future amount back to today, which uses a NEGATIVE exponent:

$$d(t) = e^{-z(t)\,t}$$

This is the mirror image of growth: growing $1 forward uses $$e^{+z t}$$, so bringing $1 back uses $$e^{-z t}$$.

Quick sanity check: with a positive rate z, the exponent is negative, so d(t) is less than 1 - which is correct, since $1 in the future is worth less than $1 today.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: A POSITIVE exponent grows money forward; that would make d(t) bigger than 1, which is wrong for a discount factor.
- C: That's simple-interest growth, not continuous discounting, and it gives a value above 1.
- D: That's just z times t, not a discount factor at all (and not between 0 and 1).

📌 Exam tip: Discounting uses a NEGATIVE exponent: d(t) = e^(-z t). Growing forward uses the positive exponent e^(+z t). A valid discount factor is always between 0 and 1 for positive rates.

**Q8.** With an upward-sloping (rising) spot curve, how does the par rate at maturity T compare to the T-year spot rate?
- A. The par rate is above the T-year spot rate.
- B. The par rate is below the T-year spot rate.
- C. The par rate equals the T-year spot rate.
- D. The par rate is above the T-year forward rate.

**Answer: B**

The question asks how par and spot rates rank when the curve is rising.

On an upward-sloping curve the ordering is:

$$\text{forward} > \text{spot} > \text{par}$$

So the par rate is BELOW the T-year spot rate.

Why? A par (coupon) bond pays coupons along the way, and those early coupons are discounted at the EARLIER, LOWER spot rates. So the par rate is a blend that gets pulled down by those lower early rates. A zero-coupon bond, by contrast, feels only the final (highest) spot rate. So par < spot when the curve rises.

Analogy: Your overall grade (par rate) is an average of all your tests; if your early tests were lower, the average is pulled below your best (final) test score (the spot rate).

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Above would be the case for a FALLING curve, not a rising one.
- C: They are equal only when the curve is FLAT.
- D: On a rising curve the forward is the HIGHEST of the three, so par is below the forward, not above it.

📌 Exam tip: Rising curve -> forward > spot > par. Falling curve -> forward < spot < par. Flat -> all equal. Par is pulled toward earlier, lower rates by its coupons.

**Q9.** Discount factors are d(0.5) = 0.9852 and d(1.0) = 0.9690. The 1-year par rate (semiannual coupon) is closest to:
- A. 3.17%
- B. 6.20%
- C. 1.59%
- D. 3.10%

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the par rate - the coupon rate that makes a 1-year bond price exactly 100 - from the discount factors.

The par rate formula (semiannual) is:

$$c(T) = \frac{2\left(1 - d(T)\right)}{A(T)}$$

where A(T) is the annuity factor = the sum of the discount factors at each coupon date.

Step by step:
- Annuity factor: $$A(1) = d(0.5) + d(1.0) = 0.9852 + 0.9690 = 1.9542$$
- Numerator: $$2 \times (1 - d(1.0)) = 2 \times (1 - 0.9690) = 2 \times 0.0310 = 0.0620$$
- $$c(1) = \frac{0.0620}{1.9542} = 0.03173 = 3.17\%$$

So the 1-year par rate is about 3.17%.

Check: a bond with a 3.17% semiannual coupon (so 1.586 each half-year) should price at 100:
$$1.586 \times 0.9852 + 101.586 \times 0.9690 = 1.563 + 98.437 = 100.00$$ ✓

Why the others are wrong:
- B (6.20%): Forgot to divide by the annuity factor (used 2 x 0.031 = 0.062 directly as a percent).
- C (1.59%): That's the half-yearly coupon rate, not the annual par rate (forgot to multiply by 2 / mixed up).
- D (3.10%): Used 2(1 - d) without dividing by A, or rounded incorrectly.

📌 Exam tip: Par rate = 2(1 - d(T)) / (sum of discount factors). The "2" comes from semiannual compounding. Always divide by the FULL annuity factor (sum of ALL discount factors), not just the last one.

**Q10.** Forward rates derived from today's spot curve are best interpreted as:
- A. Accurate forecasts of where future spot rates will actually be.
- B. Break-even (no-arbitrage) rates that need not equal realized future spot rates.
- C. The same thing as par rates.
- D. Always lower than the corresponding spot rates.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what a forward rate actually represents.

A forward rate is the rate you can LOCK IN TODAY for borrowing or lending over a future period, set so there is no arbitrage between investing long versus rolling over short investments. It is a break-even rate - the rate that makes the two strategies equal today. It is NOT a forecast: the spot rate that actually shows up in the future can be higher or lower.

Analogy: If a shop offers to fix next year's price for you today, that locked price is a break-even deal based on current conditions - it doesn't promise that next year's actual market price will match it.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: This is the classic trap - forwards are break-even rates, NOT predictions. Realized spots often differ.
- C: Par rates are coupon rates that price a bond at 100 - a different concept.
- D: On a rising curve, forwards are HIGHER than spots, so "always lower" is false.

📌 Exam tip: Forward rates = break-even / no-arbitrage rates, NOT forecasts. Realized future spot rates need not equal them - GARP loves this distinction.
