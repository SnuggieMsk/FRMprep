**Q1.** A stock trades at \$50. In one year it will be either \$60 or \$45. The risk-free rate is 5% (continuously compounded). The risk-neutral probability of an up move is closest to:
- A. 0.504
- B. 0.333
- C. 0.546
- D. 0.500

**Answer: A**

The question asks: compute the risk-neutral up-probability p from the given stock moves.

Step 1 — find u and d from the prices:
$$u = \frac{60}{50} = 1.2, \qquad d = \frac{45}{50} = 0.9$$

Step 2 — apply the risk-neutral probability formula ($$\Delta t = 1$$ year):
$$p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d} = \frac{e^{0.05} - 0.9}{1.2 - 0.9}$$

Step 3 — compute the pieces: $$e^{0.05} = 1.05127$$.
$$p = \frac{1.05127 - 0.9}{0.3} = \frac{0.15127}{0.3} = 0.504$$

- B (0.333) ignores the growth factor and just uses something like $$(1-d)/(u-d)$$ wrongly, or other miscalculation.
- C (0.546) likely uses $$e^{0.05} = 1.05$$ rounding combined with an arithmetic slip, or wrong u/d.
- D (0.500) is the naive "50/50" guess — but the risk-neutral probability is NOT a coin flip.

Analogy: p is the "tilt" of the dice in a make-believe world where the stock is expected to grow exactly at the risk-free rate. It is engineered to make the math work, not a real forecast.

📌 Exam tip: $$p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$$. Get u and d straight from the up/down prices when they're given — no volatility formula needed.

**Q2.** A stock has volatility σ = 30%, and a binomial tree uses monthly steps ($$\Delta t = 1/12$$). The up-factor u is closest to:
- A. 1.090
- B. 1.300
- C. 1.025
- D. 1.350

**Answer: A**

The question asks: calibrate the up-factor u to volatility using the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein formula.

The formula is:
$$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$$

Step 1 — compute $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$:
$$\sqrt{1/12} = \sqrt{0.08333} = 0.2887$$

Step 2 — multiply by σ:
$$0.30 \times 0.2887 = 0.08660$$

Step 3 — exponentiate:
$$u = e^{0.08660} = 1.0905$$

- B (1.300) forgets the $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$ and uses $$e^{0.30} \approx 1.35$$, or just adds 0.30 to 1.
- C (1.025) uses too small an exponent — perhaps $$0.30 \times (1/12)$$ without the square root.
- D (1.350) is $$e^{0.30}$$ — the annual figure, ignoring that each step is only one month.

Analogy: a one-month move should be smaller than a one-year move. The square root of time shrinks the volatility down to the size of a single short step.

📌 Exam tip: $$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$$ and $$d = 1/u$$. The most common error is dropping the $$\sqrt{\Delta t}$$. Volatility scales with the SQUARE ROOT of time.

**Q3.** In a binomial tree for an option on a FUTURES contract, the risk-neutral probability of an up move is:
- A. $$(e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$$
- B. $$(1 - d)/(u - d)$$
- C. $$(e^{(r-q)\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$$
- D. $$(e^{-r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)$$

**Answer: B**

The question asks: which growth factor goes in the numerator for a futures option?

Entering a futures contract costs nothing (no cash outlay), so in the risk-neutral world a futures price has ZERO drift — its growth factor is 1, not $$e^{r\Delta t}$$. Plugging growth = 1 into the template $$p = \frac{e^{(\text{growth})\Delta t} - d}{u - d}$$ gives:
$$p = \frac{1 - d}{u - d}$$

- A is the formula for a non-dividend STOCK (growth = r), not a futures.
- C is for a stock index with dividend yield q (growth = r − q).
- D mistakenly uses $$e^{-r\Delta t}$$ (a discount factor) in the numerator — that's not a growth factor.

Analogy: a stock you buy ties up cash that should earn r, so it must drift at r. A futures position ties up no cash, so there's nothing it needs to "make up" — its expected drift is zero.

📌 Exam tip: Use the template $$p = \frac{e^{(\text{growth})\Delta t} - d}{u - d}$$ with growth = r (stock), r − q (index), r − $$r_f$$ (currency), or 0 (futures). Discounting is ALWAYS at r.

**Q4.** At a node in a binomial tree, an American put has an intrinsic (exercise) value of 8.20 and a continuation value of 7.60. The value used at that node in backward induction is:
- A. 7.60
- B. 8.20
- C. 7.90
- D. 0.60

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how do you value an American option at each node?

An American option can be exercised at any time, so at each node you take the LARGER of exercising now (intrinsic value) versus holding (continuation value):
$$f_{node} = \max(\text{intrinsic}, \text{continuation}) = \max(8.20, 7.60) = 8.20$$

Here exercising (8.20) beats waiting (7.60), so the holder exercises and the node value is 8.20.

- A (7.60) is the continuation value — what you'd use only if it were larger, but it isn't.
- C (7.90) is the average of the two — never the right rule; you take the max, not the average.
- D (0.60) is the difference — also not the rule.

Analogy: at each step you ask, "Is the cash I'd get by exercising now bigger than the value of keeping my option alive?" You always pocket the bigger amount.

📌 Exam tip: American option = max(intrinsic, continuation) at EVERY non-terminal node, including the root. This early-exercise check is what makes American options worth more than European ones.

**Q5.** Using a one-step tree with $$S_0 = 100$$, $$u = 1.1$$, $$d = 0.9$$, $$r = 6\%$$ (continuous), and $$\Delta t = 0.5$$, the risk-neutral up-probability is closest to:
- A. 0.500
- B. 0.652
- C. 0.348
- D. 0.700

**Answer: B**

The question asks: compute p for the given one-step tree.

Step 1 — growth factor over the half-year:
$$e^{r\Delta t} = e^{0.06 \times 0.5} = e^{0.03} = 1.030455$$

Step 2 — apply the formula:
$$p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d} = \frac{1.030455 - 0.9}{1.1 - 0.9} = \frac{0.130455}{0.2} = 0.6523$$

- A (0.500) is the naive coin-flip guess.
- C (0.348) is $$1 - p$$ — the DOWN probability, not the up probability.
- D (0.700) is a rounding/setup slip.

Analogy: think of p as how much the tree must "lean up" so that, on average, the stock grows at the risk-free rate over the half-year.

📌 Exam tip: Don't forget $$\Delta t$$ inside the exponent — here it's 0.5, so use $$e^{0.03}$$, not $$e^{0.06}$$. And remember $$1 - p$$ is the down-probability.

**Q6.** Using $$S_0 = 100$$, $$u = 1.1$$, $$d = 0.9$$, $$r = 6\%$$, $$\Delta t = 0.5$$, $$p = 0.6523$$, the value of a two-step European call with strike K = 100 is closest to:
- A. \$13.29
- B. \$8.41
- C. \$21.00
- D. \$6.85

**Answer: B**

The question asks: value a two-step European call by backward induction.

Step 1 — terminal stock prices and payoffs ($$\max(S - 100, 0)$$):
- $$S_{uu} = 100 \times 1.1 \times 1.1 = 121 \to$$ payoff 21
- $$S_{ud} = 100 \times 1.1 \times 0.9 = 99 \to$$ payoff 0
- $$S_{dd} = 100 \times 0.9 \times 0.9 = 81 \to$$ payoff 0

Step 2 — discount one step. Discount factor $$e^{-r\Delta t} = e^{-0.03} = 0.97045$$.

Up node: $$f_u = 0.97045 \times (0.6523 \times 21 + 0.3477 \times 0) = 0.97045 \times 13.698 = 13.293$$
Down node: $$f_d = 0.97045 \times (0.6523 \times 0 + 0.3477 \times 0) = 0$$

Step 3 — roll back to root:
$$f = 0.97045 \times (0.6523 \times 13.293 + 0.3477 \times 0) = 0.97045 \times 8.671 = \$8.41$$

- A (\$13.29) is the value at the UP node only ($$f_u$$), not the root — it stops one step too early.
- C (\$21.00) is the undiscounted top payoff — no probability weighting, no discounting.
- D (\$6.85) results from a discounting or probability error.

Analogy: you work backward like rewinding a film — start from the endings (payoffs), and at each earlier scene take the discounted expected value until you reach the start (today).

📌 Exam tip: For a EUROPEAN option you can shortcut: $$f = e^{-2r\Delta t} \times p^2 \times 21 = e^{-0.06} \times 0.4255 \times 21 = 8.41$$. Same answer, faster.

**Q7.** As the number of steps in a binomial tree increases (with u and d calibrated to volatility), the price of a European option:
- A. Diverges away from the Black-Scholes-Merton price
- B. Converges to the Black-Scholes-Merton price
- C. Stays constant regardless of the number of steps
- D. Becomes increasingly inaccurate

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what happens to the binomial European price as steps increase?

As the number of steps grows (so $$\Delta t \to 0$$), with u and d calibrated to σ, the binomial price of a European option converges to the Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) closed-form price. In practice, around 30+ steps already gives a close answer.

- A is the opposite of what happens.
- C is false; few-step trees are crude approximations that refine as steps increase.
- D is the reverse — more steps means MORE accuracy.

Analogy: a binomial tree is like a staircase approximating a smooth ramp (BSM). The more (smaller) steps you use, the closer the staircase hugs the smooth ramp.

📌 Exam tip: Binomial → BSM as steps → ∞ for European options. Trees remain essential for AMERICAN options precisely because BSM has no closed form for them.

**Q8.** A binomial tree uses $$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$$ and $$d = 1/u$$. A key advantage of setting $$d = 1/u$$ is that:
- A. It guarantees the option is always in the money
- B. The tree recombines, so an n-step tree has n+1 terminal nodes instead of 2ⁿ
- C. The risk-neutral probability becomes exactly 0.5
- D. It eliminates the need to discount cash flows

**Answer: B**

The question asks: why is choosing $$d = 1/u$$ useful?

When $$d = 1/u$$, an up-then-down move ($$u \times d = u \times 1/u = 1$$) lands on the SAME stock price as a down-then-up move. The paths "recombine," so the tree collapses into a neat lattice with only n+1 terminal nodes after n steps, instead of $$2^n$$ separate paths. This makes computation vastly more manageable.

- A is nonsense; the calibration says nothing about moneyness.
- C is false; p depends on r, u, and d, and is generally not 0.5.
- D is false; you still discount at the risk-free rate.

Analogy: it's like a grid of streets where going "right then left" puts you on the same corner as "left then right" — far fewer corners to track than if every path led somewhere new.

📌 Exam tip: $$d = 1/u$$ → recombining tree → n+1 terminal nodes, not $$2^n$$. This is the computational reason the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein parameterization is so popular.

**Q9.** Consider a one-step tree where a stock at $$S_0$$ goes to $$S_0 u$$ (option payoff $$f_u$$) or $$S_0 d$$ (payoff $$f_d$$). The number of shares Δ in the riskless replicating portfolio is:
- A. $$\dfrac{f_u - f_d}{S_0 u - S_0 d}$$
- B. $$\dfrac{f_u + f_d}{S_0 u + S_0 d}$$
- C. $$\dfrac{S_0 u - S_0 d}{f_u - f_d}$$
- D. $$\dfrac{f_u - f_d}{u + d}$$

**Answer: A**

The question asks: what is the hedge ratio (delta) that makes a stock-plus-option portfolio riskless?

To build a riskless portfolio, you choose Δ so the portfolio has the SAME value whether the stock goes up or down. Solving that equality gives:
$$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_0 u - S_0 d}$$
This is the change in option value divided by the change in stock value across the two states.

- B adds instead of subtracts — but we need the DIFFERENCE between states to cancel risk.
- C is the formula upside-down (stock change over option change).
- D drops the $$S_0$$ in the denominator — wrong units.

Analogy: Δ is "how many shares move in step with one option." Matching that ratio makes the up-and-down swings cancel out, leaving a position that's risk-free.

📌 Exam tip: $$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_0 u - S_0 d}$$ (change in option ÷ change in stock). This replicating delta is the binomial ancestor of the BSM hedge ratio $$N(d_1)$$.

**Q10.** Why are the real-world probabilities of an up or down move irrelevant when pricing an option on a binomial tree?
- A. Because up and down moves are always equally likely
- B. Because the price comes from no-arbitrage replication, in which risk preferences and real-world probabilities cancel out
- C. Because the risk-free rate is zero
- D. Because options always expire worthless

**Answer: B**

The question asks: why don't actual up/down probabilities affect the option price?

The option price is found by building a riskless replicating portfolio (stock + option) that must earn the risk-free rate to avoid arbitrage. This argument never uses the real-world probability of an up move — investors' risk preferences and the true probabilities simply cancel out. We then repackage the math using the risk-neutral probability p, which is a pricing device, not a forecast.

- A is false; real-world up and down moves are generally NOT equally likely, yet the price still doesn't depend on them.
- C is false; r is generally not zero and is essential to the pricing.
- D is nonsense.

Analogy: pricing by replication is like figuring out the cost of a recipe from its ingredients. It doesn't matter how LIKELY you are to feel like cooking — the cost is set by what it takes to reproduce the dish.

📌 Exam tip: No-arbitrage pricing depends on u, d, and r — not on real-world probabilities or risk premia. The risk-neutral probability p is a calculation tool, not a prediction of the future.
