**Q1.** A bank's credit card business loses, on average, about 4% of its balances each year to defaults, and the bank builds this 4% into the interest rate it charges. The 4% is best described as:
- A. Unexpected loss
- B. Expected loss
- C. Economic capital
- D. Operational risk

**Answer: B**

The question is really asking: what do we call a loss that is average, predictable, and already priced into the product? That is the definition of expected loss (EL). EL is the average loss the firm anticipates over a period — a normal cost of doing business — so it is priced into rates and covered by reserves/provisions, not by capital.

Think of it like a grocery store that knows about 4 out of every 100 apples will rot before being sold. The store builds that spoilage into its prices. It is expected, so it is just a cost.

- A is wrong: unexpected loss is the variability around that average (e.g., a recession year where losses jump to 9%). The steady, priced-in 4% is not the surprise part.
- C is wrong: economic capital is the buffer held to absorb unexpected losses, not the average loss itself.
- D is wrong: operational risk is loss from failed people, processes, systems, or external events (fraud, IT failure) — credit card defaults are credit risk, not operational risk.

📌 Exam tip: If a loss is average and priced-in, it is EXPECTED loss (a cost). Only the surprise/variability part is true "risk" and is covered by capital.

**Q2.** Which scenario best illustrates *funding* liquidity risk?
- A. A hedge fund must sell a large block of a thinly traded stock at a steep discount
- B. A bank cannot roll over its maturing commercial paper to raise cash
- C. A dealer widens its bid-ask spreads during volatile markets
- D. A bond portfolio falls in value because interest rates rose

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to spot the type of liquidity risk where a firm cannot get the cash it needs to meet its obligations. That is funding liquidity risk. When a bank "rolls over" commercial paper, it issues new short-term IOUs to pay off the old ones; if it cannot do that, it runs short of cash — a funding problem.

Imagine you pay off one credit card by borrowing on another. If no one will lend to you anymore, you are stuck even if you own valuable things — that is a funding squeeze.

- A is wrong: being forced to dump an asset at a fire-sale price is *market (trading) liquidity* risk — the problem is the market for the asset, not raising cash.
- C is wrong: wider bid-ask spreads are also a sign of *market liquidity* risk.
- D is wrong: losing value because rates moved is interest rate risk, a type of market risk.

📌 Exam tip: Funding liquidity = "can't raise cash to pay what I owe." Market/trading liquidity = "can't sell my asset without crashing its price." GARP loves making you tell these two apart.

**Q3.** Risk management is best described as:
- A. Minimizing the firm's total risk
- B. Eliminating every exposure that could produce a loss
- C. Deliberately selecting and sizing the risks the firm is paid to bear, within its risk appetite
- D. Buying insurance on all material exposures

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the correct definition of what risk management actually is. A firm earns returns only by taking risk, so risk management is not about removing risk — it is about taking the *right* risks, in the *right* amount, on purpose, and keeping them within a chosen risk appetite.

It is like driving a car: the goal is not to never move (that earns you nothing), it is to drive purposefully while staying within safe limits.

- A is wrong: minimizing total risk would also minimize return; a firm that takes almost no risk cannot earn its cost of capital.
- B is wrong: eliminating all loss-producing exposures is impossible and would mean exiting the business entirely.
- D is wrong: transferring (insuring) risk is only one tool — and you would never insure risks you are actually paid to keep.

📌 Exam tip: Risk management is about *deliberate risk selection within appetite*, NOT minimization or elimination. A firm that "never loses money" is probably not taking enough risk.

**Q4.** A trader receives a large bonus when trades make a profit but suffers no penalty when trades lose money. The incentive problem this creates is best described as:
- A. Adverse selection
- B. Moral hazard
- C. Basis risk
- D. Funding liquidity risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks which problem arises when someone is shielded from the downside of their own risk-taking. That is moral hazard: once a person is protected from losses (heads I win, tails the firm loses), they are tempted to take on too much risk.

It is like lending a friend your car with no consequences if they crash it — they will probably drive more recklessly than if it were their own car.

- A is wrong: adverse selection happens *before* a deal, where the riskiest customers are the keenest to buy (e.g., the sickest people want insurance most). Here the issue is behavior *after* the bonus scheme is in place.
- C is wrong: basis risk is when a hedge doesn't perfectly track the thing it is hedging — unrelated to bonuses.
- D is wrong: funding liquidity risk is about raising cash, not incentives.

📌 Exam tip: Moral hazard = protected party takes MORE risk because they don't bear the downside. Adverse selection = the riskiest types self-select INTO a deal beforehand. Fix moral hazard with clawbacks and deferred pay.

**Q5.** Which of the following is the credit-risk formula for expected loss?
- A. $$EL = PD + LGD + EAD$$
- B. $$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$
- C. $$EL = PD \times (1 - LGD) \times EAD$$
- D. $$EL = \dfrac{PD \times EAD}{LGD}$$

**Answer: B**

The question tests whether you know the standard credit expected-loss formula. Expected loss equals the probability of default times the loss given default times the exposure at default:

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$

In plain words: (chance the borrower defaults) × (fraction you lose if they do) × (amount owed when they default). For example, if there is a 2% chance of default, you lose 40% when it happens, and the exposure is $1,000,000:

$$EL = 0.02 \times 0.40 \times \$1{,}000{,}000 = \$8{,}000$$

- A is wrong: you multiply these three drivers, you do not add them (adding gives a meaningless number).
- C is wrong: LGD is already the loss fraction (LGD = 1 − recovery rate). Writing (1 − LGD) would give you the recovery, not the loss.
- D is wrong: dividing by LGD makes no economic sense — a bigger loss fraction would shrink your expected loss, which is backwards.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize EL = PD × LGD × EAD (all multiplied). And remember LGD = 1 − recovery rate, so don't accidentally use the recovery rate.

**Q6.** Compared with Value-at-Risk (VaR), expected shortfall (ES) is better at capturing:
- A. The most likely day-to-day profit
- B. The average severity of losses in the tail (beyond the VaR threshold)
- C. The firm's total notional exposure
- D. The recovery rate on defaulted loans

**Answer: B**

The question asks what advantage ES has over VaR. VaR tells you a loss threshold that won't be exceeded at a given confidence (e.g., "we won't lose more than $10m on 95% of days") — but it says nothing about how bad things get on the worst 5% of days. Expected shortfall averages those tail losses, so it captures tail severity that VaR ignores.

Analogy: VaR tells you "the river won't rise above the 10-foot mark 95% of the time." ES tells you "but when it does flood, the average flood is 14 feet" — the part that actually does the damage.

- A is wrong: neither VaR nor ES is about the most likely (typical) outcome; both focus on the bad tail.
- C is wrong: notional exposure is a raw size number, unrelated to tail loss measurement.
- D is wrong: recovery rate is a credit input, not what ES measures.

📌 Exam tip: VaR = a threshold ("how bad, at minimum, on a bad day"). ES = the average of losses *beyond* that threshold ("how bad on average when it's worse than VaR"). ES sees the tail; VaR does not.

**Q7.** A bank holds a large loan to one client and worries about losing money if that client fails to repay. This concern relates most directly to which class of risk?
- A. Market risk
- B. Operational risk
- C. Credit risk
- D. Reputation risk

**Answer: C**

The question asks which risk class covers losses from a counterparty failing to pay what it owes. That is credit risk by definition — loss from a borrower or counterparty failing to perform. Default risk, downgrade risk, and concentration risk all sit inside the credit risk family.

- A is wrong: market risk is loss from moving prices/rates (equities, FX, commodities), not from a borrower defaulting.
- B is wrong: operational risk is loss from failed people, processes, systems, or external events (fraud, IT outages).
- D is wrong: reputation risk is loss of franchise value from damaged trust, usually a *consequence* of another event — not the direct default exposure here.

📌 Exam tip: "Counterparty fails to pay" = credit risk. Watch for the word "default." Heavy exposure to one name adds *concentration* risk, still within credit risk.

**Q8.** During a market crisis, market losses trigger margin calls, which force fire-sales, which cause further market losses. This sequence best illustrates that risks:
- A. Are perfectly independent of one another
- B. Interact and can amplify each other rather than simply adding up
- C. Always cancel out through diversification
- D. Can be aggregated by simply summing each silo's number

**Answer: B**

The question is about how different risk types behave together. The described loss spiral (market loss → funding/margin pressure → forced selling → more market loss) shows that risks interact and feed on each other; they do not just sit side by side and add up neatly.

Think of dominoes: knocking one over (market loss) topples the next (liquidity), which topples the next (more market loss). The total damage is bigger than any single domino.

- A is wrong: the whole scenario shows the risks are linked, not independent.
- C is wrong: diversification benefits often *shrink* in a crisis as correlations rise toward 1 — they don't reliably cancel risks out when you most need them.
- D is wrong: simply summing silo numbers ignores these interactions and the way correlations shift, so it misstates firm-wide risk.

📌 Exam tip: In aggregation, correlations are unstable and rise toward 1 in crises. Risks *interact* (and amplify), so you cannot just add up the silos.

**Q9.** A firm decides to keep a particular risk because it is core to its business and the firm has genuine expertise in assessing it, while holding capital and reserves against it. This strategy is best described as:
- A. Avoid
- B. Transfer
- C. Accept (retain)
- D. Diversify away

**Answer: C**

The question describes a firm choosing to hold on to a risk it understands well and is paid to bear, backing it with capital. That is the accept (retain) strategy: keep the risk, price it, and hold a buffer against it.

A bank keeping the credit risk on loans it can expertly assess is the classic example — it earns the spread for taking that risk knowingly.

- A is wrong: "avoid" means exiting or never entering the activity — but here the firm wants to stay in because it has an edge.
- B is wrong: "transfer" means shifting the risk to a third party (insurance, derivatives) — the opposite of keeping it.
- D is wrong: diversification is a *mitigation* technique to reduce risk you still hold; the scenario describes a deliberate decision to retain a core risk, not to spread it across many positions.

📌 Exam tip: The four strategies are Accept, Avoid, Mitigate, Transfer. Choose Accept when the risk is core and the firm is *paid* to bear it and has an edge in assessing it.

**Q10.** Which statement about expected loss (EL) and unexpected loss (UL) is correct?
- A. EL is covered by capital, while UL is priced into products
- B. UL is the predictable cost of doing business, while EL is the genuine risk
- C. EL is priced into products, while UL is the genuine risk covered by capital
- D. EL and UL are the same thing measured over different horizons

**Answer: C**

The question tests whether you can correctly pair each loss type with how it is covered. Expected loss is the average, predictable cost — it is priced into products and held against by reserves. Unexpected loss is the variability of loss around that average — the true economic risk — and it is absorbed by capital.

Picture a budget: EL is the bill you expect every month (rent), so you plan for it. UL is the surprise emergency expense, so you keep a savings buffer (capital) for it.

- A is wrong: it swaps the two — EL is priced in, UL is covered by capital, not the reverse.
- B is wrong: it reverses the definitions; EL is the predictable cost, UL is the genuine risk.
- D is wrong: they are fundamentally different concepts (average vs. variability), not the same thing on different horizons.

📌 Exam tip: EL = average, priced-in, covered by reserves. UL = the surprise, the real risk, covered by capital. Also note UL rises with the *correlation* between defaults, not just individual PDs.
