**Q1.** A bank expects to lose about 2% of its loan book in a normal year, and it builds this into the interest rate it charges. This 2% is best described as:
- A. Unexpected loss
- B. Expected loss
- C. Economic capital
- D. Stress loss

**Answer: B**

Think of a shopkeeper who knows that, on average, 2 out of every 100 apples go bad before they sell. A smart shopkeeper just adds a tiny bit to every apple's price to cover those 2 bad apples. That planned-for, average loss is the **expected loss (EL)**.

- The bank *expects* the 2% loss, so it is not a surprise. It covers it by **pricing it into the loan** (charging a little extra interest) and setting aside **provisions/reserves**.
- **Unexpected loss (A)** is the loss *above* the average in a bad year — the surprise part. That is what **capital** is for, which also rules out **(C) economic capital** (capital answers unexpected loss, not the average).
- **(D) Stress loss** is an extreme what-if scenario, far bigger than the everyday 2%.

Exam tip: the word "average" or "builds it into the price" almost always signals **expected loss**.

**Q2.** Which of the following is the clearest example of *funding* liquidity risk?
- A. A trader cannot sell a bond without dropping the price a lot
- B. A bank cannot roll over the short-term borrowing it relies on
- C. A stock price falls after bad earnings
- D. Two currencies move apart unexpectedly

**Answer: B**

There are two "liquidity" risks and the exam loves to test which is which.

- **Funding liquidity risk** = you cannot get the **cash** you need to pay what you owe right now. A bank that borrows short-term and suddenly *cannot borrow again* ("roll over") is the textbook case — that is **(B)**.
- **Market (trading) liquidity risk** = you own something but cannot **sell it** without crashing its price. That is **(A)** — a real risk, but a *different* one.
- **(C)** is plain market risk (prices move), and **(D)** is a kind of market/basis risk. Neither is about getting cash to meet obligations.

Memory hook: **Funding = can I raise cash?** · **Market liquidity = can I sell my stuff?**
