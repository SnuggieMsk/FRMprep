**Q1.** A portfolio's daily profit and loss is normally distributed with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of $2,000,000. Using $z_{0.95} = 1.645$, the 95% one-day value at risk (VaR) is closest to:
- A. $1,960,000
- B. $3,290,000
- C. $4,652,000
- D. $2,000,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the loss level that will not be exceeded on 95 out of 100 normal days. For a normal P&L with mean zero, VaR is just the z-score times the dollar standard deviation.

$$\text{VaR}_\alpha = z_\alpha \times \sigma$$

Plug in the numbers:

$$\text{VaR}_{0.95} = 1.645 \times \$2{,}000{,}000 = \$3{,}290{,}000$$

So B is correct.

- A ($1,960,000) uses $z = 1.96$, which is the two-sided 95% value, not the one-sided 95% VaR multiplier (1.645). Trap: mixing up one-tailed and two-tailed z-scores.
- C ($4,652,000) uses $z = 2.326$, which is the 99% multiplier, not 95%.
- D ($2,000,000) is just the standard deviation with no z-multiplier (as if $z = 1$).

Think of VaR like a flood wall: you build it so the river stays below it 95 days out of 100. The taller your confidence, the taller the wall (bigger z).

📌 Exam tip: Memorize $z_{0.95} = 1.645$ and $z_{0.99} = 2.326$. With mean zero, VaR = z × σ — nothing more.

**Q2.** Which of the following best describes the value at risk (VaR) of a portfolio?
- A. The maximum possible loss the portfolio can suffer
- B. The average loss the portfolio suffers over a year
- C. A loss level that will not be exceeded with a given probability over a given horizon
- D. The loss that occurs in the single worst historical scenario

**Answer: C**

The question is testing the precise definition of VaR.

VaR is a quantile of the loss distribution: at confidence level $\alpha$ over horizon $T$, it is the loss such that $P(L > \text{VaR}_\alpha) = 1 - \alpha$. In words, "we are 99% confident the loss won't be worse than this." C states exactly that.

- A is the classic trap. VaR is NOT the maximum loss; it puts no ceiling on how bad the worst day can be. A 99% one-day VaR of $10M means losses exceed $10M about 1 day in 100 — but says nothing about how far beyond $10M they go.
- B describes an average (more like expected loss), not a quantile.
- D describes the single worst scenario, which is again about the extreme tail, not the cutoff quantile.

Analogy: VaR is like saying "I'm 99% sure my commute won't take more than 40 minutes." It doesn't tell you whether the rare bad day takes 41 minutes or 3 hours.

📌 Exam tip: If an answer says "maximum loss," it is almost always the wrong choice for a VaR-definition question.

**Q3.** Which property of a coherent risk measure does VaR fail to satisfy in general?
- A. Monotonicity
- B. Translation invariance
- C. Positive homogeneity
- D. Subadditivity

**Answer: D**

The question asks which of the four coherence properties VaR breaks.

A coherent risk measure must satisfy monotonicity, subadditivity, positive homogeneity, and translation invariance. VaR satisfies three of them but can fail subadditivity when distributions are not elliptical (not normal). Subadditivity says $\rho(X_1 + X_2) \le \rho(X_1) + \rho(X_2)$ — merging two portfolios should never increase risk, because diversification helps. VaR can violate this.

- A (monotonicity) — VaR satisfies this: a portfolio that always pays less is at least as risky.
- B (translation invariance) — VaR satisfies this: adding cash reduces risk one-for-one.
- C (positive homogeneity) — VaR satisfies this: doubling the position doubles the VaR.

Analogy: subadditivity is the idea "don't put all your eggs in one basket helps you." A broken risk measure (VaR) can sometimes claim that spreading eggs across baskets is riskier — which is nonsense, and that's the flaw.

📌 Exam tip: "Which property does VaR violate?" → always subadditivity. ES fixes this and is fully coherent.

**Q4.** For the same portfolio and the same confidence level, expected shortfall (ES) compared with VaR is:
- A. Always smaller than VaR
- B. Always at least as large as VaR
- C. Equal to VaR for normal distributions
- D. Unrelated to VaR

**Answer: B**

The question asks how ES and VaR rank against each other.

ES is the average of all losses that are worse than VaR: $\text{ES}_\alpha = E[L \mid L > \text{VaR}_\alpha]$. Since ES averages outcomes that are all at least as bad as VaR, it can never be smaller than VaR. Therefore ES ≥ VaR always.

- A is backwards — ES is never smaller.
- C is wrong: even for the normal distribution, ES > VaR (e.g., at 95%, ES multiplier 2.06 vs VaR multiplier 1.645).
- D is wrong: ES is defined directly in terms of the tail beyond VaR, so they are closely related.

Analogy: VaR tells you "the cutoff for a 'bad day' is a $1M loss." ES tells you "on those bad days, the average loss is $1.4M." The average of the bad days can't be milder than the cutoff that defines them.

📌 Exam tip: Remember the one-liner: VaR says "how often," ES says "how bad." And ES ≥ VaR, always.

**Q5.** The mean-variance framework produces correct risk rankings for risk-averse investors when returns are:
- A. Negatively skewed
- B. Fat-tailed with excess kurtosis
- C. Elliptically distributed (e.g., normal)
- D. From short option positions

**Answer: C**

The question asks when ranking portfolios by just mean and standard deviation is valid.

If returns are elliptically distributed (the normal distribution is the key example), the entire distribution is fully described by its mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Then ranking by mean and variance is consistent with expected utility for any risk-averse investor. C is correct.

- A (negative skew) breaks the framework: skew is a third feature that $\mu$ and $\sigma$ cannot capture.
- B (fat tails / excess kurtosis) breaks it: σ understates true tail risk.
- D (short options) produces non-linear, skewed payoffs, so the distribution is not described by mean and variance alone.

Analogy: describing a person by only height and weight works fine if everyone has the same body shape (elliptical). Once shapes differ (skew/fat tails), two people with identical height and weight can be very different — you need more numbers.

📌 Exam tip: Mean-variance only "sees" mean and σ. If a distribution has skew or fat tails, the framework is blind to the extra risk.

**Q6.** A $20 million portfolio has daily P&L that is normal with mean zero and daily volatility of 1.5%. Using $z_{0.99} = 2.326$, the 99% one-day VaR is closest to:
- A. $300,000
- B. $493,500
- C. $698,000
- D. $465,000

**Answer: C**

The question gives volatility as a percentage and asks for dollar VaR at 99%.

Step 1 — convert percentage volatility to a dollar standard deviation:

$$\sigma_{\$} = 0.015 \times \$20{,}000{,}000 = \$300{,}000$$

Step 2 — apply the VaR formula (mean zero):

$$\text{VaR}_{0.99} = 2.326 \times \$300{,}000 = \$698{,}000$$

So C is correct.

- A ($300,000) stops at the dollar standard deviation and forgets to multiply by the z-score.
- B ($493,500) uses $z = 1.645$ (the 95% multiplier) instead of 2.326.
- D ($465,000) uses $z = 1.55$ or similar mis-read; it does not use the correct 99% multiplier.

Think of it as two steps: turn the percent into dollars, then stretch by the confidence multiplier.

📌 Exam tip: Always convert % volatility to dollars first (σ × portfolio value), then multiply by z. Don't forget the z-step.

**Q7.** Expected shortfall is best described as:
- A. The largest loss that occurs with 1% probability
- B. The expected loss given that the loss exceeds the VaR threshold
- C. The standard deviation of losses in the tail
- D. The probability that a loss exceeds VaR

**Answer: B**

The question tests the definition of expected shortfall (ES).

ES (also called conditional VaR or expected tail loss) answers: "Given that we are having a bad enough day that the loss is beyond VaR, what is the average loss?" Formally $\text{ES}_\alpha = E[L \mid L > \text{VaR}_\alpha]$. B captures this exactly.

- A describes VaR-like cutoff thinking, not the average of the tail.
- C describes a standard deviation (a spread measure), not an expected (mean) value.
- D describes a probability $(1 - \alpha)$, not a dollar loss amount.

Analogy: VaR is the "entry ticket" price into the bad-days club (the cutoff). ES is the average severity of everyone already inside the club.

📌 Exam tip: ES = average of the losses beyond VaR. Watch for distractors that describe a probability or a standard deviation instead of an average loss.

**Q8.** A $10 million portfolio has daily P&L that is normal with mean zero and daily volatility 2%. Given $\phi(1.645) = 0.1031$, the 95% expected shortfall (ES) is closest to:
- A. $329,000
- B. $412,540
- C. $200,000
- D. $533,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the parametric (normal) 95% ES in dollars.

Step 1 — dollar standard deviation:

$$\sigma_{\$} = 0.02 \times \$10{,}000{,}000 = \$200{,}000$$

Step 2 — the normal ES formula (mean zero):

$$\text{ES}_\alpha = \sigma \times \frac{\phi(z_\alpha)}{1 - \alpha}$$

Plug in $\phi(1.645) = 0.1031$ and $1 - \alpha = 0.05$:

$$\text{ES}_{0.95} = \$200{,}000 \times \frac{0.1031}{0.05} = \$200{,}000 \times 2.0627 = \$412{,}540$$

So B is correct.

- A ($329,000) is the 95% VaR (1.645 × 200,000), not ES. Remember ES > VaR.
- C ($200,000) is just the dollar standard deviation with no multiplier.
- D ($533,000) is the 99% ES (uses $\phi(2.326)/0.01$), the wrong confidence level.

Step it through slowly: dollars first, then the special ES multiplier (pdf value ÷ tail probability).

📌 Exam tip: The ES multiplier is $\phi(z)/(1-\alpha)$, NOT z. At 95% it's about 2.06; at 99% about 2.67. Check ES > VaR as a sanity test.

**Q9.** Two independent corporate bonds each have a 3% chance of defaulting over the year with a $10 million loss, and otherwise no loss. Considering the 95% one-year VaR, which statement is correct?
- A. VaR of each bond alone is $10M, and the combined VaR is $20M
- B. VaR of each bond alone is $0, but the combined portfolio VaR is $10M, violating subadditivity
- C. Combining the bonds always lowers VaR because of diversification
- D. The combined VaR equals the sum of the individual VaRs

**Answer: B**

The question is the classic counterexample showing VaR is not subadditive.

For one bond: the probability of a loss is 3%, which is less than the 5% tail. So the 95% quantile of the loss distribution is $0 — VaR = $0 for each bond. The sum of the two individual VaRs is $0.

For the portfolio of both: the chance that at least one defaults is

$$1 - (0.97)^2 = 1 - 0.9409 = 0.0591 = 5.91\%$$

Since 5.91% > 5%, the loss at the 95% quantile is now $10M. So:

$$\text{VaR}(A + B) = \$10\text{M} > \text{VaR}(A) + \text{VaR}(B) = \$0$$

Merging the portfolios increased measured VaR — that violates subadditivity. B is correct.

- A is wrong: each bond's 95% VaR is $0 (3% < 5%), not $10M.
- C is wrong: here combining raised VaR, the opposite of the expected diversification effect.
- D is wrong: the combined VaR ($10M) is far above the sum of the parts ($0).

Analogy: it's as if buying two cheap lottery-loss tickets, each "safe" on its own, becomes officially "risky" only when held together — an odd result that exposes VaR's flaw.

📌 Exam tip: This exact two-bond setup, with default probability between the two tail levels, is GARP's go-to example for VaR failing subadditivity.

**Q10.** Which statement about VaR and expected shortfall in regulation and practice is correct?
- A. VaR is coherent, while expected shortfall is not
- B. Expected shortfall is harder to backtest than VaR but captures tail severity better
- C. VaR provides full information about losses beyond its threshold
- D. Expected shortfall is always smaller than VaR for fat-tailed distributions

**Answer: B**

The question compares VaR and ES on coherence, tail information, and backtesting.

ES averages the full tail beyond VaR, so it captures how severe the bad days are — information VaR ignores. The trade-off is that ES is harder to backtest because checking it requires the magnitudes of tail losses, not just counting exceptions. B is correct, which is why the Basel FRTB framework moved to a 97.5% ES while older rules used 99% VaR (easy to backtest by counting exceedances).

- A is reversed: ES is coherent; VaR is not (it can fail subadditivity).
- C is wrong: VaR gives no information about losses beyond its threshold — that is its main limitation.
- D is wrong: ES is always at least as large as VaR, never smaller.

Analogy: counting how many times the river overflowed the wall (VaR exceptions) is easy. Measuring the average depth of every flood (ES) is more work but tells you how damaging the floods really are.

📌 Exam tip: VaR = easy to backtest, ignores tail severity, not coherent. ES = harder to backtest, captures tail severity, coherent. FRTB uses 97.5% ES.
