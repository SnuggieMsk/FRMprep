**Q1.** Under the Basel definition, operational risk is the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. Which of the following is EXCLUDED from this definition?
- A. Legal risk
- B. Cyber-fraud losses
- C. Strategic and reputational risk
- D. Rogue-trader losses

**Answer: C**

The question asks which risk is NOT part of Basel operational risk.

Basel explicitly INCLUDES legal risk but EXCLUDES strategic risk (bad business decisions) and reputational risk (damage to the firm's image). So the answer is C.

Analogy: Operational risk is about things "breaking" in the day-to-day machinery - a fraud, a system crash, a flood. Choosing the wrong business strategy or losing customer trust is a different kind of problem, so Basel leaves those out of the operational-risk box.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Legal risk is explicitly INCLUDED, so it is not excluded.
- B: Cyber-fraud is an operational loss (external fraud / business disruption), so it is included.
- D: Rogue-trader losses are internal fraud - a classic operational risk event - so they are included.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the boundary - legal risk IN; strategic and reputational risk OUT.

**Q2.** In the loss distribution approach (LDA), the frequency and severity of operational losses are most commonly modeled using which two distributions?
- A. Normal frequency and normal severity
- B. Poisson frequency and lognormal severity
- C. Binomial frequency and uniform severity
- D. Lognormal frequency and Poisson severity

**Answer: B**

The question asks which standard distributions are used for the two building blocks of op-risk modeling.

- Frequency = how MANY losses happen in a year. This is a count, and counts are typically modeled with the Poisson distribution (with intensity lambda).
- Severity = how BIG each loss is. Loss sizes are fat-tailed (a few enormous losses), so they are typically modeled with the lognormal distribution.

You then combine them (often by Monte Carlo) to get the total annual loss.

Analogy: Frequency is "how many hailstones fall" (a count - Poisson); severity is "how big each hailstone is" (a size that can occasionally be huge - lognormal).

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Normal isn't used - it has thin tails and can give negative loss counts/sizes, which makes no sense.
- C: Binomial/uniform are not the standard choices; uniform severity wrongly says all loss sizes are equally likely.
- D: This swaps the two around - frequency should be Poisson (a count), not lognormal.

📌 Exam tip: Remember the pairing - Poisson for COUNT (frequency), lognormal for SIZE (severity).

**Q3.** A business line has operational losses with Poisson frequency lambda = 12 per year and a severity distribution with a mean of $80,000 per loss. The expected aggregate annual loss is:
- A. $80,000
- B. $240,000
- C. $960,000
- D. $6,667

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the average total loss per year.

The expected aggregate loss is simply the average number of losses times the average size of each loss:

$$E[\text{annual loss}] = \lambda \times E[\text{severity}]$$

Plug in:
- lambda = 12 losses per year
- mean severity = $80,000

$$E[\text{annual loss}] = 12 \times 80{,}000 = 960{,}000$$

So the expected annual loss is $960,000.

Analogy: If you typically drop 12 plates a year, and each broken plate costs $80,000 to replace, you expect to lose 12 x $80,000 = $960,000 a year.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($80,000): That's the cost of just one loss, ignoring frequency.
- B ($240,000): Used 3 x 80,000 or some wrong count - not 12.
- D ($6,667): Divided instead of multiplied (80,000 / 12) - a units mistake.

📌 Exam tip: Expected aggregate loss = frequency x average severity. But remember: this average does NOT tell you the capital - capital depends on the tail (the severity distribution's spread), not the mean.

**Q4.** Under the current Standardized Measurement Approach (SMA), operational risk capital is calculated as:
- A. 15% of average annual gross income
- B. The Business Indicator Component (BIC) multiplied by the Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM)
- C. A 99.9% internal VaR model output
- D. A fixed percentage of total assets

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the structure of the current Basel SMA capital formula.

The SMA capital is:

$$\text{Capital} = BIC \times ILM$$

- BIC (Business Indicator Component): a size/income-based charge - bigger banks face a higher marginal rate.
- ILM (Internal Loss Multiplier): scales capital up or down based on the bank's own 10-year loss history.

So a bank's size sets the base charge, and its loss experience adjusts it.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (15% of gross income): That's the legacy Basic Indicator Approach (BIA), not the current SMA.
- C (99.9% internal VaR): That's the AMA, which was WITHDRAWN because banks' models varied too much and could be gamed.
- D (% of total assets): Not how op-risk capital is set; the SMA uses income-based indicators, not total assets.

📌 Exam tip: SMA = BIC x ILM. Know the structure (size-based charge, scaled by loss experience), not the exact coefficients.

**Q5.** Bank A and Bank B both have a Business Indicator Component (BIC) of $2.0 billion. Bank A has a loss component LC equal to its BIC, giving an Internal Loss Multiplier (ILM) of 1. Bank B has a worse loss history with a higher ILM of 1.241. What does this tell you about their capital?
- A. Both banks hold $2.0 billion.
- B. Bank B holds more capital ($2.48 billion) because of its worse loss history.
- C. Bank A holds more capital because its BIC is higher.
- D. Loss history has no effect on capital under the SMA.

**Answer: B**

The question asks how loss history changes capital under the SMA.

Capital = BIC x ILM.

- Bank A: $2.0B x 1 = $2.0 billion.
- Bank B: $2.0B x 1.241 = $2.482 billion (about $2.48 billion).

Because Bank B has a worse historical loss record, its ILM is above 1, so it must hold more capital - about 24% more than Bank A even though they are the same size.

Analogy: Two drivers with identical cars (same BIC). The one with more past accidents (worse loss history) pays a higher insurance premium (more capital).

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Only true if both had ILM = 1, but Bank B's ILM is 1.241.
- C: Their BICs are equal ($2.0B each), so this is false; and a higher ILM means MORE capital, which belongs to Bank B.
- D: False - the whole point of the ILM is that loss history DOES affect capital.

📌 Exam tip: ILM > 1 means worse-than-peer losses and MORE capital; ILM < 1 means better losses and LESS capital. ILM = 1 when LC = BIC.

**Q6.** Which of the following is a Key Risk Indicator (KRI) rather than a realized loss event?
- A. A $2 million settlement failure
- B. A rising count of unreconciled trades
- C. A regulatory fine
- D. A data-center fire

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to distinguish a KRI from an actual loss.

A KRI (key risk indicator) is a forward-looking metric that signals risk is BUILDING UP before any loss has occurred. A rising count of unreconciled trades is a warning sign - it tells you something could go wrong soon, so you can act early.

Analogy: A KRI is like the temperature gauge on your car climbing into the red zone - a warning. An actual loss event is the engine actually breaking down.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: A $2 million settlement failure is a realized loss event (it already happened), not a forward-looking indicator.
- C: A regulatory fine is a realized loss event.
- D: A data-center fire is a realized loss event (damage to physical assets).

📌 Exam tip: KRIs are forward-looking DRIVERS (staff turnover, system downtime, unreconciled trades, audit findings). If money has already been lost, it's a loss event, not a KRI.

**Q7.** Operational losses are often split into HFLS and LFHS categories. Which type primarily drives the capital tail (extreme losses)?
- A. High-frequency/low-severity (HFLS) losses, such as processing errors
- B. Low-frequency/high-severity (LFHS) losses, such as a rogue trader or massive fines
- C. Medium-frequency/medium-severity losses
- D. Losses that are excluded from Basel's definition

**Answer: B**

The question asks which loss type drives the capital tail.

- HFLS (high-frequency, low-severity): things like data-entry mistakes - they happen often but each is tiny. These are predictable and near the expected loss, so they are "budgetable."
- LFHS (low-frequency, high-severity): things like a rogue trader or a multi-billion-dollar fine - they happen rarely but are enormous. These rare giants create the dangerous tail that capital must absorb.

Analogy: Small daily spills (HFLS) are annoying but cheap to mop up. A once-in-a-decade flood (LFHS) is what you really need insurance for.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: HFLS losses are near expected loss and budgetable - they don't drive the tail.
- C: Not the curriculum's framing; the tail is driven by the rare, severe events.
- D: Excluded losses (strategic/reputational) aren't part of op-risk capital at all.

📌 Exam tip: The capital tail = LFHS events. These are exactly where internal data is sparse, so scenario analysis and external data are used to fill the gap.

**Q8.** Allocating operational risk capital to business lines based ONLY on their past loss history can create which problem?
- A. It eliminates all operational risk.
- B. Moral hazard, because business units have an incentive to hide losses to reduce their capital charge.
- C. It guarantees more accurate capital figures.
- D. It removes the need for any controls.

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the downside of allocating capital purely on loss history.

If a business unit knows that reporting more losses means being charged more capital, it has an incentive to HIDE losses to look better. This is moral hazard - the allocation method creates a bad incentive that undermines honest reporting.

Analogy: If telling your parents about every small accident means a bigger cut to your allowance, you might be tempted to hide accidents - which makes the real risk invisible.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Capital absorbs losses; it never eliminates the underlying risk. Only controls reduce risk.
- C: Hiding losses makes the figures LESS accurate, not more.
- D: Controls are always needed; capital and controls do different jobs.

📌 Exam tip: Capital ABSORBS losses; controls REDUCE them. Allocation should reward good control environments, not just punish reported losses, otherwise you breed moral hazard.

**Q9.** Scenario analysis is especially important in operational risk modeling because it:
- A. Provides precise probabilities for everyday processing errors.
- B. Fills in the tail (rare, severe events) where internal loss data is sparse or absent.
- C. Replaces the need for any internal loss data collection.
- D. Is required only for high-frequency losses.

**Answer: B**

The question asks why scenario analysis matters in op risk.

The most damaging operational events (a 1-in-40-year cyber breach, a giant fine) are so rare that a bank may have little or no internal data on them. Scenario analysis uses structured expert workshops to estimate what such extreme events could cost, filling the gap in the tail where hard data doesn't exist.

Analogy: You have plenty of data on minor fender-benders, but almost none on a catastrophic crash. To plan for the catastrophe, experts sit down and reason out "what would it cost if it happened?" - that's scenario analysis.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Everyday errors (HFLS) have plenty of data; scenario analysis is for the rare, data-poor tail, not the routine stuff.
- C: It complements internal data, it doesn't replace it - internal loss collection is still essential.
- D: It's used for low-frequency/high-severity events, not high-frequency ones.

📌 Exam tip: Scenario analysis = expert estimates for rare tail events where data is missing. Its weakness is bias (anchoring, overconfidence) because it relies on judgment.

**Q10.** A risk and control self-assessment (RCSA) is best described as:
- A. A regulator's audit of a bank's capital model.
- B. A process where business units identify their own risks, rate likelihood and impact, and assess control effectiveness.
- C. A database of every loss event that has already occurred.
- D. The 99.9% quantile of the aggregate loss distribution.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the definition of RCSA.

In an RCSA, the business units themselves periodically (a) identify the risks they face, (b) rate how likely each is and how bad the impact would be, and (c) judge how well their controls are working. Its strength is ownership and coverage of risks that haven't yet caused a loss; its weakness is subjectivity and self-serving bias (units may rate themselves too kindly).

Analogy: It's like a self-checkup where each department fills out a "what could go wrong here, and how good are our safety measures?" form.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: That's an external/regulatory review, not a self-assessment by the business units.
- C: That describes loss data collection (a historical loss database), a different tool.
- D: That's a capital/VaR concept, not a self-assessment process.

📌 Exam tip: RCSA = units self-assess risks and controls (forward-looking, covers un-materialized risks). Its main weakness is subjectivity/self-serving bias.
