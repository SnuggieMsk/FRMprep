**Q1.** An AR(1) process is $$Y_t = 1.5 + 0.7\,Y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$. Its mean-reverting (long-run) level is:
- A. 1.5
- B. 2.14
- C. 5.0
- D. 0.45

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the long-run average the series drifts back toward.

Step 1 — The mean-reverting level of an AR(1) is the intercept divided by one minus the slope:
$$\mu = \frac{\delta}{1 - \phi}$$

Step 2 — Plug in $$\delta = 1.5$$ and $$\phi = 0.7$$:
$$\mu = \frac{1.5}{1 - 0.7} = \frac{1.5}{0.3} = 5.0$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (1.5): this is just the intercept $$\delta$$ — a very common trap. The long-run mean is NOT the intercept unless $$\phi = 0$$.
- B (2.14): this is $$1.5/0.7$$, dividing by $$\phi$$ instead of $$1 - \phi$$.
- D (0.45): this is $$1.5 \times 0.3$$ — multiplying instead of dividing.

Analogy: imagine a stretched rubber band that always pulls back toward a resting point. The intercept and the "pull strength" $$\phi$$ together set where that resting point is — not the intercept alone.

📌 Exam tip: Mean reversion level = $$\delta/(1-\phi)$$, NOT $$\delta$$. The denominator is $$1 - \phi$$.

**Q2.** For a moving-average MA(2) process, the autocorrelation function (ACF):
- A. Decays geometrically forever.
- B. Is zero at all lags.
- C. Cuts off to zero after lag 2.
- D. Equals the PACF at every lag.

**Answer: C**

The question tests the identifying pattern of an MA process's ACF.

Key fact: an MA($$q$$) process has memory of exactly $$q$$ periods. Its ACF is nonzero up to lag $$q$$ and then CUTS OFF (becomes exactly zero) for all lags beyond $$q$$. For MA(2), the ACF is zero after lag 2. The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: geometric decay forever is the AR pattern, not MA. The MA cuts off sharply.
- B: the ACF is NOT zero at all lags — it is nonzero at lags 1 and 2 (the MA terms create those correlations).
- D: ACF and PACF are different functions; for an MA process the PACF decays gradually while the ACF cuts off.

Analogy: an MA(2) only "remembers" the last two shocks. Anything older than two periods has no influence, so the correlation drops to zero past lag 2 — like a memory that erases everything older than two days.

📌 Exam tip: MA($$q$$): ACF cuts off after lag $$q$$, PACF decays. AR($$p$$): the reverse — PACF cuts off after lag $$p$$, ACF decays.

**Q3.** Which is REQUIRED for a series to be covariance-stationary?
- A. The series is normally distributed.
- B. Constant finite mean and variance, with autocovariances depending only on the lag.
- C. The series has zero mean.
- D. The series is independent over time.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the precise definition of covariance stationarity.

A series is covariance-stationary when three things hold for all time $$t$$:
1. Constant mean: $$E[Y_t] = \mu$$.
2. Constant finite variance: $$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = \gamma_0 < \infty$$.
3. The covariance between two points depends only on the lag (distance) between them, not on where in time they sit.

That is exactly option B. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: normality is NOT required — a series can be stationary with any distribution shape.
- C: the mean must be CONSTANT, but it need not be zero.
- D: independence is stronger than what is needed. Stationarity only requires the covariance structure to be stable; the observations can still be correlated over time (indeed AR/MA series are).

Analogy: covariance stationarity means the series "looks statistically the same" no matter which window of time you view it through — same average level, same wobble size, same relationship between neighboring points.

📌 Exam tip: Three conditions — constant mean, constant finite variance, lag-only autocovariance. Normality, zero mean, and independence are NOT required.

**Q4.** An AR(1) process has $$\phi = 0.6$$, a long-run mean of 10, and a current value of 16. The 2-step-ahead forecast is closest to:
- A. 12.16
- B. 13.60
- C. 10.00
- D. 16.00

**Answer: A**

The question asks where the series is expected to be two periods from now, given it is currently above its mean.

Step 1 — The h-step forecast formula (mean-reversion form):
$$\hat{Y}_{t+h} = \mu + \phi^h\,(Y_t - \mu)$$

Step 2 — Identify the pieces: $$\mu = 10$$, $$\phi = 0.6$$, $$Y_t = 16$$, $$h = 2$$.
$$Y_t - \mu = 16 - 10 = 6$$
$$\phi^2 = 0.6^2 = 0.36$$

Step 3 — Combine:
$$\hat{Y}_{t+2} = 10 + 0.36 \times 6 = 10 + 2.16 = 12.16$$

The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B (13.60): this is the 1-STEP forecast ($$10 + 0.6 \times 6 = 13.6$$), not the 2-step. The trap is using $$\phi$$ instead of $$\phi^2$$.
- C (10.00): this is the long-run mean — the forecast only reaches it as $$h \to \infty$$, not at $$h = 2$$.
- D (16.00): this just repeats the current value, ignoring mean reversion entirely.

Analogy: the series is 6 units above its resting point. Each step it gives back a fraction (60%) of the remaining gap. After two steps, only $$0.6^2 = 36\%$$ of the original gap remains, leaving it at 12.16 — heading down toward 10.

📌 Exam tip: $$\hat{Y}_{t+h} = \mu + \phi^h(Y_t - \mu)$$. For h steps, use $$\phi^h$$ — forecasts glide geometrically toward the mean.

**Q5.** An interest-rate model is $$r_t = 0.6 + 0.85\,r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$ with the current rate $$r_t = 5.0\%$$. The one-step-ahead forecast is:
- A. 5.10%
- B. 4.85%
- C. 4.00%
- D. 5.85%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for next period's expected rate using the AR(1) recursion.

Step 1 — The one-step forecast sets the future shock to zero:
$$\hat{r}_{t+1} = \delta + \phi\,r_t$$

Step 2 — Plug in $$\delta = 0.6$$, $$\phi = 0.85$$, $$r_t = 5.0$$:
$$\hat{r}_{t+1} = 0.6 + 0.85 \times 5.0 = 0.6 + 4.25 = 4.85\%$$

The correct answer is B.

Note this is consistent with mean reversion: the long-run mean is $$0.6/(1-0.85) = 4.0\%$$, and since the current rate (5.0%) is ABOVE 4.0%, the forecast (4.85%) moves DOWN toward the mean.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (5.10%): comes from a mis-multiplication; the correct product is 4.25, not 4.50.
- C (4.00%): this is the long-run mean, reached only after many steps, not in one step.
- D (5.85%): this adds 0.6 to the current 5.0 plus an error — it forgets to multiply by $$\phi$$.

Analogy: take 85% of where you are now (4.25) and add the baseline pull (0.6) to predict the next value. Since you're above the resting point, the prediction nudges downward.

📌 Exam tip: One-step AR(1) forecast = $$\delta + \phi Y_t$$ (set the future shock to zero). Check direction against the mean-reversion level.

**Q6.** A series is white noise. Which of the following must be TRUE?
- A. The observations are independent and normally distributed.
- B. The mean is zero, the variance is constant, and the autocorrelations are zero at all nonzero lags.
- C. The series is perfectly forecastable from its own past.
- D. The variance grows over time.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the defining properties of white noise.

White noise $$\{\epsilon_t\}$$ requires three things:
1. Zero mean: $$E[\epsilon_t] = 0$$.
2. Constant variance: $$\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_t) = \sigma^2$$.
3. Zero autocorrelation at all nonzero lags: $$\rho_h = 0$$ for $$h \ne 0$$ (uncorrelated, though not necessarily independent).

That matches B exactly. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: plain white noise requires only UNCORRELATED, not independent or normal. (Independent white noise adds iid; Gaussian white noise adds normality — those are stronger special cases.)
- C: white noise is UNFORECASTABLE from its own past — that is why it is the benchmark for good model residuals.
- D: the variance is CONSTANT, not growing.

Analogy: white noise is like the static hiss on an old radio — it has no pattern, no trend, and knowing past hiss tells you nothing about future hiss.

📌 Exam tip: White noise = zero mean, constant variance, zero autocorrelation (uncorrelated). Independent white noise adds iid; Gaussian adds normality.

**Q7.** An MA(1) process is $$Y_t = 2 + 0.5\,\epsilon_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$ with $$\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 4$$. The variance of $$Y_t$$ is:
- A. 4
- B. 5
- C. 6
- D. 8

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the unconditional variance of an MA(1) process.

Step 1 — The MA(1) variance formula:
$$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = \sigma^2(1 + \theta^2)$$

Step 2 — Plug in $$\sigma^2 = 4$$ and $$\theta = 0.5$$:
$$\theta^2 = 0.5^2 = 0.25$$
$$\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 4 \times (1 + 0.25) = 4 \times 1.25 = 5$$

The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (4): this is just $$\sigma^2$$, ignoring the $$(1 + \theta^2)$$ factor — i.e., treating it as if $$\theta = 0$$.
- C (6): comes from using $$1 + 2\theta$$ or some other wrong factor.
- D (8): this is $$\sigma^2 \times 2$$, which would require $$\theta^2 = 1$$, i.e., $$\theta = 1$$.

Analogy: an MA(1) value is built from two shocks — today's shock and a fraction ($$\theta$$) of yesterday's. Because both contribute, the total variance is the base variance "puffed up" by the factor $$1 + \theta^2$$.

📌 Exam tip: MA(1) variance = $$\sigma^2(1 + \theta^2)$$. Square $$\theta$$, add 1, multiply by $$\sigma^2$$.

**Q8.** For the MA(1) process $$Y_t = 2 + 0.5\,\epsilon_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$ with $$\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 4$$, the lag-1 autocorrelation $$\rho_1$$ is:
- A. 0.25
- B. 0.40
- C. 0.50
- D. 0.80

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the first autocorrelation of an MA(1).

Step 1 — The MA(1) lag-1 autocorrelation formula:
$$\rho_1 = \frac{\theta}{1 + \theta^2}$$

Step 2 — Plug in $$\theta = 0.5$$:
$$\theta^2 = 0.25$$
$$\rho_1 = \frac{0.5}{1 + 0.25} = \frac{0.5}{1.25} = 0.40$$

The correct answer is B.

(And for $$h \ge 2$$, $$\rho_h = 0$$ — the MA(1) ACF cuts off after lag 1.)

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.25): this is $$\theta^2$$, not the autocorrelation.
- C (0.50): this is $$\theta$$ itself, forgetting to divide by $$1 + \theta^2$$.
- D (0.80): this divides incorrectly (e.g., $$1/1.25$$) or otherwise mishandles the formula.

Analogy: yesterday's shock leaks into today through the coefficient $$\theta = 0.5$$, but the correlation is "diluted" by the total variance factor $$1 + \theta^2 = 1.25$$, giving 0.40 rather than 0.50.

📌 Exam tip: MA(1): $$\rho_1 = \theta/(1+\theta^2)$$, and $$\rho_h = 0$$ for $$h \ge 2$$. The denominator matches the variance factor.

**Q9.** Residuals from a fitted model with $$T = 100$$ have sample autocorrelations $$\hat\rho_1 = 0.20$$, $$\hat\rho_2 = 0.10$$, $$\hat\rho_3 = 0.05$$. The Ljung–Box Q-statistic with $$m = 3$$ and the decision are (critical $$\chi^2_3$$ at 5% = 7.81):
- A. 5.42; fail to reject — residuals consistent with white noise.
- B. 5.42; reject — residuals are serially correlated.
- C. 21.0; reject — residuals are serially correlated.
- D. 0.0532; fail to reject.

**Answer: A**

The question asks you to test whether the residuals are white noise using the Ljung–Box statistic.

Step 1 — The Ljung–Box formula:
$$Q_{LB} = T(T+2)\sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{\hat\rho_h^2}{T - h}$$

Step 2 — Compute the front factor:
$$T(T+2) = 100 \times 102 = 10200$$

Step 3 — Compute each term in the sum (square each autocorrelation, divide by $$T - h$$):
$$\frac{\hat\rho_1^2}{T-1} = \frac{0.20^2}{99} = \frac{0.04}{99} = 0.000404$$
$$\frac{\hat\rho_2^2}{T-2} = \frac{0.10^2}{98} = \frac{0.01}{98} = 0.000102$$
$$\frac{\hat\rho_3^2}{T-3} = \frac{0.05^2}{97} = \frac{0.0025}{97} = 0.0000258$$

Sum: $$0.000404 + 0.000102 + 0.0000258 = 0.000532$$

Step 4 — Multiply by the front factor:
$$Q_{LB} = 10200 \times 0.000532 = 5.42$$

Step 5 — Compare to the critical value 7.81. Since $$5.42 < 7.81$$, fail to reject — the residuals are consistent with white noise. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B: the statistic 5.42 is correct, but it is BELOW 7.81, so we fail to reject, not reject. Decision backwards.
- C (21.0): this uses the Box–Pierce-like form $$T\sum\hat\rho_h^2$$ without the proper weighting, or mis-scales — it overstates the statistic.
- D (0.0532): this forgets to multiply by $$T(T+2)$$ — it is roughly the raw sum scaled by 100.

Analogy: the Ljung–Box test totals up the "leftover patterns" in the residuals. A score of 5.42 is below the 7.81 alarm line, so the residuals look like harmless static — the model has captured the structure.

📌 Exam tip: $$Q_{LB} = T(T+2)\sum \hat\rho_h^2/(T-h)$$, compared to $$\chi^2_m$$. Reject for LARGE Q (serial correlation present). Don't forget the $$T(T+2)$$ front factor.

**Q10.** An AR(1) interest-rate model is $$r_t = 0.2 + 0.95\,r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$$. Which statement is correct?
- A. The series is non-stationary because $$\phi$$ is close to 1, so it has no mean.
- B. The mean-reverting level is 0.2%.
- C. The mean-reverting level is 4.0%.
- D. The mean-reverting level is 0.19%.

**Answer: C**

The question tests the mean-reversion trap: a small intercept with $$\phi$$ near 1 can imply a large long-run mean.

Step 1 — Check stationarity first. For AR(1), stationary requires $$|\phi| < 1$$. Here $$\phi = 0.95 < 1$$, so the series IS stationary and a mean-reverting level exists.

Step 2 — Compute the mean-reverting level:
$$\mu = \frac{\delta}{1 - \phi} = \frac{0.2}{1 - 0.95} = \frac{0.2}{0.05} = 4.0\%$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: $$\phi = 0.95$$ is less than 1, so the series IS stationary and DOES have a finite mean. (Non-stationarity needs $$\phi \ge 1$$.)
- B (0.2%): this mistakes the intercept $$\delta$$ for the mean. With $$\phi$$ near 1, the true mean is far larger.
- D (0.19%): this multiplies $$0.2 \times 0.95$$ instead of dividing by $$1 - 0.95$$.

Analogy: a small steady push (intercept 0.2) combined with a very weak pull-back (only 5% reversion each period) lets the series settle at a surprisingly high resting point — like a slow leak filling a tub to a high level because it drains very slowly.

📌 Exam tip: Mean level = $$\delta/(1-\phi)$$. When $$\phi$$ is near 1, $$1 - \phi$$ is tiny, so even a small $$\delta$$ gives a large mean. Always confirm $$|\phi| < 1$$ first — otherwise no mean reversion exists.
