**Q1.** A 90-day Treasury bill is quoted at a discount rate of 8.00%. Its cash price per $100 of face value is:
- A. $92.00
- B. $98.00
- C. $98.04
- D. $96.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the actual dollar price of a T-bill given its quoted discount rate. T-bill quotes are discount rates on FACE value, using a 360-day year.

The price formula is:
$$P = 100 - \frac{n}{360} \times Q$$

where $$n = 90$$ days and $$Q = 8.00$$ (the discount rate in percent).

Step 1 — compute the day fraction:
$$\frac{90}{360} = 0.25$$

Step 2 — compute the discount:
$$0.25 \times 8.00 = 2.00$$

Step 3 — subtract from 100:
$$P = 100 - 2.00 = 98.00$$

So B is correct: $98.00 per $100 face.

Plain words: a discount quote tells you how many dollars are knocked OFF the $100 face. Over 90 days (a quarter of a 360-day year) at 8%, that's 2 dollars off, so you pay 98.

- A ($92.00) wrongly applies the full 8% for a whole year ($100 − 8) instead of just 90 days.
- C ($98.04) treats 8% as a yield and computes $$100/1.02$$ — but the quote is a discount, not a yield.
- D ($96.00) uses the wrong day fraction (e.g., 180/360).

📌 Exam tip: T-bill discount price = $$100 - (n/360)\times Q$$. The discount rate is NOT a yield — it understates the true return because it divides by face (100) and uses 360 days.

**Q2.** One basis point move in the rate underlying a three-month Eurodollar (or SOFR) futures contract changes the contract value by:
- A. $100
- B. $50
- C. $25
- D. $10

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the dollar value of a one-basis-point move on a standard three-month interest rate futures contract.

The contract has a $1,000,000 notional and covers a three-month (90/360) period. One basis point is 0.0001. So:
$$\$1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 \times \frac{90}{360}$$

Step 1 — basis point times notional:
$$1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 = 100$$

Step 2 — apply the three-month fraction:
$$100 \times \frac{90}{360} = 100 \times 0.25 = 25$$

So C is correct: each basis point is worth $25.

Plain words: a full 1% on $1,000,000 for a whole year would be $10,000. But we only want 1/100 of that (one basis point) and only a quarter of a year (three months): $$10{,}000 \times 0.01 \times 0.25 = 25$$.

- A ($100) forgets the three-month (×0.25) adjustment — that's the value for a full-year contract.
- B ($50) uses a half-year fraction by mistake.
- D ($10) drops a factor; not produced by the correct calculation.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize "$25 per basis point" for three-month Eurodollar/SOFR futures. It comes from $1M × 0.0001 × (3/12).

**Q3.** The futures settlement price is 95.00. Three deliverable bonds are: A quoted 98.00 (CF 1.0300), B quoted 105.00 (CF 1.1000), C quoted 112.50 (CF 1.1800). The cheapest-to-deliver bond is:
- A. Bond A
- B. Bond B
- C. Bond C
- D. Cannot be determined without accrued interest

**Answer: A**

The question asks which bond is cheapest for the short to deliver. The short picks the bond that minimizes the net cost.

The cost-to-deliver formula:
$$\text{Cost} = \text{Quoted price} - (\text{Futures price} \times \text{CF})$$

Bond A:
$$98.00 - (95.00 \times 1.0300) = 98.00 - 97.85 = 0.15$$

Bond B:
$$105.00 - (95.00 \times 1.1000) = 105.00 - 104.50 = 0.50$$

Bond C:
$$112.50 - (95.00 \times 1.1800) = 112.50 - 112.10 = 0.40$$

Compare: A = 0.15, B = 0.50, C = 0.40. The lowest cost is Bond A.

So A is correct.

Plain words: the short buys a bond in the market (its quoted price) and gets paid the futures price × conversion factor at delivery. The cheapest-to-deliver is the one where what you pay minus what you receive is smallest.

- B (Bond B) has the highest cost (0.50), the worst choice for the short.
- C (Bond C) has cost 0.40, still higher than A's 0.15.
- D is wrong because accrued interest is added to BOTH the cost side and the invoice side, so it cancels out — it does not affect which bond is cheapest.

📌 Exam tip: CTD minimizes (Quoted price − Futures × CF). Use the QUOTED (clean) price; accrued interest cancels, so "need accrued interest" answers are traps.

**Q4.** Relative to the forward rate for the same future period, the Eurodollar futures rate is:
- A. Lower, because of the convexity adjustment
- B. Higher, because daily settlement favors the rate position when rates rise
- C. Identical, by no-arbitrage
- D. Higher only when the yield curve is inverted

**Answer: B**

The question asks how the futures rate compares to the forward rate before any adjustment.

Why B is correct: futures are settled DAILY (marked to market). This daily settlement benefits the short-rate position priced into the futures — gains arrive when rates are high (and can be reinvested at high rates) while losses are funded cheaply when rates are low. This advantage pushes the futures RATE above the forward rate. So:
$$\text{forward} = \text{futures} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t_1 t_2$$
We SUBTRACT the convexity adjustment to go from futures down to forward, confirming the futures rate is higher.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — the convexity adjustment makes the FORWARD lower than the futures, i.e., the futures is higher; A states the comparison backwards.
- C — daily settlement breaks the perfect equality; they are not identical.
- D — the bias exists regardless of curve shape (it comes from volatility and timing), not only when inverted.

Analogy: getting paid your winnings every single day (futures) is slightly better than waiting until the end (forward), so the futures rate is priced a touch higher to reflect that timing advantage.

📌 Exam tip: Futures rate > forward rate. Convert with $$\text{forward} = \text{futures} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t_1 t_2$$. The adjustment grows with the SQUARE of volatility and with maturity — tiny for short maturities, big for long ones.

**Q5.** A US Treasury bond pays an 8% semiannual coupon (coupons March 1 and September 1). For settlement on July 3, March 1 to July 3 is 124 actual days and the full coupon period (March 1 to September 1) is 184 days. Using Actual/Actual, the accrued interest per $100 face is closest to:
- A. $2.70
- B. $4.00
- C. $5.39
- D. $2.17

**Answer: A**

The question asks for accrued interest on a Treasury bond, which uses the Actual/Actual day-count convention.

Accrued interest = coupon for the period × (days elapsed / days in period). The semiannual coupon is half of 8% on $100 = $4.

$$\text{AI} = 4 \times \frac{124}{184}$$

Step 1 — the accrual fraction:
$$\frac{124}{184} = 0.67391$$

Step 2 — multiply by the semiannual coupon:
$$4 \times 0.67391 = 2.6957 \approx \$2.70$$

So A is correct.

Plain words: by July 3, about 67% of the half-year coupon period has passed, so the seller has "earned" about 67% of the $4 coupon — roughly $2.70 — which the buyer pays as accrued interest.

- B ($4.00) is the FULL semiannual coupon, as if the whole period had elapsed.
- C ($5.39) wrongly uses the full annual 8% coupon ($8) times the fraction, double-counting.
- D ($2.17) uses a wrong fraction (e.g., treating it as months out of a full year).

📌 Exam tip: Treasuries use Actual/Actual: AI = (semiannual coupon) × (actual days elapsed ÷ actual days in the coupon period). Corporates would use 30/360 instead.

**Q6.** A manager holds a $10 million bond portfolio with duration 6.8 years and wants to hedge against rising rates over three months. T-bond futures have a contract value of $93,062.50, and the cheapest-to-deliver bond will have duration 9.2 years at expiry. The number of futures contracts for the duration-based hedge is closest to:
- A. Long 79 contracts
- B. Short 79 contracts
- C. Short 107 contracts
- D. Short 59 contracts

**Answer: B**

The question asks how many futures to trade to neutralize the portfolio's interest rate risk, and in which direction.

The duration-based hedge formula:
$$N^* = -\frac{P \times D_P}{F_C \times D_F}$$

where $$P = 10{,}000{,}000$$, $$D_P = 6.8$$, $$F_C = 93{,}062.50$$ (contract value), and $$D_F = 9.2$$ (duration of the CTD at expiry).

Step 1 — numerator:
$$10{,}000{,}000 \times 6.8 = 68{,}000{,}000$$

Step 2 — denominator:
$$93{,}062.50 \times 9.2 = 856{,}175$$

Step 3 — divide and apply the minus sign:
$$N^* = -\frac{68{,}000{,}000}{856{,}175} = -79.4 \approx -79$$

The negative sign means SHORT, and the magnitude rounds to 79. So B is correct: short 79 contracts.

Plain words: to protect a bond portfolio from rising rates (which lower bond prices), you SHORT futures so they gain when rates rise, offsetting the loss on your bonds.

- A (long 79) has the right number but wrong direction; going long would ADD to the rate risk, not hedge it.
- C (short 107) wrongly uses the portfolio duration for the futures (6.8 in the denominator) or mismatches the durations.
- D (short 59) results from swapping the durations incorrectly.

📌 Exam tip: Use the CTD bond's duration for $$D_F$$, NOT the portfolio's. A negative $$N^*$$ means short futures — the usual hedge for a long bond portfolio fearing rate increases.

**Q7.** A Eurodollar futures contract has a final settlement quote of 99.00. Using the rule that the final contract price equals $$10{,}000 \times [100 - 0.25(100 - Q)]$$, the final contract price is:
- A. $990,000
- B. $997,500
- C. $1,000,000
- D. $975,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the dollar settlement value of a Eurodollar futures contract given the final quote Q = 99.00.

The formula:
$$\text{Price} = 10{,}000 \times [100 - 0.25(100 - Q)]$$

Step 1 — compute $$100 - Q$$ (this is the implied rate in percent):
$$100 - 99.00 = 1.00$$

Step 2 — multiply by 0.25 (the three-month adjustment):
$$0.25 \times 1.00 = 0.25$$

Step 3 — subtract from 100 and multiply by 10,000:
$$100 - 0.25 = 99.75$$
$$10{,}000 \times 99.75 = 997{,}500$$

So B is correct: $997,500.

Plain words: a quote of 99.00 means an implied rate of 1%. Because the contract only covers three months, you charge a quarter of that 1% (0.25) against the $1,000,000 notional, giving a price of $997,500.

- A ($990,000) forgets the 0.25 three-month factor and uses the full 1% off ($10,000 × 99 = $990,000).
- C ($1,000,000) treats the contract as worth full notional with no rate deduction.
- D ($975,000) uses 2.5% off instead of 0.25%.

📌 Exam tip: Final ED futures price = $$10{,}000 \times [100 - 0.25(100 - Q)]$$. The 0.25 reflects the three-month period — don't drop it.

**Q8.** Bonds are quoted "clean," but settlement is on the "dirty" (cash) price. The relationship is:
- A. Dirty price = Clean price − Accrued interest
- B. Dirty price = Clean price + Accrued interest
- C. Dirty price = Clean price × Conversion factor
- D. Dirty price = Clean price, since accrued interest is ignored

**Answer: B**

The question asks how the cash (dirty) price you actually pay relates to the quoted (clean) price.

Why B is correct: the quoted price excludes interest that has built up since the last coupon. But the seller is owed that earned interest, so the buyer pays it on top:
$$\text{Cash (dirty) price} = \text{Quoted (clean) price} + \text{Accrued interest}$$

Why the others are wrong:
- A — subtracting accrued interest is backwards; the buyer must ADD it to compensate the seller.
- C — the conversion factor applies to FUTURES invoice prices, not to the clean-to-dirty conversion of a cash bond.
- D — accrued interest is NOT ignored in settlement; it's added.

Analogy: if you buy a fruit tree partway between harvests, you pay the listed tree price PLUS the value of the fruit that has already grown since the last picking. The grown fruit is the accrued interest.

📌 Exam tip: You QUOTE clean, you PAY dirty: Dirty = Clean + Accrued Interest. The buyer reimburses the seller for interest earned since the last coupon.

**Q9.** For a three-month Eurodollar futures contract, a LONG position gains when:
- A. The quoted price falls (rates rise)
- B. The quoted price rises (rates fall)
- C. Accrued interest increases
- D. The conversion factor rises

**Answer: B**

The question asks what makes the holder of a long Eurodollar futures position profit.

Why B is correct: the quote is $$100 - R$$. So when rates $$R$$ FALL, the quoted price RISES. A long position profits when the price it bought rises, so the long gains when rates fall (price rises).

Why the others are wrong:
- A — a falling quote means rates rose; that's a LOSS for the long (and a gain for the short).
- C — accrued interest is a bond concept, irrelevant to whether a Eurodollar futures long gains.
- D — the conversion factor applies to Treasury bond futures, not Eurodollar futures.

Analogy: since the price is "100 minus the rate," lower rates push the price up like a seesaw. Buying low and watching the price climb (because rates dropped) is how the long wins.

📌 Exam tip: Eurodollar quote = 100 − rate. Long gains when PRICE RISES = rates FALL. Short gains when rates rise. The seesaw between price and rate trips up many candidates.

**Q10.** A key limitation of duration-based hedging is that it assumes:
- A. A large, non-parallel twist in the yield curve
- B. A small, parallel shift in the yield curve
- C. Interest rates remain perfectly constant
- D. The portfolio contains only zero-coupon bonds

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the main assumption (and weakness) behind a duration hedge.

Why B is correct: duration measures price sensitivity to a SMALL, PARALLEL shift in the yield curve (all rates move by the same small amount). The hedge is only accurate under that assumption. It ignores convexity (so it fails for large moves) and ignores twists (so it fails when short and long rates move differently).

Why the others are wrong:
- A — duration does NOT handle large or non-parallel (twisting) moves; that's precisely where it breaks down, not what it assumes.
- C — if rates never changed, you wouldn't need a hedge at all; the hedge is for when rates DO move (by a small parallel amount).
- D — duration hedging applies to any portfolio, not just zero-coupon bonds.

Analogy: duration is like estimating how far a car travels using its current speed only — fine for a quick glance (small move), but inaccurate over a long trip where speed changes (large/non-parallel moves), which is what convexity would capture.

📌 Exam tip: Duration assumes a SMALL PARALLEL shift. It fails for large moves (ignores convexity) and curve twists. For T-bond futures, use the CTD bond's duration at expiry as $$D_F$$.
