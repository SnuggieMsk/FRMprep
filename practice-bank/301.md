**Q1.** A bank's loan portfolio has an expected one-year loss of $50 million and a 99.9th-percentile one-year loss of $230 million. How much economic capital should the bank hold against this portfolio?
- A. $50 million
- B. $180 million
- C. $230 million
- D. $280 million

**Answer: B**

The question asks for economic capital, which is the equity cushion a bank keeps to survive a really bad year.

Key idea: a bank covers two kinds of loss differently.
- Expected loss (EL) = the average loss it knows will happen. This is treated like a normal cost of business and is paid for by the interest spread on loans (pricing). It does NOT need capital.
- Unexpected loss (UL) = the extra loss beyond the average in a bad year. THIS is what capital exists to absorb.

So economic capital = (bad-year loss) − (expected loss):
$$\text{Economic capital} = 230 - 50 = 180$$
That is $180 million. Answer B.

Analogy: think of a monthly grocery bill. You expect to spend about \$400 (that is your "expected loss" — you budget for it). But to be safe you keep extra cash for the rare month a big bill hits. The extra cash is your "capital." You only set aside the extra, not the \$400 you were already going to spend.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($50M) is just the expected loss — but EL is covered by pricing, not capital. Trap: confusing EL with capital.
- C ($230M) is the total worst-case loss; it forgets to subtract the EL that pricing already covers. Trap: not subtracting EL.
- D ($280M) wrongly adds EL and the worst-case loss together instead of subtracting. Trap: wrong operation (+ instead of −).

📌 Exam tip: Economic capital = (high-percentile loss) − (expected loss). Always subtract the expected loss first, because pricing/provisions already cover it.

**Q2.** Which risk is typically the largest single risk faced by a commercial bank?
- A. Market risk
- B. Operational risk
- C. Credit risk
- D. Foreign exchange risk

**Answer: C**

The question asks which risk usually dominates for a normal deposit-and-loan commercial bank.

A commercial bank's main business is making loans. Loans can default (borrowers fail to pay back). That is credit risk, and because lending is the core activity, credit risk is normally the biggest risk on the books.

Analogy: if your business is lending money to neighbors, your biggest worry is naturally that some neighbors won't pay you back.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (market risk) hits trading positions; it matters most for investment banks/trading desks, not a typical commercial bank.
- B (operational risk) is real and can be huge (fraud, cyberattacks), but it is the hardest to quantify and is generally not the largest for a commercial bank.
- D (FX risk) is just one slice of market risk and is usually small for a domestic commercial bank.

📌 Exam tip: For a commercial bank, remember the ranking: credit risk is biggest (lending is the core), operational risk is the hardest to measure.

**Q3.** Deposit insurance (such as FDIC coverage) most directly creates moral hazard because:
- A. Depositors demand higher interest rates from riskier banks
- B. Insured depositors stop monitoring bank risk, so banks can take big risks using cheap, insured funding
- C. It forces banks to hold more capital than is economically necessary
- D. It removes the credit risk on the bank's loan book

**Answer: B**

The question asks why guaranteeing deposits encourages risk-taking ("moral hazard" = a guarantee changes behavior for the worse).

Normally, if a bank looked risky, depositors would either pull their money or demand higher interest to compensate for the danger. But if the government insures deposits (up to a limit), depositors no longer care how risky the bank is — they get paid back either way. So they stop watching, and the bank can raise cheap deposits and gamble with them: heads the shareholders win, tails the insurance fund pays.

Analogy: if your parents promised to pay any parking ticket you ever get, you might park more carelessly. The guarantee changed your behavior.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the opposite of what happens — with insurance, depositors stop demanding higher rates from risky banks.
- C describes capital regulation, which is a mitigant for moral hazard, not the cause of it.
- D is false — deposit insurance protects depositors, it does not erase the bank's loan losses.

📌 Exam tip: Moral hazard from deposit insurance = insured depositors don't monitor risk, so cheap funding flows to risky behavior. The 1980s US savings-and-loan crisis is the classic example.

**Q4.** In a firm-commitment underwriting, a bank buys 20 million shares from the issuer at $15 each and plans to offer them at $16. Demand is weak and the bank can only sell them at $14.50. What is the bank's profit or loss?
- A. +$20 million
- B. −$10 million
- C. −$20 million
- D. $0 — the issuer bears the shortfall

**Answer: B**

The question asks how much the bank makes or loses on a firm-commitment deal that went badly.

In a firm commitment, the bank actually buys the whole issue from the company first, then tries to resell it. So the bank owns the shares and bears the risk if the price falls.

The bank paid $15 per share and could only sell at $14.50, so it loses $0.50 on each share:
$$\text{Loss per share} = 14.50 - 15.00 = -0.50$$
$$\text{Total} = 20{,}000{,}000 \times (-0.50) = -\$10{,}000{,}000$$
That is a loss of $10 million. Answer B.

Analogy: you buy 20 boxes of mangoes for \$15 each to resell, but the market price drops and you can only get \$14.50. You eat the \$0.50 loss on every box.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (+$20M) uses the planned $16 offer price minus $15 — but the shares didn't sell at $16, they sold at $14.50.
- C (−$20M) wrongly uses a $1.00 loss per share (16 − 15? or 15 − 14.5 doubled). The correct loss is $0.50 × 20M.
- D ($0) describes best efforts, where the issuer bears the risk. In a firm commitment, the BANK bears the risk.

📌 Exam tip: Firm commitment = bank buys the issue and bears price risk. Best efforts = bank just tries its best for a fee, and the ISSUER bears the shortfall risk.

**Q5.** Which statement about the originate-to-distribute (OTD) model is most accurate?
- A. It increases the originating bank's required capital
- B. It eliminated credit risk from the financial system before 2007
- C. It weakened originators' incentives to screen borrowers, contributing to the subprime crisis
- D. It requires that loans be held in the trading book until maturity

**Answer: C**

The question asks about a known weakness of OTD, where a bank makes loans and then sells them off (often by securitizing them) instead of keeping them.

The problem: if you make a loan but immediately sell it to someone else, you no longer suffer if the borrower defaults. So why bother checking carefully whether the borrower can repay? Screening and monitoring incentives collapse. In the run-up to 2007–08, US mortgage lending standards fell sharply (e.g., NINJA loans), the bad loans were repackaged into securities, and the losses spread when house prices fell.

Analogy: if you could sell a used car instantly to a stranger the moment you bought it, you wouldn't care whether the engine was good. You only care about quality when you have to keep it.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is backwards — OTD frees up capital and balance-sheet capacity, lowering required capital.
- B is false — the risk didn't disappear; it was passed to investors and ultimately blew up the system.
- D is false — OTD sells loans off; it does not require holding them, and certainly not in the trading book to maturity.

📌 Exam tip: OTD benefit = frees capital, spreads risk, more credit. OTD drawback = "no skin in the game" kills lending discipline. Post-crisis rules now require risk retention.

**Q6.** A position is held in a bank's trading book rather than its banking book. Which accounting treatment applies?
- A. Historical cost, with provisions only when impairment is likely
- B. Marked to market daily, with gains and losses flowing through profit and loss
- C. Amortized cost, never revalued until maturity
- D. Off-balance-sheet treatment with no valuation

**Answer: B**

The question asks how trading-book items are accounted for.

A bank splits its assets into two "books":
- Banking book = loans and things it intends to hold (recorded at historical/amortized cost; losses only booked when a default looks likely).
- Trading book = things held for trading or market making. These are marked to market every day, meaning their value is updated to current market prices, and the daily gains/losses go straight into profit and loss.

Analogy: the banking book is like the house you live in (you don't re-price it daily). The trading book is like stocks in a brokerage app whose value you see refreshed every single day.

Why the others are wrong:
- A and C describe the BANKING book (historical/amortized cost), not the trading book.
- D is wrong — trading positions are on the balance sheet and very much valued (daily).

📌 Exam tip: Trading book = mark-to-market daily, market-risk capital rules. Banking book = cost-based accounting, credit-risk capital rules. Exam loves the regulatory-arbitrage trap of shifting assets between books.

**Q7.** A "Chinese wall" inside a universal bank is best described as:
- A. A regulatory minimum capital requirement set by the Basel Committee
- B. An information barrier preventing the flow of confidential information between departments
- C. A reserve fund held against expected loan losses
- D. A limit on how much a single client can borrow

**Answer: B**

The question asks what a Chinese wall (also called an information barrier) is.

A big bank does many things at once — lending, trading, research, advising on takeovers. Some departments learn secret information that other departments could abuse. A Chinese wall is a set of rules plus physical and IT separation that stops information from flowing between departments, backed by compliance monitoring.

Analogy: in a school, the teachers grading your exam are kept separate from the teachers who are your close family friends, so nobody can pass along answers or favors. The "wall" keeps the information from leaking.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is regulatory capital — unrelated to information barriers.
- C is a loan-loss reserve/provision — about money set aside, not information control.
- D is a credit limit — about lending size, not confidentiality.

📌 Exam tip: Chinese wall = information barrier to manage conflicts of interest (e.g., research vs. underwriting, banking vs. trading). It controls information flow, not capital.

**Q8.** In a Dutch auction IPO (the method Google used in 2004), how is the final price for the shares determined?
- A. The underwriter sets a fixed price before any bids are collected
- B. Each successful bidder pays the individual price they personally bid
- C. The clearing price is the highest price at which all shares can be sold, and every successful bidder pays that single price
- D. The lowest submitted bid becomes the price everyone pays

**Answer: C**

The question asks how the price is set in a Dutch auction IPO.

Investors submit bids saying how many shares they want and at what price. The shares are filled starting from the highest bids downward until all shares are allocated. The price at which the last share is sold (so that all shares are placed) becomes the single "clearing price," and EVERY successful bidder pays that same clearing price — even those who bid higher.

Analogy: imagine selling 100 concert tickets. You line up offers from highest to lowest and keep accepting until all 100 are gone. The price of the 100th ticket sets the price, and everyone who got in pays that one price.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes a traditional fixed-price offering, not a Dutch auction.
- B (pay-your-own-bid) is wrong — a Dutch auction uses a single uniform clearing price for everyone.
- D is backwards — the clearing price is the highest price at which all shares can be sold, not the lowest bid.

📌 Exam tip: Dutch auction = single uniform clearing price = highest price that sells all the shares. Its goal is to let the market set the price and reduce the underpricing common in normal IPOs.

**Q9.** A bank's $10 billion loan portfolio has an expected loss of 0.8% per year and a 99.9th-percentile one-year loss of 3.0%. How is the expected loss portion covered?
- A. By economic capital (equity)
- B. By the interest spread charged on loans (pricing)
- C. By the deposit insurance fund
- D. By regulatory capital only

**Answer: B**

The question asks who/what pays for the EXPECTED loss part.

Expected loss is the average loss the bank already anticipates. Because the bank knows it is coming on average, it builds it into the interest rate it charges borrowers — the loan spread. It is treated as a routine cost of doing business, like spoilage in a grocery store priced into the products.

Numbers for context (not required to answer): EL = 0.8% × \$10B = \$80 million, and that \$80M is recovered through loan pricing. Capital (about 3.0% − 0.8% = 2.2%, i.e., \$220M) covers the UNEXPECTED loss beyond the average.

Analogy: a bakery knows a few loaves will burn each day, so it raises prices slightly to cover that expected waste. It doesn't dip into emergency savings for the everyday burnt loaves.

Why the others are wrong:
- A and D — capital (economic or regulatory) covers UNEXPECTED loss, not expected loss.
- C — the deposit insurance fund only steps in if the bank fails; it does not cover routine expected losses.

📌 Exam tip: Expected loss → covered by pricing/provisions. Unexpected loss → covered by capital. Memorize this split; it's the foundation of bank capital questions.

**Q10.** In a best-efforts public offering, who bears the risk that the securities cannot all be sold at the offer price?
- A. The investment bank (underwriter)
- B. The issuer (the company raising capital)
- C. The central counterparty
- D. The retail investors who buy the shares

**Answer: B**

The question asks who carries the risk of a shortfall under a best-efforts arrangement.

In a best-efforts deal, the bank does NOT buy the securities. It simply agrees to try its hardest to sell them at the offer price and earns a fee on what it sells. If demand is weak and not everything sells, that's the issuer's problem — the issuer just raises less money. The bank's fee revenue is modest but never negative.

Contrast with firm commitment, where the bank buys the whole issue and bears the price risk itself.

Analogy: best efforts is like a real-estate agent on commission — they try to sell your house but don't buy it themselves, so if it doesn't sell, you (the owner) bear that. Firm commitment is like someone buying your house outright to resell.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the firm-commitment case, not best efforts.
- C (central counterparty) belongs to exchange-traded derivatives clearing, not underwriting.
- D — investors choose whether to buy; they don't bear the issuer's shortfall risk.

📌 Exam tip: Best efforts → ISSUER bears shortfall risk (bank just earns a fee). Firm commitment → BANK bears the price risk. This pair shows up constantly.
