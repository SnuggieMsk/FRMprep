**Q1.** A 1-year corporate bond (face $100) pays an 8% annual coupon and trades at par ($100). The probability of default during the year is 4%; in default the bond recovers 40% of face value and pays no coupon. The expected return is closest to:
- A. 8.00%
- B. 5.28%
- C. 5.00%
- D. 2.72%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the EXPECTED return — the probability-weighted average of what you actually get, including the chance of default. This is always below the promised 8%.

Step 1 — payoff in each state.
- No default (probability 96%): you get coupon + face $$= 8 + 100 = 108$$.
- Default (probability 4%): you get 40% of face only $$= 0.40 \times 100 = 40$$.

Step 2 — expected payoff (weight each by its probability):
$$E[\text{payoff}] = 0.96 \times 108 + 0.04 \times 40$$
$$= 103.68 + 1.60 = 105.28$$

Step 3 — expected return (compared to the $100 you paid):
$$E[r] = \frac{105.28}{100} - 1 = 0.0528 = 5.28\%$$

So B is correct.

Plain words: 8% is what they PROMISE if all goes well. But 4 times out of 100, things go badly and you get only 40. Averaging the good and bad outcomes pulls your realistic return down to 5.28%.

- A (8.00%) is the PROMISED yield, which assumes no default — the classic trap.
- C (5.00%) ignores that recovery (40) still pays something; it's an unweighted guess.
- D (2.72%) is the expected LOSS rate (8.00% − 5.28%), not the expected return itself.

📌 Exam tip: For any defaultable bond, expected return < promised yield. If a question gives you a default probability and recovery, it wants the EXPECTED return, not the headline coupon/yield.

**Q2.** The lowest rating that still qualifies as investment grade is:
- A. A3 / A−
- B. Ba1 / BB+
- C. Baa3 / BBB−
- D. B1 / B+

**Answer: C**

The question asks where the line is drawn between "investment grade" (safer) and "high yield / junk" (riskier).

Why C is correct: the investment-grade floor is Baa3 on Moody's scale and BBB− on S&P/Fitch. Anything at or above this is investment grade; one notch below (Ba1 / BB+) is the top of high yield.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (A3 / A−) is investment grade, but it is NOT the lowest such rating — Baa is below A.
- B (Ba1 / BB+) is the HIGHEST high-yield rating — just below the line, not on it.
- D (B1 / B+) is solidly high yield (junk), well below the boundary.

Memory aid: the cutoff is "triple-B-minus" (BBB−) / "Baa-three." Think "BBB = Barely But Bond-worthy" — still investment grade. Drop to BB and you've fallen into junk.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the boundary cold: Baa3 / BBB− = lowest investment grade; Ba1 / BB+ = highest high yield. The exam loves to test the one-notch difference.

**Q3.** A company deposits a portfolio of Treasury securities with a trustee that exactly replicates the remaining cash flows of an outstanding bond issue, removing the debt from its balance sheet. This is:
- A. A sinking fund provision
- B. A make-whole call
- C. A tender offer
- D. Defeasance

**Answer: D**

The question describes funding a trust with Treasuries that mimic the bond's payments so the debt is treated as gone.

Why D is correct: defeasance is exactly this — the issuer sets aside a Treasury portfolio whose cash flows match the bond's remaining cash flows, so the obligation is economically (and often legally) extinguished and taken off the balance sheet. Bondholders effectively end up backed by Treasuries.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — a sinking fund retires bonds GRADUALLY over time with cash (buying back or calling by lottery), not by a one-time Treasury trust.
- B — a make-whole call REDEEMS the bond by paying the present value of remaining cash flows (discounted at Treasury rate + small spread); it pays bondholders off, it doesn't fund a replicating trust.
- C — a tender offer is an OFFER TO BUY BACK bonds, often to remove covenants; the bonds are repurchased, not defeased.

Analogy: defeasance is like prepaying your phone bill for the next two years into a locked account — the debt is functionally settled even though payments still flow out over time.

📌 Exam tip: "Treasury portfolio in trust replicating cash flows + removed from balance sheet" = defeasance. Don't confuse it with the make-whole call, which is a redemption at a PV-based price.

**Q4.** Which statement about recovery rates is correct?
- A. They are highest for subordinated debentures because of their higher coupons
- B. They tend to be negatively correlated with default rates over the credit cycle
- C. They are fixed at 40% by market convention
- D. They are independent of seniority within the capital structure

**Answer: B**

The question asks about how much investors recover after a default and how that behaves.

Why B is correct: recovery rates and default rates move in OPPOSITE directions over the cycle. In recessions, lots of firms default (high default rates) AND the assets backing those firms are cheap and hard to sell (low recoveries). So defaults rise just as recoveries fall, which doubles the pain — a "negative correlation."

Why the others are wrong:
- A — subordinated debentures rank LOW in the capital structure and recover the LEAST, not the most. A higher coupon does not improve recovery.
- C — 40% is a common assumption for examples, but actual recovery varies a lot by seniority, security, and the economy; it is not a fixed convention.
- D — seniority matters enormously: first-mortgage/senior secured debt recovers most, subordinated debt least.

Analogy: in a fire sale (recession), more houses go up for sale AND each sells for less. Both bad things happen together.

📌 Exam tip: Remember "recoveries and defaults are negatively correlated" — recessions bring high defaults AND low recoveries, so the default loss rate (≈ default rate × (1 − recovery)) spikes in bad times.

**Q5.** The corporate trustee under a bond indenture is best described as:
- A. A guarantor who promises to pay bondholders if the issuer defaults
- B. A third party who enforces the indenture terms and acts in bondholders' interest
- C. The lead underwriter who sells the bonds to investors
- D. A rating agency that assigns the bond's credit rating

**Answer: B**

The question asks what role the trustee plays in a bond deal.

Why B is correct: thousands of scattered bondholders cannot individually watch the issuer. So a trustee (usually a bank's trust department) is appointed to monitor compliance with the indenture's covenants and to act on the bondholders' behalf, especially in a default. The trustee is a watchdog for the contract.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — the trustee does NOT guarantee payment. It only acts within the powers the indenture grants; it is not on the hook for the issuer's debt.
- C — that is the underwriter (investment bank), a separate role at issuance.
- D — credit ratings come from agencies like Moody's, S&P, and Fitch, not the trustee.

Analogy: the trustee is like a referee who enforces the rules of the game on behalf of the fans (bondholders) — but the referee does not pay the prize money if a team loses.

📌 Exam tip: Trustee = watchdog/enforcer of the indenture, NOT a guarantor. "Acts in bondholders' interest within the powers granted" is the GARP-correct phrasing.

**Q6.** A debenture is a corporate bond that is:
- A. Secured by a first lien on the issuer's real property
- B. Secured by financial assets deposited with a trustee
- C. Unsecured, backed only by the issuer's general credit
- D. Backed by title to specific equipment held in trust

**Answer: C**

The question asks what kind of backing (collateral) a debenture has.

Why C is correct: in US usage, a debenture is an UNSECURED bond — there is no specific collateral pledged. It is backed only by the issuer's general creditworthiness. Subordinated debentures rank even lower, below other unsecured debt.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — that describes a MORTGAGE BOND (lien on real assets like property/plant).
- B — that describes a COLLATERAL TRUST BOND (financial assets like subsidiary stocks/bonds deposited with a trustee).
- D — that describes an EQUIPMENT TRUST CERTIFICATE (title to aircraft, rail cars, etc., held by a trustee).

Important nuance: because collateral ranks claims only WITHIN an issuer, a first-mortgage bond of a weak company can still be riskier than a debenture of a very strong company.

📌 Exam tip: In US terminology, "debenture = unsecured." Match the collateral words: real property = mortgage bond; financial assets = collateral trust; equipment title = equipment trust certificate.

**Q7.** A 10-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon (face $100) and trades at $90. Its current yield is closest to:
- A. 6.00%
- B. 6.67%
- C. 7.37%
- D. 5.40%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the CURRENT yield, which is simply the annual coupon divided by the current price. It ignores any gain at maturity and time value.

Formula:
$$\text{Current yield} = \frac{\text{Annual coupon}}{\text{Price}}$$

The annual coupon is $$6\% \times 100 = \$6$$. Plug in:
$$\text{CY} = \frac{6}{90} = 0.0667 = 6.67\%$$

So B is correct.

Plain words: current yield just asks, "for each dollar I pay today, how much coupon income do I get this year?" You pay 90 and get 6, which is 6.67%.

- A (6.00%) is the COUPON rate (coupon ÷ face), not coupon ÷ price. Since the bond trades below face, the current yield must be higher than 6%.
- C (7.37%) is roughly the YIELD TO MATURITY, which also captures the pull-to-par gain from $90 up to $100 — a different and higher measure.
- D (5.40%) divides by 111.1 or makes a similar inversion error; no correct method gives it.

📌 Exam tip: For a DISCOUNT bond (price < face), the ordering is: coupon rate < current yield < YTM. For a premium bond it reverses. Use this to sanity-check your answer.

**Q8.** Which feature of a high-yield bond lets the issuer pay coupons in additional bonds instead of cash during the early, cash-tight years of a leveraged deal?
- A. Step-up bond
- B. Payment-in-kind (PIK) bond
- C. Deferred-interest (zero-coupon) bond
- D. Make-whole call

**Answer: B**

The question describes paying interest using MORE BONDS rather than cash.

Why B is correct: a payment-in-kind (PIK) bond gives the issuer the option to pay coupons in additional bonds (more debt) instead of cash. This preserves cash early in a leveraged deal when the company is short on funds.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — a step-up bond starts with a LOW cash coupon that INCREASES on a schedule; the coupon is still paid in cash, just at a rising rate.
- C — a deferred-interest (zero-coupon) bond pays NO coupons for an initial period and is sold at a deep discount; it does not pay in extra bonds.
- D — a make-whole call is a redemption feature, not a coupon-payment method.

Analogy: a PIK coupon is like paying your friend back with more IOUs instead of real cash — the debt grows but no money leaves your pocket today.

📌 Exam tip: PIK = "pay in kind" = pay coupons with MORE BONDS. Step-up = rising cash coupon. Deferred/zero = no coupons at first, deep discount. Keep the three straight.

**Q9.** A call provision in a corporate bond generally benefits the:
- A. Bondholder, when interest rates rise
- B. Issuer, when interest rates fall
- C. Trustee, by reducing monitoring duties
- D. Rating agency, by simplifying the rating

**Answer: B**

The question asks who gains from the issuer's right to redeem the bond early (the call option).

Why B is correct: a call lets the ISSUER buy back the bond at a stated call price. When rates FALL, the issuer can call the old high-coupon bond and refinance at the new lower rate — saving interest. So the call benefits the issuer in a falling-rate environment. To compensate bondholders for this disadvantage, callable bonds carry a higher yield, and deferred calls give some "call protection."

Why the others are wrong:
- A — bondholders are HURT, not helped, by a call: their good high-coupon bond gets taken away just when rates fall and they would have wanted to keep it.
- C — the trustee's duties are unrelated to whether the bond is callable.
- D — a call feature does not exist to simplify ratings.

Analogy: a call is like the issuer's right to refinance a mortgage when rates drop — great for the borrower (issuer), annoying for the lender (bondholder) who loses a high-paying loan.

📌 Exam tip: "Call benefits the issuer when rates fall." The mirror image: a put benefits the bondholder when rates rise. Track who holds the option and which direction helps them.

**Q10.** A downgrade from A to BBB widens a bond's credit spread and produces a mark-to-market loss, even though the bond has NOT defaulted. This loss is an example of:
- A. Credit default risk
- B. Credit spread risk
- C. Interest rate risk
- D. Liquidity risk

**Answer: B**

The question describes a loss caused by a rating change widening the spread — not by an actual default.

Why B is correct: credit SPREAD risk is the risk that the extra yield investors demand for a bond's credit quality (the spread over the risk-free rate) widens, lowering the bond's price. A downgrade widens the spread and causes a mark-to-market loss even with no default. That is precisely credit spread risk.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — credit DEFAULT risk is the risk the issuer actually fails to pay. Here there is no default, just a downgrade and repricing.
- C — interest rate risk is loss from a change in the general level of risk-free rates, not from a credit downgrade.
- D — liquidity risk relates to difficulty trading the bond without moving its price, not to a rating-driven spread change.

Analogy: your house didn't burn down (no default), but the neighborhood got a worse safety reputation (downgrade), so its market value dropped today (spread widening). You feel the loss even though nothing physically happened to the house.

📌 Exam tip: Downgrade + spread widening + mark-to-market loss WITHOUT default = credit SPREAD risk. Reserve "credit default risk" for the issuer actually missing a payment.
