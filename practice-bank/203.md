**Q1.** Operational events arrive at a Poisson rate of 1.5 per month. The probability of exactly 2 events next month is closest to:
- A. 18.7%
- B. 22.3%
- C. 25.1%
- D. 33.5%

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the chance of exactly 2 events when events arrive randomly at an average rate $$\lambda = 1.5$$ per month — a Poisson calculation.

The Poisson probability formula is:

$$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$

Plug in $$\lambda = 1.5$$, $$k = 2$$:

$$P(X = 2) = \frac{1.5^2 \, e^{-1.5}}{2!} = \frac{2.25 \times e^{-1.5}}{2}$$

Now $$e^{-1.5} \approx 0.2231$$, so:

$$P(X = 2) = \frac{2.25 \times 0.2231}{2} = \frac{0.5020}{2} = 0.2510 \approx 25.1\%$$

So the answer is 25.1% (option C).

- A (18.7%) uses the wrong $$\lambda$$ or $$k$$ (e.g., computing for $$k=3$$ at $$\lambda=2$$).
- B (22.3%) is roughly $$e^{-1.5} = 0.2231$$, i.e., $$P(X=0)$$, not $$P(X=2)$$.
- D (33.5%) is roughly $$P(X=1) = 1.5 \times e^{-1.5} = 0.3347$$, off by one count.

📌 Exam tip: For Poisson, plug carefully into $$\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$. Remember $$2! = 2$$ and $$3! = 6$$. The mean and variance of a Poisson are both $$\lambda$$.

**Q2.** Which distribution is bounded below by zero and right-skewed, and is the standard model for asset prices?
- A. Normal
- B. Student's t
- C. Lognormal
- D. Uniform

**Answer: C**

The question asks which distribution is used for prices (which can never be negative).

A variable is lognormal if its natural log is normally distributed. Because exponentiating a normal always gives a positive number, the lognormal lives strictly above zero and is right-skewed (a long tail to the high side). This matches asset prices, which can't go negative but can in principle rise without bound. So option C is correct.

The standard finance story: if *log returns* are normal, then the *price* is lognormal.

- A (Normal) is symmetric and allows negative values, so it can't model a strictly positive price.
- B (Student's t) is symmetric (centered at 0) and allows negatives; it's used for fat-tailed returns and test statistics.
- D (Uniform) is flat over a fixed interval, not right-skewed, and not used for prices.

📌 Exam tip: Returns ≈ normal; prices ≈ lognormal. If "log returns are normal," the price is lognormal and never negative. This swap is a classic trap.

**Q3.** The sum of the squares of 12 independent standard normal variables follows which distribution, and with what mean and variance?
- A. $$N(0, 12)$$; mean 0, variance 12
- B. $$\chi^2_{12}$$; mean 12, variance 24
- C. $$t_{12}$$; mean 0, variance 1.2
- D. $$F_{12,12}$$; mean 1, variance undefined

**Answer: B**

The question describes summing squared standard normal variables.

By definition, the sum of $$k$$ independent squared standard normals is a chi-squared distribution with $$k$$ degrees of freedom:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k} Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2_k$$

Here $$k = 12$$. A chi-squared with $$k$$ degrees of freedom has:
- Mean $$= k = 12$$
- Variance $$= 2k = 2 \times 12 = 24$$

So option B is correct.

- A treats the sum as a normal; squaring and summing destroys the normal shape (the result is positive and right-skewed).
- C names the t-distribution, which is built as a normal divided by a scaled chi-squared root — not a sum of squares.
- D names the F-distribution, which is a *ratio* of two chi-squareds, not a single sum of squares.

📌 Exam tip: Chi-squared = sum of $$k$$ squared standard normals; mean $$k$$, variance $$2k$$. The t and F are *built from* normals and chi-squareds but are not the same thing.

**Q4.** A portfolio holds 10 independent loans, each with default probability 4%. What is the expected number of defaults in a year?
- A. 0.04
- B. 0.40
- C. 0.96
- D. 4.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the mean number of defaults, where each loan defaults independently with probability $$p = 0.04$$ — a binomial setup with $$n = 10$$.

The mean (expected value) of a binomial is:

$$E[X] = n \times p = 10 \times 0.04 = 0.40$$

So we expect 0.40 defaults on average per year (option B). It's fine for an expected value to be a fraction — it's a long-run average across many years.

- A (0.04) is just $$p$$, forgetting to multiply by the number of loans $$n = 10$$.
- C (0.96) is $$1 - p$$ (the survival probability of one loan), unrelated to the expected count.
- D (4.00) divides incorrectly or treats $$p$$ as 40%; with $$p = 4\%$$ the expected count is 0.40, not 4.

📌 Exam tip: Binomial mean = $$np$$; variance = $$np(1-p)$$. Make sure you convert the percentage (4% = 0.04) before multiplying.

**Q5.** Monthly returns are normally distributed with mean 1% and standard deviation 5%. What is the probability of a return below $$-7.25\%$$? (Use: 5% of a normal lies below $$z = -1.645$$.)
- A. About 1%
- B. About 2.5%
- C. About 5%
- D. About 10%

**Answer: C**

The question asks for a left-tail probability for a normal variable. First convert the cutoff into a z-score (how many standard deviations below the mean it is).

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{-7.25 - 1}{5} = \frac{-8.25}{5} = -1.65$$

A z-score of about $$-1.645$$ marks the 5% left tail of a normal distribution (a memorized value). Since $$-1.65 \approx -1.645$$:

$$P(X < -7.25\%) \approx 5\%$$

So the answer is option C. This is exactly the kind of calculation behind a 95% Value at Risk: the $$-7.25\%$$ return is the 5% quantile.

- A (1%) corresponds to $$z \approx -2.326$$, a deeper tail than we have.
- B (2.5%) corresponds to $$z \approx -1.96$$, again deeper than $$-1.65$$.
- D (10%) corresponds to $$z \approx -1.28$$, a shallower tail.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the one-tailed z-values: 1.645 → 5%, 1.96 → 2.5%, 2.326 → 1%. Standardize with $$z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$$, then read the tail.

**Q6.** System outages follow a Poisson process averaging $$\lambda = 2$$ per quarter. If outages are independent across quarters, what is the average number of outages per year?
- A. 2
- B. 4
- C. 8
- D. 16

**Answer: C**

The question asks us to scale a Poisson rate from a quarter to a full year.

A key Poisson property: counts over disjoint (non-overlapping) periods add, and the rate scales with the length of the interval. A year contains 4 quarters, so:

$$\lambda_{\text{year}} = 4 \times \lambda_{\text{quarter}} = 4 \times 2 = 8$$

So we expect 8 outages per year on average (option C). The annual count is also Poisson, now with $$\lambda = 8$$ (mean 8 and variance 8).

- A (2) forgets to scale and just reports the quarterly rate.
- B (4) scales by the wrong factor (e.g., treats a year as 2 quarters or doubles instead of quadrupling).
- D (16) confuses the rate with the variance times 2, or squares/over-scales the factor.

📌 Exam tip: Poisson rates are additive over time: multiply $$\lambda$$ by the number of equal sub-periods. 4 quarters per year → multiply by 4.

**Q7.** A Student's t-distribution, compared to a standard normal distribution, has:
- A. Thinner tails, so its critical values are smaller.
- B. Fatter tails, so its critical values are larger.
- C. Identical tails, differing only in mean.
- D. Fatter tails, but smaller critical values.

**Answer: B**

The question compares the t-distribution to the normal.

The Student's t-distribution is symmetric like the normal but has *fatter tails* — more probability sits out in the extremes. Because more mass is in the tails, you must go *further* from the center to capture a given confidence level. That means t critical values are *larger* than the corresponding normal (z) values. So option B is correct. As degrees of freedom grow, the t converges to the normal.

Practical effect: confidence intervals built with the t are *wider* than those built with the normal.

- A is backwards on both counts: t tails are fatter, not thinner, and its critical values are larger.
- C is false: the tails differ (that's the whole point); both have mean 0.
- D correctly notes fatter tails but then wrongly says smaller critical values. Fatter tails force *larger* critical values.

📌 Exam tip: t = fatter tails = larger critical values = wider confidence intervals. The classic trap claims t intervals are narrower; they are wider.

**Q8.** A 50/50 mixture of $$N(0, 1)$$ and $$N(0, 9)$$, compared to a single normal with the same overall variance, has:
- A. Negative skew
- B. Excess kurtosis (fatter tails)
- C. Kurtosis below 3
- D. An identical shape

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the shape of a mixture of two normals that share the same mean (0) but differ in variance (1 vs 9).

A mixture randomly draws from one component or the other. With equal means but different variances, the result is symmetric (so skew is 0), but it gains *excess kurtosis*: extra probability piles up both in the center (from the low-variance component) and in the far tails (from the high-variance component). Compared to a single normal with the same total variance, the mixture is leptokurtic — fatter tails. So option B is correct.

Intuition: most days are calm (small variance), but occasionally you draw from the wild high-variance regime, producing rare extreme values — that's a fat tail.

- A (negative skew) requires the components to have *different means*; here both means are 0, so the mixture stays symmetric.
- C (kurtosis below 3) is the opposite of what happens; variance mixtures increase kurtosis above 3.
- D (identical shape) is wrong — the mixture is more peaked and fatter-tailed than a single normal.

📌 Exam tip: A mixture of normals with equal means but different variances → symmetric but fat-tailed (excess kurtosis). Different means can add skew or bimodality.

**Q9.** A portfolio of 10 independent loans each defaults with probability 4%. What is the variance of the number of defaults?
- A. 0.40
- B. 0.384
- C. 0.96
- D. 4.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the variance of a binomial count with $$n = 10$$ and $$p = 0.04$$.

The variance of a binomial is:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = n \, p \, (1 - p)$$

Plug in:

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = 10 \times 0.04 \times (1 - 0.04) = 10 \times 0.04 \times 0.96$$

$$= 0.40 \times 0.96 = 0.384$$

So the variance is 0.384 (option B).

- A (0.40) is the *mean* $$np$$, forgetting the $$(1-p)$$ factor.
- C (0.96) is just $$(1-p)$$, ignoring $$n$$ and $$p$$.
- D (4.00) misreads $$p$$ as 40% or mis-scales by a factor of 10.

📌 Exam tip: Binomial: mean $$= np$$, variance $$= np(1-p)$$. Don't stop at the mean — the variance needs the extra $$(1-p)$$ factor.

**Q10.** Events arrive at a Poisson rate of $$\lambda = 2$$ per quarter. What is the probability of zero events in a quarter? (Use $$e^{-2} \approx 0.1353$$.)
- A. 0.00%
- B. 13.5%
- C. 27.1%
- D. 50.0%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the probability of seeing no events when the average is $$\lambda = 2$$ per quarter.

Use the Poisson formula with $$k = 0$$:

$$P(X = 0) = \frac{\lambda^0 e^{-\lambda}}{0!} = \frac{1 \times e^{-2}}{1} = e^{-2}$$

Recall that anything to the power 0 is 1, and $$0! = 1$$. So:

$$P(X = 0) = e^{-2} \approx 0.1353 = 13.5\%$$

So the answer is 13.5% (option B). Even with an average of 2 events, there's a meaningful chance of a quiet quarter with none.

- A (0.00%) wrongly assumes zero events is impossible — it isn't.
- C (27.1%) is $$2 \times e^{-2} = 0.2707$$, which is actually $$P(X = 1)$$, not $$P(X = 0)$$.
- D (50.0%) is a guess unrelated to the formula.

📌 Exam tip: For Poisson, $$P(X=0) = e^{-\lambda}$$ always (because $$\lambda^0 = 1$$ and $$0! = 1$$). Keep $$e^{-\lambda}$$ handy — it anchors many Poisson tail calculations.
