**Q1.** Which statement best describes how enterprise risk management (ERM) differs from a traditional silo approach?
- A. ERM produces more separate risk reports, one per risk type
- B. ERM manages all material risks together as one portfolio against a single firm-wide risk appetite
- C. ERM removes the need for any business-line risk owners
- D. ERM only measures market risk, leaving other risks to the silos

**Answer: B**

The question is asking for the core idea of ERM versus the old silo style.

In a silo approach, each kind of risk (market, credit, operational, and so on) is handled by its own separate team, and no one looks at how the risks add up across the whole firm. ERM instead pulls everything together: all material risks are viewed as a single portfolio and judged against ONE board-approved risk appetite, with risk tied to strategy and capital. That is exactly what B says, so B is correct.

Analogy: silos are like each player on a soccer team watching only their own patch of grass; ERM is like a coach watching the whole field at once.

- A is wrong: ERM is not about producing MORE reports; it is about one integrated, aggregated view. More paperwork is not the point.
- C is wrong: ERM still relies on business-line risk owners; it adds firm-wide coordination on top, it does not abolish local ownership.
- D is wrong: ERM covers ALL material risks (market, credit, operational, liquidity, strategic, reputational), not just market risk.

📌 Exam tip: The one-line definition to memorize: ERM = all risks as one portfolio, against one firm-wide risk appetite, linked to strategy and capital.

**Q2.** Which of the following is a HEDGING-COST argument in favor of adopting ERM?
- A. ERM removes the need to hedge at all
- B. By netting natural offsets across the firm before hedging, ERM avoids over-hedging and lowers hedge cost
- C. Silo hedging is illegal under Basel III
- D. ERM guarantees lower derivative bid-ask spreads from dealers

**Answer: B**

The question asks specifically for the argument about saving money on hedging.

When each silo hedges its own risk separately, two desks might be hedging exposures that already cancel each other out. For example, one desk is long an exposure and another is short the same thing — together the firm is roughly flat, but each silo buys protection anyway. ERM looks across the firm first, NETS those natural offsets, and only hedges the leftover net exposure. Less hedging bought means lower cost. So B is correct.

Analogy: if one family member turns the heater up while another opens the window, coordinating first saves the energy bill.

- A is wrong: ERM does not eliminate hedging; it makes hedging more efficient.
- C is wrong: silo hedging is not illegal under Basel III; that is a made-up claim.
- D is wrong: ERM does not guarantee tighter dealer spreads; the saving comes from hedging less, not from cheaper quotes.

📌 Exam tip: The hedging-cost benefit of ERM is "net first, then hedge only the leftover." It reduces over-hedging, not the existence of hedging.

**Q3.** Under best-practice ERM governance, who has ultimate ownership of the firm's risk appetite?
- A. The head of the trading desk
- B. The external auditor
- C. The board of directors
- D. Individual line managers acting independently

**Answer: C**

The question asks who is ultimately responsible for approving and owning the firm's risk appetite.

In sound ERM, the board of directors approves the risk appetite and holds management accountable to it. The appetite then cascades down into limits and policies across all risk types, and a CRO (Chief Risk Officer) with firm-wide stature oversees the framework day to day. But the buck stops at the board. So C is correct.

Analogy: the board sets the speed limit for the whole company; the CRO is the traffic officer who makes sure everyone obeys it.

- A is wrong: a trading desk head runs one part of the business; they do not own the firm-wide appetite.
- B is wrong: the external auditor checks the books from outside; they do not set or own risk appetite.
- D is wrong: line managers operating independently is the silo problem ERM is meant to fix; appetite must be set centrally, not by each manager alone.

📌 Exam tip: Board approves and owns the risk appetite; the CRO implements and oversees it with an independent reporting line.

**Q4.** Risk culture is best described as:
- A. The set of formal limit documents a firm files with regulators
- B. The shared attitudes, values, and behaviors that drive how people actually act on risk, especially when no one is watching
- C. The amount of economic capital a firm holds
- D. The number of risk committees a firm operates

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the meaning of "risk culture."

Risk culture is about behavior, not paperwork. It is the shared attitudes, values, and habits that decide what employees really do when faced with risk — including what they do when no supervisor is looking. A firm can have perfect written policies but still behave badly. So B captures the idea.

Analogy: a household can have a "no junk food" rule on the fridge door, but the culture is whether everyone actually follows it when home alone.

- A is wrong: formal limit documents are part of the framework, not the culture. Culture is how people behave around those documents.
- C is wrong: economic capital is a number measuring risk capacity, not a description of culture.
- D is wrong: counting committees measures structure, not the lived behaviors that define culture.

📌 Exam tip: Culture questions are answered by behavior, not documents. A great framework with a weak culture still fails (Barings, SocGen, the London Whale all had limit frameworks).

**Q5.** Which observation most strongly signals a WEAK risk culture?
- A. The CRO reports directly to the board's risk committee
- B. Junior staff are afraid to escalate concerns about a highly profitable desk
- C. Compensation includes deferred, risk-adjusted components
- D. The board receives a quarterly aggregated risk report

**Answer: B**

The question asks which sign points to a poor risk culture.

A hallmark of a strong culture is that bad news travels up quickly and people feel safe to challenge or escalate ("psychological safety," no shooting the messenger). If junior staff are scared to raise concerns about a profitable desk, that is exactly the opposite — an untouchable profit center where problems get buried. That is a classic red flag, so B is correct.

Analogy: in a healthy kitchen, the new cook can tell the head chef "this chicken smells off." In a toxic one, they stay silent and someone gets sick.

- A is wrong: a CRO reporting directly to the board's risk committee is a sign of GOOD governance (independence), not weakness.
- C is wrong: deferred, risk-adjusted pay is a good practice — it rewards sustainable, prudent performance.
- D is wrong: the board receiving an aggregated risk report is healthy ERM behavior, not a warning sign.

📌 Exam tip: Suppressed escalation and "untouchable" profitable desks are the textbook culture red flags. Look for behavior that silences bad news.

**Q6.** A key advantage of scenario analysis over a standard VaR model is that scenario analysis:
- A. Produces a precise probability for each loss outcome
- B. Requires no assumptions or management judgment
- C. Can capture coherent, multi-factor extreme events that may never have appeared in historical data
- D. Always gives a smaller estimated loss than VaR

**Answer: C**

The question asks for a genuine strength of scenario analysis relative to VaR.

Scenario analysis describes a specific adverse state of the world (say, a cyber attack combined with a funding freeze) and moves MANY risk factors together in a coherent story. It is forward-looking, so it can include events that have never been seen in the data — which a history-based model like VaR can miss. So C is the real advantage.

Analogy: VaR is like predicting tomorrow's weather from past records; scenario analysis is like asking "what if a hurricane AND a heatwave hit at once?" even if that exact combo never happened before.

- A is wrong: scenario analysis does NOT attach precise probabilities to outcomes; that is actually one of its disadvantages.
- B is wrong: scenarios rely heavily on judgment to choose and build them; "no assumptions" is false.
- D is wrong: there is no rule that scenario losses are always smaller than VaR; often they reveal LARGER tail losses.

📌 Exam tip: Scenario analysis strengths = forward-looking, multi-factor, captures tail interactions. Weaknesses = subjective choice, no probabilities attached, resource-intensive.

**Q7.** Which is a recognized DISADVANTAGE of scenario analysis?
- A. It produces objective probabilities that cannot be questioned
- B. The choice of scenarios is subjective, risking a "failure of imagination"
- C. It is mathematically guaranteed to capture every possible crisis
- D. It is free of any modeling assumptions

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a true weakness of scenario analysis.

Because humans pick which scenarios to test, they may simply fail to imagine the crisis that actually happens. This subjectivity — the "failure of imagination" — is a well-known drawback. Scenarios also lack attached probabilities and can be resource-intensive. So B is correct.

Analogy: a fire drill only prepares you for the fires you thought of; if you never imagined a flood, the flood still catches you off guard.

- A is wrong: scenario analysis does NOT produce objective, unquestionable probabilities; it attaches no probabilities at all.
- C is wrong: nothing guarantees every crisis is captured; that is the very flaw being described.
- D is wrong: translating a scenario into profit-and-loss requires many modeling assumptions, so it is not assumption-free.

📌 Exam tip: The big knock against scenario analysis is subjectivity (you only test what you think of) plus no probabilities. Pair these with its forward-looking strength.

**Q8.** In regulatory stress testing and capital planning (for example, CCAR/DFAST), scenario analysis is used primarily to:
- A. Replace a bank's entire accounting system
- B. Project losses, revenues, and capital ratios under prescribed adverse scenarios to confirm the bank stays above minimum capital
- C. Eliminate the need for any economic capital model
- D. Set the bank's daily trading limits automatically

**Answer: B**

The question asks what scenario analysis does inside a stress-testing and capital-planning program.

Regulators hand banks a set of severe but plausible macro scenarios. The bank must translate each scenario into projected losses, revenues, and capital ratios over a multi-year horizon, and then show that it would still stay ABOVE the minimum required capital even under that stress. Passing also affects whether the bank can pay dividends or buy back shares. So B is correct.

Analogy: it is like a stress test for the heart — the doctor pushes you on a treadmill (the adverse scenario) to check you stay healthy under strain.

- A is wrong: stress testing does not replace accounting systems; it is a forward-looking risk exercise.
- C is wrong: scenario results sit ON TOP of (complement) economic capital models; they do not eliminate them.
- D is wrong: daily trading limits are a different control; stress tests look at multi-year, firm-wide capital adequacy.

📌 Exam tip: Stress-test purpose = "do we stay above minimum capital under a severe scenario?" Results feed the capital buffer and can gate dividends/buybacks.

**Q9.** Which of the following is a common OBSTACLE or cost when implementing ERM?
- A. It is impossible to assign any executive accountability
- B. Data and systems integration is expensive, and there can be turf resistance from business units
- C. ERM is prohibited for banks under most regulations
- D. ERM automatically removes all hard-to-quantify risks

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a realistic difficulty of putting ERM in place.

Bringing every risk into one consistent, firm-wide view requires integrating lots of data and systems, which is costly. On top of that, business units may resist giving up control of "their" risk (turf wars). Other challenges include forcing hard-to-quantify risks into one framework and the danger of false comfort if the aggregate model is wrong. So B is correct.

Analogy: merging many separate spreadsheets from different departments into one master file is expensive and people often don't want to hand theirs over.

- A is wrong: ERM actually assigns clear accountability, typically to a CRO; lack of accountability is not the obstacle.
- C is wrong: ERM is encouraged, not prohibited; regulators and rating agencies assess ERM quality.
- D is wrong: ERM does not magically remove hard-to-quantify risks; struggling to fit them in is itself a challenge.

📌 Exam tip: ERM costs/obstacles = expensive data/systems integration, turf resistance, hard-to-quantify risks, and false comfort if the aggregate model is wrong.

**Q10.** Which of the following is a marker of a STRONG risk culture according to common (FSB-style) guidance?
- A. Leaders only send memos about risk but behave differently themselves
- B. Tone from the top, where leaders model good risk behavior and bad news can travel up safely
- C. Compensation that rewards short-term profit regardless of risk taken
- D. A culture where questioning a star trader is discouraged

**Answer: B**

The question asks which feature signals a healthy risk culture.

A strong risk culture has several markers: genuine tone from the top (leaders actually behave the way they preach), clear accountability, effective communication and challenge (dissent is safe and bad news rises quickly), and incentives that reward risk-adjusted performance. Option B describes tone from the top plus safe escalation, which are core strong-culture markers. So B is correct.

Analogy: a good captain doesn't just post safety rules — they wear the life jacket too, and they thank the sailor who shouts "iceberg."

- A is wrong: leaders saying one thing and doing another is the OPPOSITE of genuine tone from the top.
- C is wrong: rewarding short-term profit regardless of risk encourages reckless behavior — a weak-culture trait.
- D is wrong: discouraging challenge of a star trader is a classic weak-culture red flag.

📌 Exam tip: Strong-culture markers = tone from the top (lead by example), accountability, safe challenge/communication, and risk-adjusted incentives.
