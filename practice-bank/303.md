**Q1.** An open-end mutual fund holds $250 million of securities and cash, owes $10 million in accrued fees and payables, and has 12 million shares outstanding. What is its net asset value (NAV) per share?
- A. $20.83
- B. $20.00
- C. $21.67
- D. $18.40

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the NAV per share, which is simply the fund's net worth divided by the number of shares.

The formula:
$$\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}$$

Plug in the numbers:
$$\text{NAV} = \frac{250 - 10}{12} = \frac{240}{12} = 20.00$$

So NAV = $20.00 per share. Every purchase and redemption that day happens at $20.00.

Analogy: if a pizza is worth \$240 of toppings after you subtract the \$10 box cost, and you cut it into 12 slices, each slice is worth \$20.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($20.83) forgets to subtract liabilities: 250/12 = 20.83. Trap: ignoring the $10M owed.
- C ($21.67) adds the liability instead of subtracting: 260/12 = 21.67. Trap: wrong sign.
- D ($18.40) is just a distractor that doesn't match any sensible calculation.

📌 Exam tip: NAV = (Assets − Liabilities) / Shares. Always subtract liabilities first. Open-end funds always transact at NAV; that's their defining feature.

**Q2.** A closed-end fund holds assets worth $360M, has $24M of liabilities, and 16M shares outstanding. Its shares trade on the exchange at $18.90. The fund is trading at:
- A. Exactly NAV, by construction
- B. A 10% discount to its $21.00 NAV
- C. A 10% premium to its $21.00 NAV
- D. A 16% discount to its $22.50 NAV

**Answer: B**

The question asks whether the closed-end fund's market price is above or below its NAV, and by how much.

Step 1 — Find NAV:
$$\text{NAV} = \frac{360 - 24}{16} = \frac{336}{16} = 21.00$$

Step 2 — Compare the $18.90 market price to the $21.00 NAV:
$$\frac{18.90}{21.00} = 0.90 = 90\%$$
The price is 90% of NAV, which is 10% below NAV — a 10% discount.

Analogy: if a \$21 toy is selling in the store for \$18.90, it's marked down 10%.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the open-end fund rule, not closed-end. Closed-end funds CAN deviate from NAV.
- C (premium) is the wrong direction — $18.90 is below $21.00, so it's a discount, not a premium.
- D uses a wrong NAV ($22.50) and a wrong discount; the correct NAV is $21.00.

📌 Exam tip: Closed-end funds often trade at a DISCOUNT to NAV. Price below NAV = discount; price above NAV = premium. ETFs, by contrast, stay near NAV thanks to creation/redemption arbitrage.

**Q3.** Which feature best distinguishes an open-end mutual fund from a closed-end fund?
- A. An open-end fund trades intraday on an exchange; a closed-end fund trades once per day
- B. An open-end fund creates and redeems shares daily and always transacts at NAV; a closed-end fund has a fixed share count and trades on an exchange
- C. Only closed-end funds can hold stocks
- D. Open-end funds can be sold short intraday; closed-end funds cannot

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the core difference between these two fund types.

Open-end fund: the fund itself creates new shares when people buy and cancels shares when people redeem, so the number of shares changes daily. You always buy/sell directly with the fund at that day's NAV (set once per day at the 4 p.m. close).

Closed-end fund: after its IPO, the number of shares is FIXED. You buy and sell those shares from other investors on a stock exchange throughout the day, at a market price that can be above or below NAV.

Analogy: an open-end fund is like a water tank that refills/drains to match demand at a fixed posted price. A closed-end fund is like a limited print run of trading cards — fixed quantity, and the market sets the price.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is reversed — it's the closed-end fund (and ETFs) that trade intraday; open-end funds transact once per day at NAV.
- C is false — both types can hold stocks.
- D is reversed — you can short closed-end funds (exchange-traded) but not open-end mutual funds.

📌 Exam tip: Open-end = variable shares, transacts at NAV once daily. Closed-end = fixed shares, exchange-traded intraday, can sit at a premium/discount.

**Q4.** A mutual fund charges a 5% front-end load. On a $10,000 investment, how much actually gets invested, and roughly what return is needed just to break even on the load?
- A. $9,500 invested; needs about 5.26% to break even
- B. $9,500 invested; needs exactly 5.00% to break even
- C. $10,000 invested; the load is taken at redemption
- D. $10,500 invested; the load is added on top

**Answer: A**

The question asks how a front-end load reduces the invested amount and what return undoes it.

A front-end load is charged when you BUY. A 5% load on $10,000 means 5% is taken off the top:
$$\text{Load} = 0.05 \times 10{,}000 = 500$$
$$\text{Amount invested} = 10{,}000 - 500 = 9{,}500$$

To get back to your original \$10,000 from \$9,500, you need to grow \$9,500 by:
$$\frac{10{,}000 - 9{,}500}{9{,}500} = \frac{500}{9{,}500} = 0.0526 = 5.26\%$$

Notice the break-even return (5.26%) is bigger than the 5% load, because you must grow the SMALLER base ($9,500) back up to the full $10,000.

Analogy: if a gatekeeper takes \$5 of your \$100 before you enter, you only have \$95 inside, and you need to gain more than 5% on that \$95 to get back to \$100.

Why the others are wrong:
- B says "exactly 5.00%" — but 5% of $9,500 is only $475, not enough to recover the $500. You need 5.26%.
- C describes a back-end load (charged at redemption), not a front-end load.
- D is wrong — the load is taken OUT of your money, not added on top.

📌 Exam tip: Front-end load shrinks your invested base, so the break-even return is always a bit higher than the load percentage. Load% on the gross ≠ recovery% on the net.

**Q5.** Placing a mutual fund order AFTER the 4 p.m. close but still receiving that same day's NAV is known as:
- A. Market timing — discouraged but legal
- B. Late trading — illegal
- C. Front running — legal if disclosed
- D. Directed brokerage

**Answer: B**

The question asks to name the abusive practice of trading after the cutoff at the old price.

Late trading means submitting an order after the 4 p.m. NAV cutoff but still being given today's (already-set) NAV. This lets the trader act on news that came out after the close while paying a price that doesn't reflect that news — an unfair, illegal advantage.

Analogy: it's like being allowed to place your bet after the race has already finished, but at the pre-race odds. Obviously unfair and not allowed.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (market timing) is rapid in-and-out trading that exploits STALE prices, but it happens BEFORE the cutoff; it's prohibited by fund rules but is a different practice.
- C (front running) is trading ahead of a known large order — it is illegal, not "legal if disclosed."
- D (directed brokerage) is a kickback arrangement where the fund steers trades to a broker who pushes its shares — unrelated to the timing of orders.

📌 Exam tip: Late trading = after the cutoff, today's NAV, ILLEGAL. Market timing = before the cutoff, exploiting stale prices. Keep these two straight — they're easy to confuse.

**Q6.** A hedge fund charges a 20% incentive fee subject to a high-water mark (HWM) of $125M. After a losing year its AUM is $110M; this year it gains 20% (pre-fee). The incentive fee is closest to:
- A. $0
- B. $1.4M
- C. $4.4M
- D. $6.6M

**Answer: B**

The question asks how much incentive fee is owed when there's a high-water mark and the fund is recovering from a loss.

A high-water mark means the manager only earns an incentive fee on gains ABOVE the highest value previously reached ($125M here). Losses must first be recovered before new fees apply.

Step 1 — Pre-fee AUM after this year's 20% gain:
$$110 \times 1.20 = 132 \text{ (million)}$$

Step 2 — Gain that is above the high-water mark of $125M:
$$132 - 125 = 7 \text{ (million)}$$
Only this $7M is fee-bearing; the climb from $110M up to $125M just recovers past losses and earns no fee.

Step 3 — Incentive fee = 20% of the fee-bearing gain:
$$0.20 \times 7 = 1.4 \text{ (million)}$$

So the fee is $1.4M. Answer B.

Analogy: the HWM is like a personal-best high score. You only earn a bonus for points scored ABOVE your old record — climbing back up to your previous best earns nothing.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($0) wrongly assumes the fund hasn't passed the HWM — but it ended at $132M, above $125M.
- C ($4.4M) charges 20% on the full $22M gain (132 − 110) and ignores the HWM entirely.
- D ($6.6M) is just an inflated wrong figure (e.g., 20% × 33). The fee applies only to the $7M above the HWM.

📌 Exam tip: With a HWM, the incentive fee applies only to gains ABOVE the previous peak. Always (1) compute pre-fee AUM, (2) subtract the HWM, (3) apply the fee rate to that excess.

**Q7.** Compared with mutual funds, hedge funds are generally able to:
- A. Offer guaranteed daily redemption to all investors
- B. Use leverage, short selling, and derivatives freely, and impose lock-up periods
- C. Sell freely to the general retail public with full disclosure
- D. Charge no performance fees by regulation

**Answer: B**

The question asks what hedge funds can do that mutual funds typically cannot.

Because hedge funds sell only to accredited/qualified (wealthy, sophisticated) investors, they are lightly regulated. This freedom lets them short sell, use leverage (borrow to amplify bets), concentrate positions, and use derivatives freely. They also do NOT have to offer daily redemption — they can impose lock-up periods, redemption gates, and notice periods so investors can't pull money out instantly. And they charge performance/incentive fees (like "2 and 20").

Analogy: a mutual fund is like a public swimming pool with strict safety rules for everyone. A hedge fund is a private club for experienced swimmers — fewer rules, but you can't leave the pool whenever you want.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is backwards — hedge funds RESTRICT redemptions (lock-ups), unlike mutual funds.
- C is false — hedge funds sell only to accredited investors, not the general public, and they disclose little.
- D is false — hedge funds are known for charging performance fees; nothing bans them.

📌 Exam tip: Hedge funds = light regulation, accredited investors only, can short/lever/use derivatives, lock-ups, performance fees. Mutual funds = heavily regulated, retail, daily NAV redemption.

**Q8.** A large bank acting as a prime broker for a hedge fund typically provides all of the following EXCEPT:
- A. Margin financing (leverage) and securities lending for short sales
- B. Trade clearing, settlement, and custody
- C. A government guarantee of the hedge fund's returns
- D. Capital introduction to potential investors

**Answer: C**

The question asks which service a prime broker does NOT provide.

A prime broker is a big bank that supports a hedge fund's operations: it clears and settles trades, holds the fund's assets in custody, lends money (margin financing for leverage), lends securities so the fund can short sell, and introduces the fund to potential investors (capital introduction). In return it takes collateral and monitors the fund's positions — so it is often the first to spot a fund in trouble.

What it does NOT do is guarantee the fund's investment returns — no one guarantees hedge fund returns.

Analogy: a prime broker is like a full-service support crew for a race car — fuel (financing), tools (lending), garage (custody), introductions to sponsors. But the crew never promises the car will win the race.

Why the others are wrong:
- A, B, and D are all genuine prime-broker services.
- C is the odd one out — guaranteeing returns is not something any prime broker (or anyone) does.

📌 Exam tip: Prime broker services = clearing/settlement, custody, margin financing, securities lending, capital introduction. It manages risk for itself (collateral, monitoring) — it does NOT guarantee fund performance.

**Q9.** A hedge fund database adds a fund's favorable PRE-entry track record once the fund starts reporting. This inflates reported industry returns through:
- A. Survivorship bias
- B. Measurement bias
- C. Backfill bias
- D. Look-ahead bias

**Answer: C**

The question asks which bias comes from importing a fund's good history when it joins a database.

Backfill bias: funds join databases voluntarily, and they tend to join AFTER they've had good performance. When they join, their earlier (self-selected, favorable) returns get "backfilled" into the database. Since only the funds that did well bother to report, the imported history is rosier than reality, inflating average reported returns.

Analogy: imagine a "best students" yearbook that lets you add your old report cards only after you start scoring well — and naturally you only add the good ones. The yearbook then looks like everyone was always brilliant.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (survivorship bias) is about failed funds DROPPING OUT of the database, so only winners remain — a different mechanism (exit, not entry).
- B (measurement bias) is about illiquid assets being marked at smoothed manager valuations, understating volatility — not about historical track records.
- D (look-ahead bias) is using information that wasn't actually available at the time; it's not the standard hedge-fund-reporting bias described here.

📌 Exam tip: Backfill = adding favorable PAST returns on ENTRY. Survivorship = losers DROPPING OUT. Measurement = smoothed marks on illiquid assets. All three make reported hedge fund returns look better than the real investable experience.

**Q10.** Which best describes the option-like asymmetry built into a "2 and 20" hedge fund fee structure?
- A. The manager shares in both gains and losses equally
- B. The manager shares in gains (via the incentive fee) but not in losses, creating an incentive to take on more volatility
- C. The 2% management fee only applies to profitable years
- D. The high-water mark fully removes any incentive to take risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the risk-taking incentive hidden in the "20%" performance fee.

Under "2 and 20," the manager takes 20% of the profits but does not pay 20% of the losses. This is like owning an option: you get the upside but are protected from the downside. Because the manager keeps a slice of gains but isn't hit by losses, there's a temptation to add volatility (swing for the fences) — big wins pay the manager handsomely, while big losses are mostly the investors' problem. High-water marks and clawbacks only PARTIALLY offset this incentive.

Analogy: imagine a fund manager who gets 20% of any treasure found but pays nothing if the ship sinks. They might steer toward riskier, treasure-rich (but dangerous) waters.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — the manager does NOT share in losses; that's the whole asymmetry.
- C is false — the 2% management fee is charged on assets every year, profitable or not.
- D overstates it — the HWM only PARTIALLY offsets the risk-taking incentive; it doesn't remove it.

📌 Exam tip: The "20" in 2-and-20 is option-like: upside participation, no downside participation → an incentive to increase volatility. HWMs and clawbacks reduce but don't eliminate this.
