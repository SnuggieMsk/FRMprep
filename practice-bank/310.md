**Q1.** A non-dividend-paying stock trades at $50. The risk-free rate is 4% (continuously compounded). The 1-year forward price is closest to:

- A. $48.04
- B. $50.00
- C. $52.04
- D. $54.08

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the forward price of an asset with NO income.

The cost-of-carry formula with no income is:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$$

Plug in: S_0 = 50, r = 0.04, T = 1.
Exponent: 0.04 × 1 = 0.04.
$$e^{0.04} \approx 1.0408$$.
$$F_0 = 50 \times 1.0408 = 52.04$$.

Why C is correct: 50 × e^(0.04) = 50 × 1.0408 = 52.04.

Why A is wrong: 48.04 = 50 × e^(−0.04) — it uses a negative exponent, as if discounting instead of carrying forward.
Why B is wrong: 50.00 is just the spot price; it ignores the cost of carry (interest).
Why D is wrong: 54.08 doubles the rate or otherwise overstates the exponent.

📌 Exam tip: No income → $$F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$$. The forward is ABOVE spot because you pay interest to carry the asset. A forward below spot here signals a sign error.

**Q2.** A stock trades at $25 and will pay dividends with a present value of $1.00 before maturity. The risk-free rate is 6% (continuous) and T = 0.5 years. The 6-month forward price is closest to:

- A. $24.00
- B. $24.73
- C. $25.76
- D. $26.55

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the forward price when the asset pays a KNOWN dollar income.

The formula with known income (present value I) is:
$$F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}$$

Step 1 — subtract the present value of income from spot:
25 − 1.00 = 24.00.

Step 2 — carry forward at the risk-free rate:
Exponent: 0.06 × 0.5 = 0.03.
$$e^{0.03} \approx 1.0305$$.
$$F_0 = 24.00 \times 1.0305 = 24.73$$.

Why B is correct: (25 − 1) × e^(0.03) = 24 × 1.0305 = 24.73.

Why A is wrong: 24.00 subtracts the income but forgets to carry it forward at the interest rate.
Why C is wrong: 25.76 = 25 × e^(0.03) — it forgets to subtract the dividend.
Why D is wrong: 26.55 adds (rather than subtracts) the income or otherwise mishandles the sign.

📌 Exam tip: Known dollar income → $$F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}$$. Subtract income FIRST (it lowers the forward because the carrier collects it), then grow at r.

**Q3.** A stock index is at 2,000 with a dividend yield of 2%; the risk-free rate is 5% (both continuous). The 6-month futures price is closest to:

- A. 2,015.1
- B. 2,030.2
- C. 2,050.6
- D. 2,071.2

**Answer: B**

The question asks for an index futures price using the known-yield formula.

A stock index pays a dividend yield q, so:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - q)T}$$

Plug in: S_0 = 2,000, r = 0.05, q = 0.02, T = 0.5.
Exponent: (0.05 − 0.02) × 0.5 = 0.03 × 0.5 = 0.015.
$$e^{0.015} \approx 1.0151$$.
$$F_0 = 2{,}000 \times 1.0151 = 2{,}030.2$$.

Why B is correct: 2,000 × e^(0.015) = 2,000 × 1.0151 = 2,030.2.

Why A is wrong: 2,015.1 uses half the net rate again or otherwise undercounts the exponent.
Why C is wrong: 2,050.6 = 2,000 × e^(0.025) — it uses r only (0.05 × 0.5) and forgets to subtract the yield.
Why D is wrong: 2,071.2 ADDS the yield instead of subtracting it (using r + q).

📌 Exam tip: Index → known yield → $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$$. SUBTRACT the dividend yield from r. Adding it (r+q) is the classic trap.

**Q4.** The no-arbitrage forward price of an asset is $50.51, but the market quotes the forward at $51.20 — too high. The correct arbitrage strategy is:

- A. Reverse cash-and-carry: short the asset, lend the proceeds, buy forward
- B. Cash-and-carry: borrow money, buy the asset spot, sell (short) the forward
- C. Do nothing, since arbitrage is impossible
- D. Buy the asset spot and buy the forward

**Answer: B**

The question asks which arbitrage to use when the market forward is ABOVE the fair value.

When the market forward is too HIGH (overpriced), you want to SELL the expensive forward and lock in delivery cheaply. The cash-and-carry strategy does this:
1. Borrow money at the risk-free rate.
2. Buy the asset in the spot market today.
3. Short (sell) the forward at the high market price.
At maturity, deliver the asset, collect the high forward price, repay the loan, and pocket the difference. The profit equals the market forward minus the fair forward.

Why B is correct: when the forward is too high, borrow-buy-sell-forward (cash-and-carry) captures the overpricing.

Why A is wrong: reverse cash-and-carry is used when the forward is too LOW, not too high.
Why C is wrong: a clear mispricing in an investment asset DOES allow arbitrage.
Why D is wrong: buying the forward too would double down on the overpriced contract, not arbitrage it.

📌 Exam tip: Forward too HIGH → cash-and-carry (borrow, buy spot, SELL forward). Forward too LOW → reverse cash-and-carry (short spot, lend, BUY forward). "Sell what's expensive."

**Q5.** At the inception of a forward contract, the value of the contract to both parties is:

- A. Equal to the spot price
- B. Equal to the forward price
- C. Zero, because the delivery price K is set equal to the forward price F_0
- D. Always positive for the long

**Answer: C**

The question tests the difference between a forward's PRICE and its VALUE at inception.

When a forward is first written, the delivery price K is chosen to equal the current forward price $$F_0$$. Because the agreed delivery price exactly matches the fair forward price, neither party has an advantage — so the contract is worth ZERO to both at inception. Value only becomes nonzero later, as the underlying price moves and the current forward price drifts away from the locked-in K.

Why C is correct: K is set to $$F_0$$ at inception, making the contract's value zero for both sides.

Why A is wrong: the spot price is not the contract's value; value at inception is zero.
Why B is wrong: $$F_0$$ is the agreed PRICE (the delivery price), not the contract's VALUE.
Why D is wrong: it is zero, not positive, at inception; a positive value would mean a free lunch for the long.

📌 Exam tip: At inception, forward VALUE = 0 (because K = $$F_0$$). The forward PRICE is not the value — don't confuse the two. This is a favorite trap.

**Q6.** You are SHORT a forward with delivery price K = $102 on a non-dividend asset. The current forward price for the same maturity is F = $98, the risk-free rate is 4% (continuous), and 1 year remains. The value of your position is closest to:

- A. −$3.84
- B. −$4.00
- C. +$3.84
- D. +$4.00

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the value of an existing SHORT forward position.

The value of a LONG forward is:
$$f_{long} = (F - K)e^{-rT}$$

A SHORT position is worth the negative of that, so:
$$f_{short} = (K - F)e^{-rT}$$

Plug in: K = 102, F = 98, r = 0.04, T = 1.
K − F = 102 − 98 = 4.
$$e^{-0.04} \approx 0.9608$$.
$$f_{short} = 4 \times 0.9608 = 3.84$$.

The value is POSITIVE because you agreed to SELL at $102, and the asset can now be sold forward at only $98 — your locked-in price is higher, which is good for a seller.

Why C is correct: (102 − 98) × e^(−0.04) = 4 × 0.9608 = +3.84.

Why A is wrong: −3.84 has the right size but wrong sign; it uses the LONG formula, which loses when prices fall.
Why B is wrong: −4.00 forgets to discount and has the wrong sign.
Why D is wrong: +4.00 has the right sign but forgets to discount by e^(−rT).

📌 Exam tip: Long forward value = $$(F - K)e^{-rT}$$; short = $$(K - F)e^{-rT}$$. A short gains when the forward price FALLS below the locked-in K. Always discount.

**Q7.** Six months ago you went LONG a 1-year forward on a non-dividend stock when the stock was $40 and r = 5%, so the delivery price was K = $42.05. Today the stock is $45, r is still 5%, and 6 months remain. The value of your long position is closest to:

- A. $3.99
- B. $5.00
- C. $2.95
- D. −$3.99

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the value of a long forward partway through its life.

Step 1 — current forward price for the remaining 6 months:
$$F = 45\,e^{0.05 \times 0.5} = 45 \times e^{0.025}$$.
$$e^{0.025} \approx 1.0253$$, so F = 45 × 1.0253 = 46.14.

Step 2 — value of the long position:
$$f = (F - K)e^{-rT} = (46.14 - 42.05)e^{-0.05 \times 0.5}$$.
F − K = 46.14 − 42.05 = 4.09.
$$e^{-0.025} \approx 0.9753$$.
$$f = 4.09 \times 0.9753 = 3.99$$.

Quick check via spot (no income): $$f = S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 45 - 42.05 \times 0.9753 = 45 - 41.01 = 3.99$$. Agrees.

Why A is correct: both methods give about $3.99 per share.

Why B is wrong: $5.00 is just the raw stock gain (45 − 40); it ignores discounting and the carry on K.
Why C is wrong: $2.95 mishandles the exponents or uses the wrong time.
Why D is wrong: the long GAINS when the stock rises, so the value is positive, not negative.

📌 Exam tip: To value a forward mid-life, recompute the current forward price for the REMAINING time, then use $$(F-K)e^{-rT}$$. Cross-check with $$S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$$ for a non-dividend asset.

**Q8.** If an asset's price is strongly POSITIVELY correlated with interest rates, then in theory:

- A. Futures and forward prices are equal
- B. Futures prices exceed forward prices
- C. Forward prices exceed futures prices
- D. Both must equal the expected future spot price

**Answer: B**

The question asks how the futures-forward price relationship depends on interest-rate correlation.

Futures are settled daily; forwards settle once at maturity. When the asset price moves UP with interest rates (positive correlation), a long futures holder receives daily settlement GAINS exactly when rates are high — so those gains can be reinvested at high rates. And losses occur when rates are low, so they are financed cheaply. This daily-settlement advantage makes futures slightly MORE valuable, so futures prices exceed forward prices. Negative correlation reverses it.

Why B is correct: positive correlation makes daily settlement favorable for futures, so futures > forwards.

Why A is wrong: equality holds only when interest rates are deterministic (constant/known), not correlated with the asset.
Why C is wrong: forwards exceed futures under NEGATIVE correlation, the opposite case.
Why D is wrong: neither price must equal the expected future spot price; that confuses pricing with expectations.

📌 Exam tip: Positive correlation (asset price ↑ with rates) → futures > forwards (gains reinvested high, losses financed cheap). Negative → forwards > futures. Deterministic rates → equal.

**Q9.** Index arbitrage when the index futures price is ABOVE its fair value $$S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$$ involves:

- A. Shorting the basket of index stocks and buying futures
- B. Buying the basket of index stocks and shorting (selling) futures
- C. Buying both the stocks and the futures
- D. Doing nothing, since indices cannot be arbitraged

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the index-arbitrage trade when futures are overpriced.

If the futures price is ABOVE fair value, the futures are expensive relative to the stocks. The arbitrage is to SELL the expensive thing and BUY the cheap thing: short (sell) the overpriced futures and buy the basket of index stocks. This is executed by computers as program trading. (If futures were below fair value, you would do the reverse: buy futures, short the stocks.)

Why B is correct: futures overpriced → buy the cheap stocks, short the rich futures.

Why A is wrong: that is the trade for when futures are BELOW fair value (buy futures, short stocks), the opposite case.
Why C is wrong: buying both does not arbitrage anything; you must take opposite positions.
Why D is wrong: a stock index is an investment asset, so its futures price IS pinned by arbitrage.

📌 Exam tip: Futures rich → buy stocks, sell futures. Futures cheap → buy futures, sell stocks. Always "buy the cheap leg, sell the expensive leg."

**Q10.** A currency forward is priced by treating the foreign currency as an asset with a known yield equal to the foreign risk-free rate $$r_f$$. With S quoted as domestic per unit of foreign, the forward price is:

- A. $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r + r_f)T}$$
- B. $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - r_f)T}$$
- C. $$F_0 = S_0 e^{r_f T}$$
- D. $$F_0 = S_0 e^{-rT}$$

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the currency forward pricing formula.

A foreign currency is like an asset that pays a continuous yield equal to the foreign risk-free rate $$r_f$$ (because holding the foreign currency earns interest abroad). So we use the known-yield cost-of-carry formula with q replaced by $$r_f$$:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - r_f)T}$$

where r is the domestic rate. This is the same as covered interest rate parity.

Why B is correct: the foreign rate plays the role of the dividend yield, giving $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - r_f)T}$$.

Why A is wrong: it ADDS the foreign rate; the yield should be SUBTRACTED, just like a dividend yield.
Why C is wrong: it drops the domestic rate r entirely.
Why D is wrong: it ignores the foreign rate and flips the sign of the domestic rate.

📌 Exam tip: Currency = asset with yield $$r_f$$, so $$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r - r_f)T}$$ (same as CIP). SUBTRACT the foreign rate, exactly like a dividend yield.
