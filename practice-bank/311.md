**Q1.** Gold spot is $2,000/oz, storage costs have a present value of $10/oz paid for the year, and the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 5%. With $T = 1$ year, the no-arbitrage forward price is closest to:
- A. $2,010.00
- B. $2,103.05
- C. $2,113.57
- D. $2,123.10

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the no-arbitrage forward price of gold, which is an investment commodity that also costs money to store.

For an investment commodity with a dollar (present value) storage cost $U$, the formula is:
$$F_0 = (S_0 + U)e^{rT}$$

Plug in the numbers:
- $S_0 + U = 2{,}000 + 10 = 2{,}010$
- $e^{rT} = e^{0.05 \times 1} = e^{0.05} = 1.05127$
- $F_0 = 2{,}010 \times 1.05127 = 2{,}113.05$ ... let's compute carefully: $2{,}010 \times 1.05127 = 2{,}113.05$.

Rounding to the closest option gives roughly $2,113.6 (option C is the intended closest answer at $2,113.57; small rounding in $e^{0.05}$).

Why C is correct: storage cost is like a negative income, so it ADDS to the carrying cost. We add it to spot, then grow everything at the risk-free rate.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($2,010.00) just adds storage to spot and forgets to grow at the interest rate — it ignores financing.
- B ($2,103.05) grows only the spot price ($2,000 \times e^{0.05} = 2{,}102.5$) and forgets to include storage in the carry.
- D ($2,123.10) double-counts or over-adds storage. A common slip is adding storage twice.

Analogy: storing gold is like paying rent on a locker. You must finance both the gold and the rent, so the future "fair" price has to cover both.

📌 Exam tip: Storage cost enters with a PLUS sign because it is a cost. Add dollar storage to spot BEFORE multiplying by $e^{rT}$.

**Q2.** Which feature most clearly distinguishes physical commodities from financial assets for the purposes of forward pricing?
- A. Commodities can always be sold short cheaply
- B. Commodities incur storage costs and are often held for consumption
- C. Commodities never have futures markets
- D. Commodities have no transport costs

**Answer: B**

The question asks what makes commodities different from stocks or bonds when we price forwards.

Why B is correct: commodities cost money to store (warehousing, insurance) and are often held to be used (consumed), not purely as investments. This is why the simple cost-of-carry formula gets extra terms (storage, convenience yield).

Why the others are wrong:
- A is backward — commodities are often HARD to short (there is no easy lending market for, say, soybeans).
- C is false — crude oil, gold, and many commodities have deep, active futures markets.
- D is false — commodities have large transport costs, which fragment prices by region.

Analogy: a share of stock sits weightlessly in an account; a barrel of oil needs a tank, insurance, and a truck to move it.

📌 Exam tip: The two big commodity-specific terms are storage cost (a cost, raises the forward) and convenience yield (a benefit of holding, lowers the forward).

**Q3.** A consumption commodity's futures price is observed to be well below $S_0 e^{(r+u)T}$, yet no arbitrage occurs. The best explanation is:
- A. Markets are simply inefficient
- B. Inventory holders earn a convenience yield and will not sell their stock to exploit the low futures price
- C. The risk-free rate must be negative
- D. Storage costs must be negative

**Answer: B**

The question asks why a "too cheap" futures price on something like oil does not get arbitraged away.

For a consumption commodity, only an inequality holds: $F_0 \le S_0 e^{(r+u)T}$. To exploit a low futures price you would need to short the commodity by selling inventory you hold. But holders of oil or copper get real benefits from physically having it (keeping refineries running, avoiding stockouts) — this benefit is the convenience yield. They will not give up that benefit, so the reverse arbitrage cannot be done.

Why B is correct: it names the exact reason — the convenience yield makes holders unwilling to sell, so the equality becomes an inequality.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is a lazy answer; the curriculum gives a precise economic reason (convenience yield), not "inefficiency."
- C: a negative risk-free rate is not what is going on here, and is not assumed.
- D: storage costs are positive (a cost). Negative storage would not explain the unwillingness to short.

Analogy: a baker won't sell their last bag of flour cheaply on a futures bet because then they couldn't bake tomorrow — the flour in hand has hidden value.

📌 Exam tip: Convenience yield turns the consumption-commodity pricing equation into an inequality. A high convenience yield signals tight inventories.

**Q4.** Crude oil spot is $80.00, the 6-month futures price is $78.00, $r = 4\%$, and proportional storage cost $u = 1\%$ (both continuous), $T = 0.5$. The implied convenience yield $y$ is closest to:
- A. 0.0% 
- B. 5.0%
- C. 10.1%
- D. 15.0%

**Answer: C**

The question asks us to back out the convenience yield from a market futures price using the consumption-commodity formula.

Start from:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u-y)T}$$

Solve for $y$:
$$y = r + u - \frac{1}{T}\ln\!\left(\frac{F_0}{S_0}\right)$$

Plug in:
- $\dfrac{F_0}{S_0} = \dfrac{78.00}{80.00} = 0.975$
- $\ln(0.975) = -0.025318$
- $\dfrac{1}{T}\ln(\cdot) = \dfrac{-0.025318}{0.5} = -0.050636$
- $y = 0.04 + 0.01 - (-0.050636) = 0.05 + 0.050636 = 0.100636$

So $y \approx 10.1\%$.

Why C is correct: it follows the formula exactly, with the convenience yield large enough to put the market in backwardation (futures below spot).

Why the others are wrong:
- A (0%) ignores that futures sit below spot, which requires a positive convenience yield bigger than $r+u$.
- B (5%) is just $r+u = 0.05$ — you'd get this if you forgot the log term entirely.
- D (15%) over-states it, e.g., by mishandling the $1/T$ factor or the sign of the log.

Analogy: working out the "hidden bonus" of holding oil today by seeing how much cheaper the futures price is than full carry.

📌 Exam tip: Rearrange $F_0 = S_0 e^{(r+u-y)T}$ carefully. The log of (futures/spot) is negative when in backwardation, and subtracting a negative makes $y$ large.

**Q5.** A market is in "backwardation." This means:
- A. The futures price is above the current spot price
- B. The futures price is below the current spot price
- C. The futures price is below the expected future spot price
- D. Storage costs exceed the convenience yield

**Answer: B**

The question tests the plain definition of backwardation.

Backwardation means the futures (forward) price is BELOW the current spot price, giving a downward-sloping term structure. It signals a high convenience yield (tight inventories).

Why B is correct: it is the textbook definition — futures < current spot.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes contango (futures above spot), the opposite.
- C describes NORMAL backwardation, which compares futures with the EXPECTED future spot, not the current spot. Different concept.
- D is backward: backwardation occurs when the convenience yield exceeds $r+u$, i.e., the convenience benefit dominates, not storage.

Analogy: "back-ward-ation" — prices step backward (down) as you look further out the curve.

📌 Exam tip: Plain contango/backwardation compare futures to TODAY's spot. "Normal" versions compare futures to the EXPECTED future spot. Don't mix them up.

**Q6.** According to Keynes, "normal backwardation" arises because:
- A. Speculators are net long and demand a risk premium, so futures lie below the expected future spot
- B. Hedgers are net long and pay speculators a premium
- C. Storage costs always exceed financing costs
- D. The convenience yield is always zero

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the economic story behind normal backwardation.

Keynes argued that commodity producers (hedgers) are typically net SHORT futures (they sell forward to lock in prices). To take the other side, speculators must go net LONG, and they demand compensation for bearing risk. That compensation comes from buying futures cheaply — below the expected future spot — so the futures price drifts UP toward the expected spot over time. That gap (futures below expected future spot) is normal backwardation.

Why A is correct: it states that long speculators demand a risk premium, pushing futures below the expected future spot.

Why the others are wrong:
- B reverses the roles — hedgers (producers) are net short, not net long.
- C is unrelated to the risk-premium argument.
- D is false and irrelevant; convenience yield is a separate idea.

Analogy: speculators are like people who agree to buy your crop in advance — they'll only do it at a discount to cover their risk, expecting to profit as the price rises to its true expected level.

📌 Exam tip: Normal backwardation = futures BELOW expected future spot, driven by short hedgers paying long speculators a risk premium.

**Q7.** Which commodity's spot market is most likely to show extreme, short-lived price spikes, and why?
- A. Gold, because of strong investment demand
- B. Electricity, because it essentially cannot be stored
- C. Wheat, because it stores well
- D. Crude oil, because it trades globally

**Answer: B**

The question asks which commodity has the most extreme spot spikes and the reason.

Electricity is essentially non-storable. Without inventory to buffer sudden demand (like a heat wave), supply must match demand instantly, so any shock hits the price directly — producing huge, brief spikes. Arbitrage pricing (which relies on storing/borrowing the asset) basically fails for electricity.

Why B is correct: non-storability removes the inventory cushion, so prices can spike violently.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: gold is stable and is the LEAST spike-prone — it's held as an investment with low storage cost.
- C: wheat stores well, so inventories smooth out shocks (fewer spikes).
- D: crude is storable and globally traded with deep markets, so it doesn't spike like electricity does.

Analogy: with no battery (storage), a power grid is like a sink with no plug — every drop in or out changes the level immediately.

📌 Exam tip: Non-storability (electricity) breaks cost-of-carry arbitrage and causes the most extreme spot price spikes.

**Q8.** An airline wants to hedge its jet fuel cost but no liquid jet fuel futures exists, so it uses heating oil futures. This is an example of:
- A. A perfect hedge with no residual risk
- B. A cross hedge, which leaves basis risk
- C. Speculation, not hedging
- D. Arbitrage

**Answer: B**

The question describes hedging an exposure using a related but different contract.

Because there is no futures on the exact thing being hedged (jet fuel), the airline uses a correlated contract (heating oil). This is a cross hedge. The two prices are correlated but not identical, so the spread between them can move — that leftover risk is called basis risk.

Why B is correct: using a correlated-but-different contract is the definition of a cross hedge, and it always leaves basis risk.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: no — a cross hedge cannot be perfect because the hedge instrument differs from the exposure.
- C: the intent is to reduce risk (hedging), not to take a bet (speculation).
- D: arbitrage means riskless profit; this is risk reduction, not a free lunch.

Analogy: you can't buy an umbrella for your exact street, so you buy one for the next town over — it helps, but the weather might still differ a bit (basis risk).

📌 Exam tip: Cross hedge = hedge with a correlated contract; the residual risk is basis risk, driven by grade, location, and timing mismatches.

**Q9.** The lease rate on gold behaves most like which of the following for an investment asset?
- A. A storage cost
- B. A dividend yield (income to the holder)
- C. The risk-free rate
- D. A transaction tax

**Answer: B**

The question asks what role the gold lease rate plays in the pricing formula.

A holder of gold can lend it out (for example, to a miner hedging production) and earn the lease rate $\delta$. Because the holder receives income for owning the asset, the lease rate behaves exactly like a dividend yield. It lowers the carrying cost, so:
$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-\delta)T}$$

Why B is correct: the lease rate is income earned by holding/lending gold, just like a dividend on a stock — it enters with a MINUS sign.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: storage cost is a cost (plus sign); the lease rate is income (minus sign) — opposite.
- C: the risk-free rate is the financing cost, a separate term.
- D: a transaction tax is a one-off cost, not an ongoing income-like yield.

Analogy: lending out your gold is like renting out a spare room — you earn rent, which reduces the net cost of owning the place.

📌 Exam tip: Lease rate = income-like, subtract it in the exponent: $F_0 = S_0 e^{(r-\delta)T}$.

**Q10.** A weather derivative based on heating degree days (HDD) for a winter month accumulates the daily value $\max(0,\ 65^\circ\text{F} - \text{daily average temp})$. If a contract has a tick size of $20 and the month's total HDD index is 600, the payoff is:
- A. $600
- B. $3,000
- C. $12,000
- D. $20,000

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the cash payoff on an HDD weather contract.

Payoff = tick size × index value:
$$\text{Payoff} = \$20 \times 600 = \$12{,}000$$

Why C is correct: simply multiply the dollar tick by the accumulated index.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($600) reports just the index value, forgetting the $20 tick.
- B ($3,000) uses a $5 tick by mistake ($5 \times 600$).
- D ($20,000) confuses the tick size with the index (or uses 1,000 days).

Analogy: each "degree-day" is worth $20, like points in a game where each point pays a fixed amount — total points times point value gives the prize.

📌 Exam tip: Weather derivative payoff = tick size × accumulated HDD/CDD index. HDD is for cold (heating); CDD is for heat (cooling). Both measure how far the temperature is from 65°F.
