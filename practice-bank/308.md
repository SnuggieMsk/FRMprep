**Q1.** A company knows it will need to BUY a large quantity of copper in three months and worries that copper prices will rise. To hedge, the company should:

- A. Take a short hedge by selling copper futures
- B. Take a long hedge by buying copper futures
- C. Do nothing, since futures cannot hedge purchases
- D. Buy the copper now regardless of price

**Answer: B**

The question asks which hedge protects a future BUYER against rising prices.

A long hedge means buying futures. It is appropriate when you will need to BUY the asset later and fear a price increase. If copper prices rise, the spot purchase costs more, but the long futures position gains, offsetting the higher cost. Think of it like locking in today's pizza price for a party next month: if pizza gets pricier, your locked-in deal saves you.

Why B is correct: a future buyer fearing price rises buys futures (a long hedge) so futures gains offset the higher purchase cost.

Why A is wrong: a short hedge (selling futures) protects an owner/seller against FALLING prices, the opposite situation.
Why C is wrong: futures absolutely can hedge a future purchase — that is the long hedge.
Why D is wrong: buying now may not be possible or desirable (storage, cash, timing); futures hedge the price without taking early delivery.

📌 Exam tip: Will SELL/own it later → SHORT hedge. Will BUY it later → LONG hedge. Match the futures direction to your future cash need.

**Q2.** Monthly price changes have standard deviations $$\sigma_S = 0.04$$ and $$\sigma_F = 0.05$$, with correlation $$\rho = 0.85$$. The minimum-variance hedge ratio is closest to:

- A. 0.85
- B. 1.06
- C. 0.68
- D. 0.43

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the minimum-variance hedge ratio.

The formula is:
$$h^* = \rho \frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}$$

Note: spot volatility goes on TOP, futures volatility on the bottom.

Plug in:
$$h^* = 0.85 \times \frac{0.04}{0.05}$$

First the ratio: 0.04 / 0.05 = 0.8.
Then: 0.85 × 0.8 = 0.68.

Why C is correct: 0.85 × 0.8 = 0.68.

Why A is wrong: 0.85 is just the correlation; it forgets to multiply by the volatility ratio.
Why B is wrong: 1.06 = 0.85 × (0.05/0.04), the INVERTED ratio — the classic distractor with futures vol on top.
Why D is wrong: 0.43 does not match any correct combination of these inputs.

📌 Exam tip: $$h^* = \rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F$$ — spot on top. If your answer used futures vol on top, you flipped the ratio and fell for the trap.

**Q3.** An airline will buy 2,000,000 gallons of jet fuel and hedges with heating oil futures of 42,000 gallons each. The minimum-variance hedge ratio is $$h^* = 0.78$$. Ignoring tailing, how many futures contracts are needed (rounded)?

- A. 16 contracts
- B. 37 contracts
- C. 48 contracts
- D. 61 contracts

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the optimal number of contracts using the quantity-based formula.

The formula is:
$$N^* = \frac{h^* \times Q_A}{Q_F}$$

where $$Q_A$$ = size of position to hedge and $$Q_F$$ = contract size.

Plug in:
$$N^* = \frac{0.78 \times 2{,}000{,}000}{42{,}000}$$

Numerator: 0.78 × 2,000,000 = 1,560,000.
Divide: 1,560,000 / 42,000 = 37.14.
Round to 37 contracts.

Why B is correct: 1,560,000 / 42,000 ≈ 37.14, which rounds to 37.

Why A is wrong: 16 would come from dividing by a much larger number or other slip.
Why C is wrong: 48 ≈ 2,000,000 / 42,000 — it forgets to multiply by the hedge ratio 0.78.
Why D is wrong: 61 overstates the count; no correct combination gives this.

📌 Exam tip: $$N^* = h^* Q_A / Q_F$$. Apply the hedge ratio FIRST, then divide by contract size. Forgetting $$h^*$$ gives the common too-high answer.

**Q4.** A short hedger benefits when:

- A. The basis weakens unexpectedly
- B. The basis strengthens unexpectedly
- C. The futures price falls below zero
- D. Basis risk is removed by daily settlement

**Answer: B**

The question asks which basis move helps a short hedger.

Recall the basis is $$b = S - F$$ (spot minus futures), and the effective price a short hedger locks in is:
$$\text{Effective price} = F_1 + b_2$$

where $$b_2$$ is the basis at close-out. A STRENGTHENING (higher) basis means $$b_2$$ goes up, which raises the effective SALE price for the short hedger — good for someone selling.

Why B is correct: a stronger basis raises $$F_1 + b_2$$, so the short hedger effectively sells at a higher price.

Why A is wrong: a weakening basis LOWERS the effective sale price — that hurts the short hedger and helps the long hedger.
Why C is wrong: futures prices do not fall below zero in normal markets; irrelevant to basis.
Why D is wrong: daily settlement does not remove basis risk; basis risk comes from the spot-futures gap at close.

📌 Exam tip: STRONG basis helps the SHORT hedger (sells higher). WEAK basis helps the LONG hedger (buys lower). Memorize "strong = short."

**Q5.** A short hedge is initiated at a futures price of 60. At close-out, spot is 53 and the futures price is 55. The effective sale price achieved is:

- A. 53
- B. 55
- C. 58
- D. 60

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the effective sale price locked in by a short hedge.

Step 1 — find the basis at close:
$$b_2 = S_2 - F_2 = 53 - 55 = -2$$

Step 2 — effective price for a short hedger:
$$\text{Effective price} = F_1 + b_2 = 60 + (-2) = 58$$

Check the other way: the short sells futures at 60 and buys back at 55, a futures gain of 60 − 55 = 5. Then sells the actual asset at spot 53. Total = 53 + 5 = 58. Both methods agree.

Why C is correct: $$F_1 + b_2 = 60 − 2 = 58$$ (also 53 + futures gain of 5 = 58).

Why A is wrong: 53 is just the spot sale price; it ignores the $5 futures gain.
Why B is wrong: 55 is the closing futures price, not the effective sale price.
Why D is wrong: 60 is the initial futures price; the basis at close shifts the effective price to 58.

📌 Exam tip: Effective price (short hedge) = $$F_1 + b_2$$ = spot at close + futures gain. Always cross-check with the futures gain method.

**Q6.** A $50 million equity portfolio has a beta of 1.4. Index futures trade at 5,000 with a $250 multiplier. To reduce the portfolio beta to 0.8, the manager should:

- A. Buy 24 contracts
- B. Sell 24 contracts
- C. Sell 56 contracts
- D. Buy 40 contracts

**Answer: B**

The question asks how many contracts to trade, and in which direction, to lower beta.

Step 1 — value of one futures contract:
$$F = 5{,}000 \times 250 = \$1{,}250{,}000$$

Step 2 — contracts to change beta:
$$N = (\beta^* - \beta)\frac{P}{F}$$

Plug in:
$$N = (0.8 - 1.4) \times \frac{50{,}000{,}000}{1{,}250{,}000}$$

The P/F part: 50,000,000 / 1,250,000 = 40.
The beta part: 0.8 − 1.4 = −0.6.
So N = −0.6 × 40 = −24.

A NEGATIVE N means SELL (short) contracts. So the manager sells 24 contracts.

Why B is correct: N = −24, and the negative sign means sell 24 contracts.

Why A is wrong: it has the right number but the wrong direction; reducing beta requires SELLING, not buying.
Why C is wrong: 56 does not come from these numbers; likely a miscomputed P/F or beta term.
Why D is wrong: buying 40 would raise beta and uses the wrong figure.

📌 Exam tip: $$N = (\beta^* - \beta)P/F$$. Negative result = SELL (lowers beta). Positive = BUY (raises beta). Reducing beta always means shorting index futures.

**Q7.** A manager wants to RAISE a $24 million portfolio's beta from 1.2 to 1.8. Index futures trade at 4,000 with a $250 multiplier. The required trade is closest to:

- A. Long 14 contracts
- B. Short 14 contracts
- C. Long 29 contracts
- D. Short 29 contracts

**Answer: A**

The question asks the number and direction of contracts to increase beta.

Step 1 — value of one contract:
$$F = 4{,}000 \times 250 = \$1{,}000{,}000$$

Step 2 — contracts to change beta:
$$N = (\beta^* - \beta)\frac{P}{F} = (1.8 - 1.2)\times\frac{24{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000}$$

P/F: 24,000,000 / 1,000,000 = 24.
Beta part: 1.8 − 1.2 = +0.6.
N = 0.6 × 24 = +14.4 ≈ 14.

A POSITIVE N means BUY (long) contracts. So go long about 14 contracts.

Why A is correct: N = +14.4, positive, so buy (long) 14 contracts.

Why B is wrong: shorting would LOWER beta; the sign is positive here, meaning buy.
Why C is wrong: 29 ≈ full-hedge size (1.2 × 24), which is unrelated to this beta change.
Why D is wrong: wrong direction and wrong number.

📌 Exam tip: Raising beta → positive N → BUY contracts. The change uses $$(\beta^* - \beta)$$, not the full beta. Here that is only 0.6, giving ~14 contracts.

**Q8.** When fully hedging a long equity portfolio with index futures to earn roughly the risk-free rate, a manager would prefer to hedge rather than sell the stocks mainly because:

- A. Hedging guarantees a higher return than holding the stocks
- B. Hedging avoids transaction costs and capital gains taxes, keeps the chosen stocks, and is quickly reversible
- C. Selling the portfolio is legally prohibited
- D. Futures always outperform the underlying stocks

**Answer: B**

The question asks why a manager hedges instead of liquidating.

Selling the whole portfolio triggers transaction costs and capital gains taxes, and forces the manager to give up the carefully selected stocks (and any alpha). A futures hedge temporarily removes market risk while keeping the stocks, and it can be unwound quickly and cheaply when the manager wants market exposure back.

Why B is correct: it lists the real reasons — avoid costs/taxes, keep the stocks/alpha, and reverse easily.

Why A is wrong: hedging does not guarantee a higher return; a fully hedged portfolio earns roughly the risk-free rate plus alpha.
Why C is wrong: selling is not prohibited; it is just costly and disruptive.
Why D is wrong: futures do not "always outperform" stocks; that is not the reason to hedge.

📌 Exam tip: Hedge instead of sell to dodge taxes/transaction costs, keep your stock picks (alpha), and stay nimble. A fully hedged portfolio earns about the risk-free rate.

**Q9.** In a "stack and roll" strategy used to hedge a long-dated exposure with short-dated futures, the two key risks the hedger faces are:

- A. Convergence risk and tick-size risk
- B. Rollover basis risk and cash-flow timing risk from margin calls
- C. Counterparty default risk and translation risk
- D. Dividend risk and beta drift

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the main risks created by stacking and rolling futures.

A stack-and-roll hedge puts the full position in a near-dated contract, then repeatedly closes it before delivery and re-opens it in the next contract. Two dangers arise:
1. Rollover basis risk — the price difference between the expiring and the new contract at each roll is unknown in advance.
2. Cash-flow timing risk — futures losses trigger immediate margin calls (real cash out the door), while the offsetting gains on the long-term position are unrealized (paper gains, no cash in).

This is exactly what sank Metallgesellschaft in 1993.

Why B is correct: rollover basis risk plus margin-call cash-flow timing are the two classic stack-and-roll risks.

Why A is wrong: tick size is trivial; convergence is normal and not the key danger here.
Why C is wrong: counterparty risk is minimal in cleared futures; translation risk is an FX-accounting concept, not relevant.
Why D is wrong: dividends and beta drift relate to equity hedges, not commodity stack-and-roll.

📌 Exam tip: Stack and roll → (1) rollover basis risk at each roll and (2) margin-call cash drain while hedge gains are unrealized. Remember Metallgesellschaft.

**Q10.** For a cross hedge with correlation $$\rho = 0.928$$ between changes in the hedged asset's price and the futures price, the hedge effectiveness (proportion of variance eliminated) is closest to:

- A. 92.8%
- B. 86.1%
- C. 13.9%
- D. 96.3%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for hedge effectiveness, which is the fraction of variance removed by the hedge.

The formula is:
$$R^2 = \rho^2$$

Plug in:
$$R^2 = 0.928^2 = 0.861$$

So about 86.1% of the variance of the hedged item is eliminated. The remaining 1 − 0.861 = 0.139, or 13.9%, stays as basis risk.

Why B is correct: 0.928 squared = 0.861, i.e., 86.1% of variance removed.

Why A is wrong: 92.8% is just $$\rho$$ itself, not squared; effectiveness is $$\rho^2$$.
Why C is wrong: 13.9% is the variance REMAINING, not the variance eliminated.
Why D is wrong: 96.3% would be the square root of 0.928, not its square.

📌 Exam tip: Hedge effectiveness = $$\rho^2$$ (square the correlation). The leftover (1 − $$\rho^2$$) is the basis risk you could not hedge away.
