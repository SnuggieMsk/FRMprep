**Q1.** A portfolio's key-rate durations are 0.8 (2-year), 1.5 (5-year), 3.2 (10-year), and 1.5 (30-year). Its effective (parallel-shift) duration is approximately:
- A. 3.2
- B. 7.0
- C. 1.75
- D. Cannot be determined from key-rate durations

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how do the four key-rate durations relate to the overall effective duration?

Because the four key-rate shifts, when added together, reproduce a single parallel 1bp shift, the key-rate durations SUM to the effective duration:
$$\sum_k D_k = 0.8 + 1.5 + 3.2 + 1.5 = 7.0$$

- A (3.2) picks only the largest single key-rate duration (the 10-year) — but effective duration combines them all.
- C (1.75) is the average of the four — but they sum, they don't average.
- D is wrong; the sum is exactly how you recover effective duration.

Analogy: if you split a parallel push on a whole row of dominoes into four separate nudges (one per zone), adding up the four nudges gives you back the single big push.

📌 Exam tip: Key-rate durations add up to effective duration; key-rate '01s (KR01s) add up to total DV01. This "sums-to-total" property is heavily tested.

**Q2.** A trader is long a 10-year bullet bond, hedged to zero DV01 with a short, duration-matched 2s/30s barbell. This position will lose money when:
- A. The curve shifts up in parallel
- B. The curve shifts down in parallel
- C. The curve's curvature changes — the 2-year and 30-year fall relative to the 10-year
- D. Never — zero DV01 means the position is fully hedged

**Answer: C**

The question asks: where is the hidden risk in a DV01-neutral bullet-vs-barbell trade?

A zero-DV01 position is protected against parallel shifts by construction — both A and B net to zero. But "zero DV01" is a single-factor measure; it does not protect against the curve changing shape. The trade is effectively short curvature (short the barbell's convexity), so it loses when the wings (2-year and 30-year) rally relative to the belly (10-year) — a curvature/butterfly move.

- A and B are exactly the moves the hedge IS designed to neutralize.
- D is the trap: zero DV01 means flat to parallel moves only, NOT fully hedged. Curve reshaping slips through.

Analogy: balancing a seesaw at the center protects you if both ends go up or down together. But if one end rises while the other drops (a twist), your "balance" no longer holds.

📌 Exam tip: A DV01-neutral position is still exposed to non-parallel (steepener, flattener, butterfly) moves. This is the headline limitation of single-factor hedging.

**Q3.** In the standard key-rate framework, the 5-year key-rate shift is best described as:
- A. A 1bp parallel move in all rates from 0 to 5 years
- B. A 1bp move at the 5-year point, declining linearly to zero at the 2- and 10-year points
- C. A 1bp move at the 5-year point only, with all other maturities unchanged
- D. A 1bp move in all forward rates between years 2 and 10

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what shape does a single key-rate shift take?

Key-rate shifts are "tent-shaped." The chosen rate (here, 5-year) moves by 1bp at its peak and then tapers LINEARLY down to zero at the adjacent key rates (the 2-year and 10-year points). This way, when you add up all the key-rate shifts, they recombine into a smooth parallel shift.

- A is too broad — it shifts a whole block of rates equally, which is not the tent shape.
- C is too narrow — a spike at one single point with no tapering is not how key rates are defined (and wouldn't sum to a parallel shift).
- D describes a forward-bucket shift, a different (forward-rate-based) method.

Analogy: imagine pulling up a tent pole at the 5-year mark — the fabric (rate change) is highest right at the pole and slopes down to the ground (zero) at the neighboring poles.

📌 Exam tip: Key-rate shift = tent shape: peak 1bp at the key maturity, linear decay to zero at adjacent key rates, flat beyond the first/last key rate.

**Q4.** A portfolio has a 10-year KR01 of +\$2,400 (it loses \$2,400 per bp rise in the 10-year key rate). Hedge Bond X has a 10-year KR01 of \$60 per \$1M face, with other KR01s ≈ 0. The appropriate hedge is to:
- A. Buy \$40M face of Bond X
- B. Sell \$40M face of Bond X
- C. Sell \$4M face of Bond X
- D. Buy \$24M face of Bond X

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how much of Bond X, and which direction, offsets the portfolio's 10-year exposure?

We need a hedge with a 10-year KR01 equal and opposite to +\$2,400, i.e., −\$2,400.

Step 1 — face amount:
$$\frac{2{,}400}{60} = 40 \;\Rightarrow\; \$40\text{M face}$$

Step 2 — direction. The portfolio LOSES when the 10-year rate rises (positive KR01, long-bond convention). To offset, you need a position that GAINS when that rate rises — a SHORT bond position. So sell \$40M face.

- A buys instead of sells — that doubles the exposure to +\$4,800.
- C is off by a factor of 10 ($$2{,}400/600$$ or a decimal slip).
- D uses 2,400 inconsistently (perhaps $$2{,}400/100$$).

Analogy: your boat leans because of weight on the right (the 10-year exposure). You add equal weight on the left (short the bond) to level it — same size, opposite side.

📌 Exam tip: Positive KR01 = you lose when that rate rises = hedge by going SHORT a bond with matching KR01. Decide your sign convention first and carry it through.

**Q5.** To fully hedge a portfolio's exposure to n distinct key rates (so that the KR01 at each key rate is zero), you need at least:
- A. 1 hedging instrument
- B. 2 hedging instruments
- C. n hedging instruments
- D. 2n hedging instruments

**Answer: C**

The question asks: how many hedge instruments are needed for n key-rate exposures?

Each key rate is a separate dimension of risk. Setting the total KR01 to zero at every one of the n key rates gives you n equations. To solve n equations, you need n unknowns — that is, n hedging instruments (ideally each concentrated near one key rate). One instrument can only zero out one exposure reliably.

- A would only handle parallel risk (one factor), leaving the other n−1 exposures open.
- B is enough only for two key rates.
- D is more than necessary; n equations need exactly n unknowns.

Analogy: to balance a table with n wobbly legs, you need n adjustable feet — one knob per leg. One knob can't level four legs independently.

📌 Exam tip: n key-rate exposures → n hedge instruments → n equations in n unknowns. Choose each hedge to be concentrated near a different key rate so the system is easy to solve.

**Q6.** A portfolio has KR01s of \$500 (5-year) and \$400 (10-year). Two hedge bonds have the following KR01s per \$1M face: Bond A — \$40 (5-year), \$5 (10-year); Bond B — \$0 (5-year), \$80 (10-year). The face of Bond A to short is closest to:
- A. \$12.5M
- B. \$4.22M
- C. \$10.0M
- D. \$5.0M

**Answer: A**

The question asks: solve the hedge system for the amount of Bond A.

Set up equations so the hedge KR01s match the portfolio's at each key rate. Let $$F_A, F_B$$ be face amounts in \$M:
- 5-year: $$40 F_A + 0 \cdot F_B = 500$$
- 10-year: $$5 F_A + 80 F_B = 400$$

The first equation has only one unknown — solve it directly:
$$F_A = \frac{500}{40} = 12.5$$

So short \$12.5M of Bond A. (Back-substituting gives $$5(12.5) + 80 F_B = 400 \Rightarrow F_B = 337.5/80 = 4.22$$M, but the question asked for Bond A.)

- B (\$4.22M) is the answer for Bond B, not Bond A — read carefully.
- C (\$10.0M) and D (\$5.0M) come from mis-setting the equations or using the wrong KR01.

Analogy: solving these is like a recipe where one ingredient (Bond A) is fixed by a single constraint first; once you know it, the rest falls into place.

📌 Exam tip: Start with the equation that has only ONE unknown (these systems are usually triangular), solve it, then back-substitute. Bond A is fixed entirely by the 5-year row since Bond B has zero 5-year KR01.

**Q7.** A swap desk wants to measure interest-rate risk by shifting EACH curve-fitting instrument's rate by 1bp, refitting the curve, and repricing. This measure is called:
- A. Effective duration
- B. Partial '01s
- C. Macaulay duration
- D. DV01

**Answer: B**

The question asks: name the risk measure that shifts each individual curve input by 1bp.

Partial '01s are defined exactly this way: shift one fitting instrument's rate (e.g., each par swap rate used to build the curve) by 1bp, refit the curve, and reprice. There is one '01 per curve input — often 10 to 20 buckets — and the partial '01s sum to the total '01.

- A (effective duration) is a single parallel-shift, one-factor measure.
- C (Macaulay duration) is the average time to cash flows — unrelated to curve-input shifts.
- D (DV01) is the total sensitivity to one parallel 1bp shift, not a per-input breakdown.

Analogy: instead of measuring how a bridge responds to shaking the whole thing (DV01), partial '01s wiggle each support pillar one at a time to see which pillar your position depends on.

📌 Exam tip: Three resolutions, same idea — key rates (par yields, tent-shaped), partial '01s (each curve-fitting instrument), and forward-bucket '01s (all forwards in a maturity bucket). Each set sums to the total '01.

**Q8.** A DV01 hedge implicitly assumes the position's yield and the hedge's yield move one-for-one. If, empirically, the position's yield moves only 0.9bp for every 1bp move in the hedge's yield, a volatility-weighted (regression-based) hedge would:
- A. Increase the hedge size by dividing by 0.9
- B. Scale the hedge by the regression slope β = 0.9
- C. Leave the hedge unchanged
- D. Use β = 1.0 always

**Answer: B**

The question asks: how does a regression-based hedge adjust the plain DV01 hedge?

A volatility-weighted hedge multiplies the standard ratio by the regression slope β (the change in the position's yield per unit change in the hedge's yield):
$$N = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{position}}{\text{DV01}_{hedge}} \times \beta$$
Here β = 0.9, so the hedge is scaled by 0.9. Because the position's yield moves LESS than the hedge's yield, you need a slightly SMALLER hedge.

- A divides by 0.9 (making the hedge bigger) — wrong direction; the position is less sensitive, so it needs less hedging.
- C ignores the empirical relationship entirely — that is the plain DV01 hedge being corrected.
- D assumes one-for-one (β = 1), which is the very assumption the regression hedge is improving on.

Analogy: if your end of the seesaw moves only 90% as much as your friend's end, you need only 90% of the counterweight you'd otherwise add.

📌 Exam tip: Volatility-weighted hedge = DV01 hedge × β. It minimizes P&L variance but adds model risk, because β is estimated from history and drifts over time.

**Q9.** A coupon bond, valued off a curve fitted through par key rates, sometimes shows a NEGATIVE key-rate duration at one maturity. The best interpretation is:
- A. The bond is mispriced and should be sold immediately
- B. There is a calculation error; key-rate durations cannot be negative
- C. It is a normal fitting artifact — a single key-rate shift can raise the bond's value at certain maturities
- D. The bond must contain an embedded option

**Answer: C**

The question asks: how should you read a negative key-rate duration?

A negative key-rate duration is a known, normal artifact of how the curve is fitted through par key rates. A single tent-shaped key-rate shift can, at certain maturities, actually raise a bond's value, producing a negative KRD. It does not signal an error or a trading opportunity by itself.

- A overreacts; a negative KRD is not a mispricing signal.
- B is false; negative KRDs genuinely occur and GARP has tested exactly this.
- D is wrong; this happens for plain coupon bonds — no embedded option required.

Analogy: when you push up one tent pole, the fabric somewhere else can dip down a little. Likewise, nudging one key rate can nudge a bond's value the "wrong" way at another point — perfectly normal.

📌 Exam tip: Don't panic at a negative key-rate duration — it is a fitting artifact, not a mistake. Remember they still SUM to the (positive) effective duration.

**Q10.** A portfolio's KR01s are +\$300 (2-year) and +\$500 (10-year). Over a day, the 2-year key rate FALLS by 4bp while the 10-year key rate RISES by 2bp. The approximate change in portfolio value is:
- A. −\$2,200
- B. +\$200
- C. −\$200
- D. +\$2,200

**Answer: B**

The question asks: compute the P&L from a set of key-rate moves using the KR01 dot product.

The formula is:
$$\Delta P \approx -\sum_k \text{KR01}_k \times \Delta y_k^{(bp)}$$

(A positive KR01 means the portfolio loses when that rate rises, so we put a minus sign in front.)

Step 1 — 2-year contribution. The rate FELL 4bp, so $$\Delta y = -4$$:
$$-300 \times (-4) = +1{,}200$$ (a falling rate is good for a long position).

Step 2 — 10-year contribution. The rate ROSE 2bp, so $$\Delta y = +2$$:
$$-500 \times (+2) = -1{,}000$$ (a rising rate is bad).

Step 3 — add: $$+1{,}200 - 1{,}000 = +\$200$$.

- A (−\$2,200) gets both signs wrong (treats the fall as a rise and vice versa) and adds magnitudes.
- C (−\$200) flips the final sign — likely a single sign error.
- D (+\$2,200) adds the magnitudes the same way as A but keeps a positive sign.

Analogy: each key rate is a separate scoreboard — a falling 2-year scores points (gain), a rising 10-year loses points; net the two for the final score.

📌 Exam tip: P&L = −Σ (KR01 × rate change in bp). Mind the signs: a rate FALL with a positive KR01 is a GAIN. Letting one rate fall while another rises is the classic trap — the signs do all the work.
