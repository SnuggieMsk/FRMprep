**Q1.** You buy one call option contract (on 100 shares) with strike $K = \$40$ for a premium of $4.00 per share. At expiration the stock is $47. Your profit per share is closest to:
- A. $3.00
- B. $4.00
- C. $7.00
- D. −$4.00

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the profit (not just the payoff) on a long call at expiration.

Step 1 — Payoff of a long call:
$$\text{Payoff} = \max(S_T - K, 0) = \max(47 - 40, 0) = \$7.00$$

Step 2 — Profit = payoff − premium paid:
$$\text{Profit} = 7.00 - 4.00 = \$3.00$$

Why A is correct: you make $7 by exercising, but you already paid $4 for the option, so the net gain is $3.

Why the others are wrong:
- B ($4.00) is the premium you paid, not the profit.
- C ($7.00) is the payoff before subtracting the premium — the classic payoff-vs-profit trap.
- D (−$4.00) would be your loss only if the option expired worthless ($S_T \le 40$).

Analogy: you pay $4 for a coupon that lets you buy something $7 below market. You're ahead by $3 after paying for the coupon.

📌 Exam tip: Profit = payoff − premium for a buyer. Always subtract what you paid.

**Q2.** A put option with strike $K = \$60$ trades at a premium of $9.50 when the stock is at $52. The option's time value is:
- A. $0.00
- B. $1.50
- C. $8.00
- D. $9.50

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the time value, given the total premium.

Step 1 — Intrinsic value of a put = $\max(K - S, 0)$:
$$\max(60 - 52, 0) = \$8.00$$

Step 2 — Time value = premium − intrinsic value:
$$9.50 - 8.00 = \$1.50$$

Why B is correct: the premium ($9.50) splits into intrinsic value ($8.00, the immediate exercise value) and time value ($1.50, the extra paid for remaining optionality).

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($0.00) would mean the option has no time value, which is wrong since premium exceeds intrinsic value.
- C ($8.00) is the intrinsic value, not the time value.
- D ($9.50) is the whole premium, not just the time portion.

Analogy: the $9.50 price is like a sandwich: $8 of "meat" (intrinsic value you'd get today) plus $1.50 of "extra topping" (time value for the chance things improve before expiry).

📌 Exam tip: Premium = intrinsic value + time value. For a put, intrinsic = max(K − S, 0).

**Q3.** A company pays an ordinary cash dividend of $1.00 per share. An exchange-traded call option on that stock with strike $K = \$40$ will have its terms:
- A. Adjusted so the strike falls to $39
- B. Adjusted only if the option is in the money
- C. Left unchanged
- D. Adjusted so each contract covers 102.5 shares

**Answer: C**

The question tests whether ordinary cash dividends change exchange-traded option terms.

Exchange-traded options are NOT adjusted for ordinary cash dividends. The stock simply drops on the ex-dividend date, and option holders bear that — the expected dividend was already built into the premium when they bought. Option terms ARE adjusted for stock splits and stock dividends, but not ordinary cash dividends.

Why C is correct: ordinary cash dividends leave the strike and contract size unchanged.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the classic trap — "reduce the strike by the dividend." Only large/special distributions trigger discretionary adjustments, not ordinary dividends.
- B invents a rule based on moneyness that does not exist.
- D describes a stock-dividend-style share adjustment, which does not apply to cash dividends.

Analogy: everyone already knew the dividend was coming, so the option's price had it baked in — no need to retune the contract afterward.

📌 Exam tip: Splits and stock dividends adjust option terms; ordinary cash dividends do NOT.

**Q4.** After a 4-for-1 stock split, a put option contract originally with strike $K = \$120$ on 100 shares becomes:
- A. $K = \$120$ on 100 shares (no change)
- B. $K = \$30$ on 100 shares
- C. $K = \$480$ on 25 shares
- D. $K = \$30$ on 400 shares

**Answer: D**

The question asks how an n-for-m split adjusts option terms.

For an $n$-for-$m$ split: strike is multiplied by $m/n$, and the shares per contract are multiplied by $n/m$. A 4-for-1 split means $n = 4$, $m = 1$.

- New strike: $120 \times \dfrac{m}{n} = 120 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = \$30$
- New shares: $100 \times \dfrac{n}{m} = 100 \times \dfrac{4}{1} = 400$ shares

Why D is correct: the strike drops to $30 and shares rise to 400, exactly preserving the contract's economics ($120 \times 100 = \$12{,}000 = \$30 \times 400$).

Why the others are wrong:
- A ignores the adjustment — splits DO adjust terms.
- B adjusts the strike but forgets to increase the share count.
- C inverts the ratios (multiplying strike by 4 and dividing shares by 4) — the wrong direction.

Analogy: cutting one pizza into four slices: each slice is worth a quarter as much, but you now have four times as many.

📌 Exam tip: For an n-for-1 split: divide the strike by n, multiply the shares by n. Total economic value stays the same.

**Q5.** Which statement about exercise styles is correct?
- A. European options can be exercised any time before expiration
- B. American options can be exercised only at expiration
- C. American options can be exercised any time up to expiration; most exchange-traded stock options are American
- D. European options are always worth more than American options

**Answer: C**

The question tests the basic difference between American and European exercise.

American options can be exercised at any time up to and including expiration. European options can be exercised only at expiration. Most exchange-traded stock options in the US are American style.

Why C is correct: it states the American rule (exercise any time) and the real-world fact that exchange-traded stock options are typically American.

Why the others are wrong:
- A swaps the definitions — European is exercise-at-expiration-only.
- B swaps them the other way — American allows early exercise.
- D is backward: an American option has all the rights of a European one plus more, so American $\ge$ European, never strictly less.

Analogy: an American option is an "anytime" ticket; a European option is a "valid only on the final day" ticket. The anytime ticket is at least as valuable.

📌 Exam tip: American = exercise anytime (≥ European value). Most listed equity options are American.

**Q6.** An investor writes (sells) one naked call: strike $K = \$55$, stock at $S = \$51$, premium $2.00 per share. Using the two standard formulas, the initial margin per contract (100 shares) is closest to:
- A. $710
- B. $820
- C. $1,020
- D. $400

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the naked call writer's margin, which is the GREATER of two formula amounts.

The option is out of the money by $55 - 51 = \$4$ (a call is OTM when stock < strike).

Formula 1: premium + 20% of stock − amount OTM
$$2.00 + 0.20 \times 51 - 4.00 = 2.00 + 10.20 - 4.00 = \$8.20 \text{ per share}$$

Formula 2: premium + 10% of stock (for naked calls)
$$2.00 + 0.10 \times 51 = 2.00 + 5.10 = \$7.10 \text{ per share}$$

Take the greater: $\max(8.20, 7.10) = \$8.20$ per share.

Per contract (100 shares):
$$8.20 \times 100 = \$820$$

Why B is correct: it uses the larger of the two formulas times 100 shares.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($710) used Formula 2 (the smaller), but you must take the GREATER.
- C ($1,020) forgot to subtract the OTM amount in Formula 1 ($2.00 + 10.20 = 12.20$... per share $\times 100$ is wrong direction; this reflects skipping the OTM deduction).
- D ($400) just multiplied the OTM amount by 100 — not a valid formula.

Analogy: like an insurer setting a deposit — they compute two safety cushions and keep the bigger one to be safe.

📌 Exam tip: Naked writer margin = MAX of (premium + 20% stock − OTM amount) and (premium + 10% of stock for calls / strike for puts), then × 100 × contracts.

**Q7.** Why does a covered call writer (long stock, short call) generally need no margin beyond the stock position?
- A. Covered calls cannot lose money
- B. The long stock collateralizes the obligation to deliver shares
- C. The OCC waives all margin on covered positions
- D. Covered calls are always out of the money

**Answer: B**

The question asks why covered calls don't require extra margin.

If the short call is exercised, the writer must deliver shares. But the writer already OWNS the shares (the long stock). Those shares stand ready to be delivered, so there's no uncovered risk that requires extra collateral.

Why B is correct: the stock already held collateralizes the delivery obligation.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — a covered call still loses if the stock falls a lot; it just has no extra margin requirement.
- C overstates it; it's not a blanket OCC waiver, it's that the stock itself covers the obligation.
- D is false — a covered call can be ITM or OTM; moneyness isn't the reason.

Analogy: if you've promised to sell your bike and the bike is already in your garage, the buyer doesn't need you to post a cash bond — the bike is the guarantee.

📌 Exam tip: "Covered" means the underlying is already owned, so no extra margin. "Naked" means uncovered, requiring margin.

**Q8.** Which best describes the role of the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC)?
- A. It sets the premiums for all listed options
- B. It is the central counterparty that guarantees performance, becoming buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer
- C. It writes most of the options traded on exchanges
- D. It decides which stocks are allowed to pay dividends

**Answer: B**

The question asks what the OCC actually does.

The OCC is the central counterparty (clearinghouse) for listed options. It guarantees that contracts are honored by stepping in between parties — becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer — and it nets positions. On exercise it assigns the obligation to a member firm with a short position.

Why B is correct: it states the OCC's central-counterparty guarantee role precisely.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — prices are set by supply/demand and market makers, not the OCC.
- C is false — options are written by investors; the OCC clears, it doesn't write them.
- D is unrelated — dividend decisions are made by companies' boards.

Analogy: the OCC is like an escrow service that sits between buyer and seller so neither has to trust the other directly.

📌 Exam tip: OCC = central counterparty that guarantees performance and handles exercise/assignment. It does not set prices.

**Q9.** How do warrants and convertible bonds differ economically from ordinary exchange-traded call options?
- A. They are always cheaper than exchange-traded options
- B. They are issued by the company itself and create new shares (dilution) when exercised
- C. They can never be exercised early
- D. They are cleared by the OCC like listed options

**Answer: B**

The question contrasts company-issued instruments with regular listed options.

Warrants, employee stock options, and the conversion feature of convertibles are issued by the company itself. When exercised, the company issues NEW shares, which dilutes existing shareholders. Ordinary exchange-traded options are written by investors and settled with existing shares, so there's no dilution.

Why B is correct: the issuer is the company, and exercise creates new shares (dilution).

Why the others are wrong:
- A is not generally true — pricing varies; dilution and custom terms, not a blanket discount, are the distinction.
- C is false — exercise style varies; this isn't the defining difference.
- D is false — warrants/convertibles are company instruments, not OCC-cleared listed options.

Analogy: a listed option is like reselling an existing concert ticket; a warrant is the venue printing a brand-new ticket, which means a slightly more crowded show for everyone (dilution).

📌 Exam tip: Company-issued instruments (warrants, ESOs, convertibles) create new shares → dilution. Listed options use existing shares → no dilution.

**Q10.** A binary (digital) call option:
- A. Pays the full intrinsic value $\max(S_T - K, 0)$
- B. Pays a fixed amount if it finishes in the money, and nothing otherwise
- C. Must always be exercised early
- D. Can never expire worthless

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the defining feature of a binary (digital) option.

A binary call pays a fixed, pre-set amount if the option is in the money at expiration, and pays nothing if it is not. The payoff is "all or nothing," unlike a vanilla option whose payoff scales with how far in the money it is.

Why B is correct: fixed payoff if ITM, zero otherwise — the "digital" (0 or 1) nature.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes a vanilla call, whose payoff scales with $S_T - K$, not a fixed amount.
- C is false — there's no requirement to exercise early; it settles based on the finish.
- D is false — a binary option pays nothing (expires worthless) if it finishes out of the money.

Analogy: a binary option is like a bet that pays a flat $100 if your team wins and $0 if it loses — the margin of victory doesn't matter.

📌 Exam tip: Binary/digital = fixed payoff if ITM, else nothing. Vanilla = payoff grows with how deep ITM it is.
