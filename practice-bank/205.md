**Q1.** A sample of four returns is 4, 6, 8, 10. What is the unbiased sample variance?
- A. 5.0
- B. 6.0
- C. 6.67
- D. 20.0

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the *unbiased* sample variance, which uses the $$n - 1$$ divisor.

Step 1 — sample mean:

$$\bar{X} = \frac{4 + 6 + 8 + 10}{4} = \frac{28}{4} = 7$$

Step 2 — deviations from the mean and their squares:
- $$4 - 7 = -3 \Rightarrow 9$$
- $$6 - 7 = -1 \Rightarrow 1$$
- $$8 - 7 = +1 \Rightarrow 1$$
- $$10 - 7 = +3 \Rightarrow 9$$

Sum of squared deviations $$= 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 = 20$$.

Step 3 — divide by $$n - 1 = 4 - 1 = 3$$:

$$s^2 = \frac{20}{3} = 6.67$$

So the unbiased sample variance is 6.67 (option C).

- A (5.0) divides by $$n = 4$$ (the biased estimator: $$20/4 = 5$$), but the question asked for unbiased.
- B (6.0) is a miscalculation of the sum of squares or divisor.
- D (20.0) is just the sum of squared deviations, with no division at all.

📌 Exam tip: "Unbiased" / "sample" variance uses $$n - 1$$. Population or "biased" variance uses $$n$$. Read the stem carefully — this divisor choice is a guaranteed trap.

**Q2.** An estimator whose expected value equals the true parameter for every sample size is described as:
- A. Consistent
- B. Efficient
- C. Unbiased
- D. BLUE

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the property defined by "expected value equals the true parameter."

By definition, an estimator $$\hat\theta$$ is unbiased if its bias is zero:

$$\mathrm{Bias}(\hat\theta) = E[\hat\theta] - \theta = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad E[\hat\theta] = \theta$$

This must hold for every sample size, which is exactly what the question states. So option C is correct. On average, an unbiased estimator hits the bullseye.

- A (consistent) is an *asymptotic* property: the estimator converges to the truth as $$n \to \infty$$. A consistent estimator can be biased in small samples, so "for every sample size" rules it out.
- B (efficient) means smallest variance among unbiased estimators — about precision, not centering.
- D (BLUE) is a stronger combined property (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator); it includes unbiasedness but adds "linear" and "minimum variance," which the bare definition given doesn't require.

📌 Exam tip: Unbiased = correct *on average* at any $$n$$. Consistent = correct in the *limit* as $$n \to \infty$$. These are different; an estimator can be one without the other.

**Q3.** The Central Limit Theorem states that for iid data with finite variance, as $$n$$ grows:
- A. The raw data become normally distributed.
- B. The sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal distribution.
- C. The sample variance approaches zero.
- D. The population mean approaches the sample mean.

**Answer: B**

The question tests the precise statement of the Central Limit Theorem (CLT).

The CLT says: for iid data with finite variance, the *sampling distribution of the sample mean* becomes approximately normal as $$n$$ grows, regardless of the shape of the original data:

$$\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)$$

So option B is correct. This is why z- and t-tests on means work even when the underlying data are skewed, once $$n$$ is reasonably large (rule of thumb 30+).

- A is the most common trap: the CLT does NOT make the *raw data* normal. Skewed data stay skewed; it's the *average* whose distribution becomes normal.
- C confuses the CLT with the behavior of the standard error. The sample variance estimates $$\sigma^2$$ and does not go to zero; the variance *of the mean* shrinks, but that's not "the sample variance."
- D reverses the Law of Large Numbers: the *sample* mean approaches the *population* mean, not the other way around.

📌 Exam tip: CLT is about the *distribution of the mean*, not the raw data. LLN says where the mean goes ($$\to \mu$$); CLT says how it fluctuates (normally).

**Q4.** Quadrupling the sample size changes the standard error of the mean by a factor of:
- A. 1/16
- B. 1/4
- C. 1/2
- D. 2

**Answer: C**

The question asks how the standard error of the mean responds to multiplying $$n$$ by 4.

The standard error of the mean is:

$$SE(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

It is inversely proportional to $$\sqrt{n}$$. If $$n$$ becomes $$4n$$, then:

$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{4n}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

So the standard error is multiplied by $$1/2$$ (option C). To halve your error, you need 4× the data — diminishing returns.

- A (1/16) squares the factor incorrectly, treating SE as proportional to $$1/n^2$$.
- B (1/4) treats SE as proportional to $$1/n$$, forgetting the square root.
- D (2) gets the direction wrong; more data *reduces* error, it doesn't double it.

📌 Exam tip: $$SE \propto 1/\sqrt{n}$$. To cut the standard error in half, you must *quadruple* the sample size. Squaring the data improvement gives the cost.

**Q5.** Losses have mean $10M and standard deviation $30M, with an unknown, skewed distribution. For a sample of $$n = 100$$ independent losses, the standard error of the average loss is:
- A. $0.30M
- B. $3.00M
- C. $30.0M
- D. $300M

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the standard error (standard deviation) of the *average* of 100 independent losses.

The standard error of the mean is:

$$SE(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{30}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{30}{10} = 3$$

So the standard error is $3M (option B). Averaging 100 losses smooths out the noise, shrinking the spread from $30M (for one loss) to $3M (for the average).

- A ($0.30M) divides by $$n = 100$$ instead of $$\sqrt{n} = 10$$.
- C ($30.0M) is the standard deviation of a *single* loss, forgetting to divide by $$\sqrt{n}$$.
- D ($300M) multiplies by $$\sqrt{n}$$ instead of dividing — that would be the SD of the *sum*, and even then it's $$30 \times 10 = 300$$, but the question asks about the average.

📌 Exam tip: Standard error of the mean = $$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$$. Divide by $$\sqrt{n}$$, not $$n$$. The CLT lets you treat the average as approximately normal even with skewed individual losses.

**Q6.** Using the setup in Q5 (average loss approximately $$N(10, 3^2)$$ for $$n=100$$), what is the approximate probability that the average loss exceeds $16M? (Use: $$P(Z > 2) \approx 2.28\%$$.)
- A. 2.28%
- B. 5.00%
- C. 16.00%
- D. 50.00%

**Answer: A**

The question asks for a tail probability of the sample mean, using the CLT-justified normal approximation $$\bar{X} \sim N(10, 3^2)$$.

Step 1 — standardize the cutoff $16M into a z-score (mean 10, standard deviation 3):

$$z = \frac{16 - 10}{3} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

Step 2 — read the upper tail:

$$P(\bar{X} > 16) \approx P(Z > 2) = 2.28\%$$

So the answer is 2.28% (option A). The CLT lets us use the normal even though individual losses are skewed, because we're looking at an *average* of 100.

- B (5.00%) corresponds to $$z \approx 1.645$$, a shallower cutoff than $$z = 2$$.
- C (16.00%) corresponds to $$z \approx 1$$, again the wrong z-score (likely standardizing with $$\sigma = 6$$ or skipping the $$\sqrt{n}$$ step).
- D (50.00%) is the probability of exceeding the mean ($16 vs $10), ignoring the standardization entirely.

📌 Exam tip: Standardize using the standard error of the *mean* ($$\sigma/\sqrt{n} = 3$$), not the raw $$\sigma = 30$$. Then read the normal tail. $$z=2 \to 2.28\%$$ in one tail.

**Q7.** Three paired observations are $$X$$: 1, 3, 5 and $$Y$$: 2, 2, 8. The means are $$\bar{X}=3$$, $$\bar{Y}=4$$. The sample covariance (using the $$n-1$$ divisor) is:
- A. 4
- B. 6
- C. 12
- D. 24

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the sample covariance using the unbiased $$n - 1$$ divisor.

The formula is:

$$\hat\sigma_{XY} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})$$

Compute the deviations and their products:
- Obs 1: $$(1-3)(2-4) = (-2)(-2) = 4$$
- Obs 2: $$(3-3)(2-4) = (0)(-2) = 0$$
- Obs 3: $$(5-3)(8-4) = (2)(4) = 8$$

Sum of products $$= 4 + 0 + 8 = 12$$.

Divide by $$n - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2$$:

$$\hat\sigma_{XY} = \frac{12}{2} = 6$$

So the sample covariance is 6 (option B).

- A (4) is only the first product, not the full sum.
- C (12) is the sum of products without dividing by $$n - 1$$.
- D (24) divides nothing and doubles, or uses a wrong divisor.

📌 Exam tip: Sample covariance sums the products of paired deviations, then divides by $$n - 1$$. Don't forget the divisor, and keep the signs of each deviation straight.

**Q8.** Which statement correctly describes the meaning of "the sample mean is BLUE"?
- A. It is the estimator with the smallest possible bias.
- B. It is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator of the population mean.
- C. It assumes the data are normally distributed.
- D. It is biased but consistent.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what the acronym BLUE means for the sample mean.

BLUE stands for **Best Linear Unbiased Estimator**. For the population mean, the sample mean is BLUE: among all estimators that are (i) linear in the data and (ii) unbiased, it has the smallest variance ("best" = minimum variance). This requires only iid data with finite variance — no assumption that the data are normal. So option B is correct.

- A is imprecise: BLUE estimators are *unbiased* (zero bias), and "best" refers to minimum *variance*, not minimum bias.
- C is wrong: the BLUE property does not require normality, only iid with finite variance.
- D contradicts the definition: BLUE estimators are unbiased, not biased.

📌 Exam tip: BLUE = Best (minimum variance) Linear Unbiased Estimator. "Best" means smallest variance among unbiased linear estimators; normality is NOT required.

**Q9.** A sample's data are heavily skewed with a few extreme outliers. Which measure of central tendency is most robust to those outliers?
- A. The mean
- B. The median
- C. The variance
- D. The standard error

**Answer: B**

The question asks which location measure is least affected by extreme values.

The median is simply the middle value when the data are sorted. Changing a few extreme observations (even making them enormous) does not move the middle value much, because the median only cares about *rank*, not magnitude. So the median is robust to outliers — option B.

The mean, by contrast, adds up every value, so one giant outlier can drag it far away.

- A (the mean) is *not* robust; a single extreme value can pull it strongly in that direction.
- C (the variance) is a measure of *spread*, not central tendency, and it is extremely sensitive to outliers (it squares deviations).
- D (the standard error) is also a spread/precision measure, not a measure of central tendency, and inherits the variance's sensitivity to outliers.

📌 Exam tip: Median = robust to outliers (rank-based). Mean and variance = sensitive (value-based). For skewed financial data, the median often tells a steadier story.

**Q10.** For two jointly normal random variables, the coskewness is:
- A. Always 0
- B. Always 1
- C. Equal to their correlation
- D. Undefined

**Answer: A**

The question asks about the coskewness of jointly normal variables.

Coskewness extends the idea of skewness to a pair of variables, capturing how one variable behaves when the other moves to extremes. The normal distribution is perfectly symmetric, so it has zero skewness — and jointly normal variables likewise have zero coskewness. So option A is correct. (As a related fact, the central *cokurtosis* measure for jointly normal variables equals 1, not 0 — don't confuse the two.)

- B (always 1) is the value of the *central cokurtosis* measure under joint normality, not coskewness.
- C (equal to correlation) confuses a third-moment co-measure with a second-moment relationship; coskewness is not the correlation.
- D (undefined) is wrong; coskewness is well-defined and simply equals zero here.

📌 Exam tip: Joint normality ⟹ coskewness = 0 (symmetry) and central cokurtosis = 1. Negative coskewness with the market signals an asset that suffers when the market is volatile/falling, deserving extra risk premium.
