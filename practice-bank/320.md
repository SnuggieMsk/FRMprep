**Q1.** In a plain vanilla interest rate swap, the notional principal is:
- A. Exchanged at initiation only
- B. Exchanged at both initiation and maturity
- C. Never exchanged; it is only used to compute the interest payments
- D. Exchanged only if one party defaults

**Answer: C**

The question asks what happens to the notional (face) amount in a standard interest rate swap.

Why C is correct: in a plain vanilla interest rate swap, both legs are in the SAME currency, so exchanging the principal would be pointless (each side would just hand the other the same amount). The notional is only a reference number used to calculate the fixed and floating interest payments, which are netted each period. The notional itself never changes hands.

Why the others are wrong:
- A and B — exchanging principal happens in CURRENCY swaps (different currencies), not in single-currency interest rate swaps.
- D — default triggers replacement-cost exposure and ISDA netting, but it does not cause a "notional exchange."

Analogy: two friends agree to swap interest on an imaginary $1 million loan. They never actually pass $1 million back and forth — they just use it to figure out who owes whom the interest difference each month.

📌 Exam tip: Interest rate swap → notional is NEVER exchanged (same currency). Currency swap → principals ARE exchanged at start and end (different currencies). This contrast is heavily tested.

**Q2.** A counterparty PAYS fixed in a $100 million interest rate swap. Current zero rates give $$B_{\text{fixed}} = \$102.0$$M and $$B_{\text{float}} = \$100.5$$M. The value of the swap to this counterparty is:
- A. +$1.5M
- B. −$1.5M
- C. +$2.0M
- D. −$0.5M

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the value of the swap to the PAY-FIXED (payer) party, using the two-bond view.

For the payer, the swap is like being SHORT the fixed bond and LONG the floating bond:
$$V_{\text{payer}} = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$

Plug in:
$$V = 100.5 - 102.0 = -1.5$$

So B is correct: −$1.5M.

Plain words: the payer is paying out the fixed coupons (the fixed bond, worth $102.0M to give up) and receiving the floating coupons (the floating bond, worth $100.5M to receive). Since what they pay is worth more than what they receive, the swap has a negative value of −$1.5M.

- A (+$1.5M) is the value to the RECEIVE-FIXED party (the receiver) — the sign-flip trap. The payer's value is the opposite.
- C (+$2.0M) ignores the floating leg entirely.
- D (−$0.5M) subtracts in a muddled way; not the correct formula.

📌 Exam tip: Receiver value = $$B_{\text{fixed}} - B_{\text{float}}$$; Payer value = $$B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$ (opposite sign). Always confirm WHICH side the question asks about before reporting the sign.

**Q3.** Just after a reset date (the next floating rate has just been set and the current payment made), the floating leg of a vanilla interest rate swap is worth:
- A. Zero
- B. The notional principal (par)
- C. The present value of all projected future floating coupons only
- D. The fixed-leg value minus the swap value

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the value of the floating-rate side immediately after a reset.

Why B is correct: a floating-rate note always reprices to PAR (its notional) right at each reset date. This is because the coupon for the next period is set at the current market rate, so the note is "fairly priced" at face value. This fact makes swap valuation fast: at a reset, $$B_{\text{float}}$$ = notional.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — zero is the value of the whole SWAP at initiation, not the value of the floating leg.
- C — you do NOT project all future floating coupons; the floating note reprices to par, which already captures all of them.
- D — that's a circular, made-up relationship, not how the floating leg is valued.

Analogy: a floating-rate note is like a savings account that always pays the current going rate — right after the rate resets, the account is worth exactly its balance (par), because it's neither over- nor under-paying.

📌 Exam tip: At a reset date, $$B_{\text{float}}$$ = par (notional). BETWEEN resets, it equals (notional + the already-fixed next payment) discounted back to the next payment date. Don't project floating coupons to maturity.

**Q4.** Firm X can borrow fixed at 6.0% or floating at SOFR + 0.2%; Firm Y can borrow fixed at 7.0% or floating at SOFR + 0.6%. The total gain available to be shared from a swap is:
- A. 1.0%
- B. 0.4%
- C. 0.6%
- D. 1.4%

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the total comparative-advantage gain available when the two firms borrow where each is relatively cheap and then swap.

The total gain equals the difference between the two firms' borrowing-cost spreads:
$$\text{Gain} = (\text{fixed-rate difference}) - (\text{floating-rate difference})$$

Step 1 — fixed-rate difference (Y minus X):
$$7.0\% - 6.0\% = 1.0\%$$

Step 2 — floating-rate difference (Y minus X):
$$(\text{SOFR} + 0.6\%) - (\text{SOFR} + 0.2\%) = 0.4\%$$

Step 3 — subtract:
$$1.0\% - 0.4\% = 0.6\%$$

So C is correct: 0.6% total gain to be shared.

Plain words: Firm Y pays 1.0% more than X in the fixed market but only 0.4% more in the floating market. That mismatch (1.0% − 0.4% = 0.6%) is the total savings the two can split by each borrowing where they're relatively best and swapping.

- A (1.0%) is just the fixed-rate difference alone — only half the calculation.
- B (0.4%) is just the floating-rate difference alone.
- D (1.4%) wrongly ADDS the two differences instead of subtracting.

📌 Exam tip: Total swap gain = |fixed spread − floating spread|. Be careful: the criticism of comparative advantage is that the fixed spread is locked-in long-term while the floating spread can be repriced at the next reset — so the "gain" partly compensates for rollover risk.

**Q5.** A swap in which one party pays the total return on a stock index in exchange for a fixed or floating rate is a(n):
- A. Basis swap
- B. Equity swap
- C. Amortizing swap
- D. Currency swap

**Answer: B**

The question describes exchanging the return on a stock index for an interest rate.

Why B is correct: an equity swap exchanges the total return on an equity index (price change plus dividends) for a fixed or floating interest rate. It lets an investor get equity exposure ("equitize cash") without actually buying the stocks.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — a basis swap exchanges one FLOATING rate for ANOTHER floating rate (e.g., SOFR vs. commercial paper), plus a spread; no equity involved.
- C — an amortizing swap is a regular interest rate swap where the NOTIONAL shrinks over time; still rates, not equity.
- D — a currency swap exchanges principal and interest in two different CURRENCIES; no equity index involved.

Analogy: an equity swap is like betting on the stock market's performance through a contract, while paying a steady "rental fee" (the interest leg), without ever owning the actual shares.

📌 Exam tip: Match the swap to its legs — equity swap = index return vs. rate; basis swap = floating vs. floating; currency swap = different currencies; amortizing = shrinking notional.

**Q6.** A $100 million interest rate swap has 1.25 years left; the institution PAYS 8% fixed (semiannual) and receives 6-month floating. Continuous zero rates are 10% (3 mo), 10.5% (9 mo), 11% (15 mo). Using the given discount factors, the fixed bond is $$B_{\text{fixed}} = 4(0.97531) + 4(0.92427) + 104(0.87153)$$. This equals closest to:
- A. $98.24M
- B. $100.00M
- C. $104.00M
- D. $96.00M

**Answer: A**

The question asks us to compute the value of the fixed-rate bond leg by discounting each fixed cash flow at its matching zero rate.

The institution pays an 8% semiannual coupon on $100M, so each semiannual coupon is $$8\%/2 \times 100 = \$4$$M, paid at 0.25, 0.75, and 1.25 years; the $100M notional comes at 1.25 years (so the last cash flow is 4 + 100 = 104).

Step 1 — first coupon (3 months):
$$4 \times 0.97531 = 3.9012$$

Step 2 — second coupon (9 months):
$$4 \times 0.92427 = 3.6971$$

Step 3 — final coupon + notional (15 months):
$$104 \times 0.87153 = 90.6391$$

Step 4 — sum:
$$3.9012 + 3.6971 + 90.6391 = 98.2374 \approx \$98.24\text{M}$$

So A is correct.

Plain words: a fixed-rate bond is just a set of known future payments. Discount each one back to today using the rate for its date, then add them all up.

- B ($100.00M) assumes the bond trades at par, but the 8% coupon is BELOW the ~10–11% rates, so it trades at a DISCOUNT (below par).
- C ($104.00M) just sums the raw cash flows with no discounting.
- D ($96.00M) under-discounts incorrectly; not produced by the steps.

📌 Exam tip: A fixed bond with a coupon BELOW current rates prices BELOW par. Discount each coupon plus the final notional at its own zero rate and sum. Sanity-check against par.

**Q7.** Continuing Q6, the floating leg is valued as (notional + the known next payment) discounted to the next reset. The next floating payment was set at 10.2% semiannual, so it equals $$100 \times 0.102/2 = \$5.1$$M, due in 3 months ($$e^{-0.10 \times 0.25} = 0.97531$$). The floating bond $$B_{\text{float}}$$ is closest to:
- A. $100.00M
- B. $102.51M
- C. $105.10M
- D. $98.24M

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the value of the floating-rate side between resets. The trick: only the NEXT payment is already known; everything after that is captured by the note repricing to par at the next reset.

So $$B_{\text{float}}$$ = (notional + next known coupon) discounted to the next payment date.

Step 1 — the known next floating payment:
$$100 \times \frac{0.102}{2} = 100 \times 0.051 = 5.1$$

Step 2 — add the notional (which is worth par at the next reset):
$$100 + 5.1 = 105.1$$

Step 3 — discount from 3 months back to today:
$$105.1 \times 0.97531 = 102.505 \approx \$102.51\text{M}$$

So B is correct.

Plain words: think of the floating note as a short bond that pays $5.1M plus $100M par in 3 months. We discount that single bundle back to today.

- A ($100.00M) wrongly assumes par BETWEEN resets — the note is par only AT a reset, not between.
- C ($105.10M) forgets to discount the bundle back to today.
- D ($98.24M) is the FIXED bond value from Q6, not the floating one.

📌 Exam tip: Between resets, $$B_{\text{float}}$$ = (notional + already-fixed next payment) × discount factor to the next payment date. Never project floating coupons beyond the next one.

**Q8.** Combining Q6 and Q7 ($$B_{\text{fixed}} = \$98.24$$M, $$B_{\text{float}} = \$102.51$$M), the value of the PAY-FIXED swap to the institution is closest to:
- A. −$4.27M
- B. +$4.27M
- C. +$200.75M
- D. −$0.51M

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the total swap value to the payer, now that both bond legs are known.

For the payer (pays fixed, receives floating):
$$V_{\text{payer}} = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$

Plug in:
$$V = 102.51 - 98.24 = +4.27$$

So B is correct: +$4.27M.

Plain words: the institution is paying only 8% fixed while the market rates are around 10–11%. Paying a below-market fixed rate is a good deal, so the swap is worth a positive amount to the payer.

- A (−$4.27M) flips the sign — that would be the RECEIVER's value. Paying below-market fixed is favorable, so the payer's value is positive.
- C (+$200.75M) adds the two bonds instead of subtracting.
- D (−$0.51M) is unrelated arithmetic.

📌 Exam tip: When the fixed rate you PAY is below current market rates, the payer swap is IN THE MONEY (positive value). Payer value = $$B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}$$; receiver value is the negative of that.

**Q9.** Compared with an interest rate swap of equal notional and maturity, a currency swap typically has:
- A. Less credit risk, because principals are exchanged
- B. Greater credit risk, concentrated late in the swap's life because of the principal exchange
- C. Identical credit risk, since both are valued as bond portfolios
- D. No credit risk if it is cleared bilaterally

**Answer: B**

The question asks how the credit risk of a currency swap compares to that of an interest rate swap.

Why B is correct: a currency swap exchanges PRINCIPAL at maturity (in two different currencies). That final principal exchange is large, so the potential exposure GROWS toward the end of the swap's life. By contrast, an interest rate swap never exchanges principal, so its exposure peaks mid-life and is much smaller. Hence currency swaps carry materially MORE credit risk than rate swaps of the same notional.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — exchanging principals ADDS risk (the big final exchange), it does not reduce it.
- C — although both can be valued as bond portfolios, their credit-exposure PROFILES differ a lot; they are not identical.
- D — clearing reduces but does not eliminate credit risk, and "bilaterally cleared" isn't the same as CCP clearing anyway.

Analogy: an interest rate swap only ever settles small interest differences. A currency swap also hands over a big lump sum of principal at the end — and the risk that your counterparty fails right before that huge final payment grows as you approach maturity.

📌 Exam tip: Interest rate swap exposure PEAKS MID-LIFE (no principal). Currency swap exposure GROWS toward maturity (principal exchange dominates) → currency swaps are riskier for the same notional.

**Q10.** A swap exposes a counterparty to credit (default) risk only when:
- A. The swap's value to that counterparty is positive
- B. The swap's value to that counterparty is negative
- C. Interest rates are rising
- D. The notional principal exceeds $100 million

**Answer: A**

The question asks when a party in a swap actually faces credit risk from the other side defaulting.

Why A is correct: you only LOSE from a counterparty's default when they OWE you money — that is, when the swap has a POSITIVE value to you (it is an asset). If the counterparty walks away then, you lose that positive replacement value. If the swap has negative value to you, you owe them, so their default doesn't cost you (you might even benefit). So credit exposure exists only when the swap's value is positive to you.

Why the others are wrong:
- B — if the swap is negative to you, YOU are the one who owes; their default does not create a loss for you.
- C — rising or falling rates change the swap's value, but credit risk depends on the SIGN of that value, not the direction of rates by itself.
- D — the size of the notional does not determine WHETHER credit risk exists; even small swaps have exposure when in-the-money. (And exposure is far smaller than notional — it's the replacement cost.)

Analogy: you only fear a borrower running off if they OWE you money. If you actually owe THEM, their disappearing doesn't hurt your wallet.

📌 Exam tip: Swap credit exposure = the swap's value WHEN POSITIVE (the replacement cost) — far smaller than notional, and mitigated by netting, collateral, and CCP clearing under ISDA.
