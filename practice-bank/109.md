**Q1.** Metallgesellschaft's (MGRM) losses are best attributed to:
- A. Speculative directional bets that oil prices would rise
- B. Funding and rollover strains from hedging long-dated supply contracts with short-dated futures
- C. A counterparty defaulting on its supply contracts
- D. A rogue trader concealing positions in a secret account

**Answer: B**

The question asks which risk really sank Metallgesellschaft.

MGRM had promised to deliver oil at fixed prices far into the future (long-dated contracts). To hedge, it used a "stack-and-roll" strategy: many short-dated futures that had to be rolled over again and again. When oil prices fell, those short-dated futures generated huge cash margin calls and rollover losses RIGHT AWAY, even though the long-dated contracts would have profited later. The firm couldn't fund the cash drain and unwound at the worst time. That is funding liquidity risk from a maturity mismatch, so B is correct.

Analogy: you bet on a slow-cooking dish that pays off in an hour, but you have to keep feeding coins into the oven every minute — and you run out of coins before dinner is ready.

- A is wrong: MGRM was hedging, not making a one-way speculative bet that prices would rise.
- C is wrong: the problem was its own cash flows on the hedge, not a counterparty defaulting.
- D is wrong: there was no rogue trader hiding positions; that describes Barings or SocGen.

📌 Exam tip: Metallgesellschaft = an economically reasonable hedge killed by funding liquidity (margin calls) and maturity mismatch on the futures roll — NOT speculation.

**Q2.** Which case is correctly matched to its PRIMARY risk type?
- A. Barings — model risk
- B. Northern Rock — funding liquidity risk
- C. Enron — cyber risk
- D. LTCM — rogue trading

**Answer: B**

The question asks which case-to-risk pairing is correct.

Northern Rock was a UK mortgage lender that funded most of its long-term loans with short-term wholesale and securitization money. When those markets froze in 2007, it could not roll its funding and suffered the first UK bank run in 140 years. That is the textbook funding liquidity case, so B is correct.

- A is wrong: Barings is the classic ROGUE TRADING case (Nick Leeson), not model risk.
- C is wrong: Enron is a CORPORATE GOVERNANCE / accounting fraud case, not cyber risk.
- D is wrong: LTCM is primarily a MODEL RISK (plus leverage and liquidity) case, not rogue trading.

📌 Exam tip: Lock in the famous pairs: Northern Rock = funding liquidity, Barings = rogue trading, Enron = governance, LTCM = model risk.

**Q3.** The common thread linking Barings (Leeson) and Société Générale (Kerviel) is:
- A. Heavy reliance on flawed VaR models
- B. Off-balance-sheet conduit funding
- C. Weak segregation of duties that let a trader hide unauthorized positions
- D. Mis-selling complex derivatives to corporate clients

**Answer: C**

The question asks what these two rogue-trading disasters had in common.

In both cases, a single trader exploited gaps in back-office controls to conceal large unauthorized positions. Leeson controlled both trading and the back office in Singapore and hid losses in account 88888; Kerviel used his back-office knowledge to fake offsetting trades. The shared root cause is weak segregation of duties — the front office and the controls weren't properly separated. So C is correct.

Analogy: it's like letting the same person both spend the company credit card and approve the expense reports — fraud becomes easy.

- A is wrong: VaR model failures describe LTCM or the London Whale, not these rogue-trading cases.
- B is wrong: off-balance-sheet conduits describe Sachsen Landesbank, not Barings/SocGen.
- D is wrong: mis-selling derivatives to corporates describes Bankers Trust, not these cases.

📌 Exam tip: Rogue-trading cases (Barings, Sumitomo, Kerviel) all point to one lesson: segregate front and back office, and investigate profits that look "too good."

**Q4.** The collapse of LTCM in 1998 most directly demonstrated that:
- A. Convergence trades are always unprofitable
- B. In a crisis, correlations and market liquidity can move sharply away from model assumptions, and leverage magnifies the damage
- C. Regulators will always bail out hedge funds with public money
- D. Interest rate risk is the dominant risk for all hedge funds

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the central lesson of LTCM's near-failure.

LTCM ran convergence trades at roughly 25x leverage, with models assuming fairly normal price moves and stable correlations. When Russia defaulted in 1998, investors fled to safe assets, correlations jumped toward 1, liquidity dried up, and a margin spiral began. The huge leverage turned a model error into a near-collapse. So B captures the lesson.

Analogy: the model assumed the boats would drift apart calmly; in the storm they all slammed together at once, and the firm had borrowed 25 boats for every one it owned.

- A is wrong: convergence trades are not always losers; the problem was extreme conditions plus leverage, not the strategy in normal times.
- C is wrong: the LTCM rescue was a PRIVATE, Fed-COORDINATED bailout, not public money, and bailouts are never guaranteed.
- D is wrong: interest rate risk was not the dominant cause; correlation/liquidity breakdown and leverage were.

📌 Exam tip: LTCM = model risk + leverage + liquidity. Crisis correlations rise toward 1 and liquidity vanishes; leverage magnifies any model error.

**Q5.** The savings and loan (S&L) crisis of the 1980s is primarily an example of:
- A. Cyber risk
- B. Interest rate risk
- C. Rogue trading
- D. Reputational risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks which risk type the S&L crisis illustrates.

Thrifts (S&Ls) had funded long-term, fixed-rate mortgages using short-term deposits. When interest rates spiked, they had to pay much more on the short-term deposits while still earning the old low rate on the long mortgages — a negative spread. That is a classic asset-liability repricing mismatch, which is interest rate risk. So B is correct.

Analogy: you locked in renting out your house cheaply for 30 years, but the cost of your own short-term loan suddenly jumps — you bleed cash every month.

- A is wrong: cyber risk describes the SWIFT/Bangladesh Bank attack, not the S&Ls.
- C is wrong: rogue trading describes Barings; the S&L problem was structural, about rates.
- D is wrong: reputational risk describes Volkswagen; the S&L core issue was interest rates.

📌 Exam tip: S&L crisis = interest rate risk (repricing/maturity gap). Funding long fixed-rate assets with short-term deposits is the danger.

**Q6.** The London Whale episode at JPMorgan's CIO is best classified in GARP's taxonomy as a case of:
- A. Reputational risk only
- B. Model risk and operational/control failures
- C. Pure credit default by a counterparty
- D. Interest rate risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks how to classify the London Whale loss.

The CIO's synthetic credit portfolio ballooned, and the VaR model was changed (with spreadsheet errors) in a way that halved the reported risk. Limit breaches were ignored and positions were mismarked, leading to about $6 billion in losses. The combination of bad model governance and failed controls makes this a model-risk plus operational-risk case. So B is correct.

Analogy: it's like quietly swapping the bathroom scale for one that reads light, then ignoring the doctor's warnings — the problem is both the rigged measurement (model) and the ignored alarms (controls).

- A is wrong: there was reputational fallout, but the PRIMARY classification is model and operational risk.
- C is wrong: this was not a counterparty defaulting; the loss came from the firm's own positions and risk measurement.
- D is wrong: interest rate risk was not the driver; it was a credit-derivatives portfolio with broken risk governance.

📌 Exam tip: London Whale lessons: independent model validation, escalate limit breaches, and a "hedge" that keeps growing for profit is really a prop trade.

**Q7.** Volkswagen's 2015 "defeat device" emissions scandal is the standard example of:
- A. Funding liquidity risk
- B. Model risk
- C. Reputational risk arising from a conduct failure
- D. Interest rate risk

**Answer: C**

The question asks which risk type the VW scandal represents.

VW installed software that cheated emissions tests. When this conduct failure came to light, it triggered fines, recalls, criminal charges, and roughly a 30%+ drop in the share price. The damage flowed from harm to the company's reputation and trust, making this the classic reputational-risk case. So C is correct.

Analogy: a restaurant caught faking its hygiene scores may not run out of cash overnight, but customers stop coming — the brand damage is the real blow.

- A is wrong: VW's issue was not an inability to fund itself; it was reputational.
- B is wrong: there was no risk-model breakdown at the center of this; it was deliberate misconduct.
- D is wrong: interest rates had nothing to do with it.

📌 Exam tip: Volkswagen = reputational risk from a conduct/compliance failure. Cutting ethical corners can erase decades of brand value.

**Q8.** Enron's 2001 collapse is primarily an example of:
- A. Corporate governance and accounting fraud
- B. Funding liquidity risk
- C. Cyber risk
- D. Hedging implementation risk

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the primary risk type behind Enron.

Enron used aggressive mark-to-market accounting and hid debt in special purpose entities (SPEs) to fabricate earnings. Its board and its auditor (Arthur Andersen) failed to catch or stop this. The core failure was governance and accounting fraud, which is why the Sarbanes-Oxley Act followed. So A is correct.

Analogy: it's like a family hiding huge credit-card debt in a relative's name so their household budget looks healthy — until it all unravels.

- B is wrong: while Enron did run out of cash at the end, the root cause was fraudulent reporting and weak governance, not a classic funding run.
- C is wrong: cyber risk describes the SWIFT attack, not Enron.
- D is wrong: hedging implementation risk describes Metallgesellschaft, not Enron.

📌 Exam tip: Enron = corporate governance / accounting fraud (SPEs, mark-to-market abuse, failed board and auditor). It led to Sarbanes-Oxley.

**Q9.** The 2016 SWIFT / Bangladesh Bank incident, in which hackers used stolen credentials to order nearly $1 billion in fraudulent transfers, is the textbook example of:
- A. Market risk
- B. Cyber risk as part of operational risk
- C. Interest rate risk
- D. Reputational risk only

**Answer: B**

The question asks which risk type the SWIFT/Bangladesh Bank attack illustrates.

Hackers obtained stolen SWIFT credentials and instructed the New York Fed to send out $951 million; about $81 million slipped through via Philippine casinos. The lesson is that cyber controls, payment authentication, and reconciliation are core parts of operational risk — and that people and processes, not just firewalls, matter. So B is correct.

Analogy: a thief who steals the master key and the right paperwork can walk out the front door — locks alone don't help if the keys and checks fail.

- A is wrong: no market price movement caused this loss; it was a security breach.
- C is wrong: interest rates were irrelevant here.
- D is wrong: there was reputational damage, but the PRIMARY classification is cyber/operational risk.

📌 Exam tip: SWIFT/Bangladesh Bank = cyber risk within operational risk. Authentication, controls, and reconciliation matter as much as technical firewalls.

**Q10.** Across many of the disasters (LTCM, Lehman, Northern Rock, Orange County), one recurring fatal combination was:
- A. Low leverage with highly liquid assets and stable long-term funding
- B. Leverage plus illiquid assets funded with short-term money, so losses became fatal only when funding ran
- C. Excessive holdings of cash and government bonds
- D. Over-diversification across too many uncorrelated assets

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the common dangerous pattern shared by several blow-ups.

In LTCM, Lehman, Northern Rock, and Orange County, the deadly recipe was the same: high leverage, hard-to-sell (illiquid) assets, and reliance on short-term funding that had to be rolled over constantly. Ordinary losses only turned deadly when lenders refused to keep rolling the funding, forcing fire sales. So B describes the recurring killer combination.

Analogy: it's like buying a mansion (illiquid) mostly with a loan you must renew every night — one night the lender says no, and you must dump the house at any price.

- A is wrong: this is the SAFE setup, the opposite of what caused the disasters.
- C is wrong: holding lots of cash and government bonds is conservative, not the cause of these failures.
- D is wrong: over-diversification was not the problem; dangerous concentration and leverage were.

📌 Exam tip: The recurring death spiral = leverage + illiquidity + short-term funding. Losses turn into failure when the short-term funding runs.
