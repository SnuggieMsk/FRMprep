**Q1.** Heteroskedasticity in OLS regression errors causes:
- A. Biased coefficients and invalid standard errors.
- B. Unbiased coefficients but invalid standard errors.
- C. Biased coefficients but valid standard errors.
- D. No consequences in large samples.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what heteroskedasticity (error variance that changes with $$X$$) does to a regression.

The key facts:
- OLS coefficients stay UNBIASED and consistent — the point estimates are still on target.
- The usual textbook standard errors assume constant error variance, so they become WRONG (usually understated), which makes t-tests and F-tests unreliable.

So coefficients survive but inference does not. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: heteroskedasticity does not bias the coefficients — only the standard errors are affected.
- C: the standard errors are the part that breaks; they are not valid.
- D: it does have consequences — the inference (t/F tests) is misleading regardless of sample size, unless you use robust standard errors.

Analogy: a scale that is correct on average but jittery for heavy items. The readings are right on average (unbiased coefficients), but the stated "± precision" (standard error) is wrong.

📌 Exam tip: Heteroskedasticity → unbiased coefficients, broken SEs. Fix with White (robust) standard errors. Keep this straight from omitted-variable bias, which biases the coefficients.

**Q2.** A regression shows $$R^2 = 0.85$$, a highly significant overall F-statistic, but no individual t-statistic is significant. The most likely cause is:
- A. Heteroskedasticity.
- B. Omitted variable bias.
- C. Multicollinearity.
- D. Too large a sample size.

**Answer: C**

The question describes a tell-tale pattern: the regressors explain a lot jointly (high $$R^2$$, significant F) but no single one is individually significant.

This is the classic signature of multicollinearity. When regressors are highly correlated with each other, OLS cannot cleanly attribute the effect to any one of them, so each individual standard error inflates and each t-stat shrinks toward insignificance. Yet collectively they still explain $$Y$$, so $$R^2$$ and the F-stat remain strong. The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: heteroskedasticity distorts standard errors generally but does not specifically produce "F-significant, all-t-insignificant."
- B: omitted variable bias distorts the size of coefficients but does not create this joint-vs-individual contradiction.
- D: a large sample tends to make t-stats LARGER, increasing significance — the opposite of the symptom described.

Analogy: a band sounds great together (significant F) but you cannot pick out which musician is carrying the tune (no individual significance) because they are all playing nearly the same notes (correlated regressors).

📌 Exam tip: Significant F + all t's insignificant = multicollinearity. Detect with VIF; the group matters but individuals can't be separated.

**Q3.** A relevant regressor is omitted from a model. It is positively correlated with $$Y$$ AND positively correlated with the included regressor $$X_1$$. The effect on $$\hat\beta_1$$ is to:
- A. Bias it upward.
- B. Bias it downward.
- C. Leave it unbiased but inefficient.
- D. Affect only the intercept.

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the DIRECTION of omitted-variable bias.

The rule for the sign of the bias:
$$\text{bias direction} = (\text{sign of omitted variable's effect on } Y) \times (\text{sign of its correlation with } X_1)$$

Here both signs are positive:
$$(+) \times (+) = (+)$$

So the bias is positive — $$\hat\beta_1$$ is biased UPWARD. The included regressor "steals credit" for the omitted variable's positive effect. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B: a downward bias would require the two signs to multiply to negative (one positive, one negative).
- C: omitting a RELEVANT variable that is correlated with $$X_1$$ biases the coefficient — it is not merely inefficient. (Inefficiency-only happens when you INCLUDE an irrelevant variable.)
- D: the bias hits the slope $$\hat\beta_1$$, not just the intercept.

Analogy: if you forget to account for "hours studied" (which boosts grades and is correlated with "hours of tutoring"), then tutoring will look more powerful than it really is — it grabs the missing variable's credit. Upward bias.

📌 Exam tip: OV bias sign = (effect of omitted on Y) × (corr of omitted with included X). Same signs → upward; opposite signs → downward.

**Q4.** In the auxiliary regression for diagnosing multicollinearity, regressing $$X_j$$ on the other regressors gives $$R_j^2 = 0.80$$. The variance inflation factor (VIF) for $$X_j$$ is:
- A. 1.25
- B. 4
- C. 5
- D. 10

**Answer: C**

The question asks you to compute the VIF, which measures how much collinearity inflates a coefficient's variance.

Step 1 — The formula:
$$VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

Step 2 — Plug in $$R_j^2 = 0.80$$:
$$VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - 0.80} = \frac{1}{0.20} = 5$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (1.25): this is $$1/0.80$$ — dividing by $$R_j^2$$ instead of $$1 - R_j^2$$.
- B (4): comes from a wrong denominator like $$1 - 0.75$$ or arithmetic slip.
- D (10): this is the rule-of-thumb THRESHOLD for "trouble" ($$VIF > 10$$, i.e., $$R_j^2 > 0.9$$), not the value for $$R_j^2 = 0.80$$.

Analogy: VIF tells you how many times bigger the coefficient's variance is, compared with a world where regressors don't overlap. Here it is 5 times bigger — noticeable but below the "alarm" level of 10.

📌 Exam tip: $$VIF = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$$. Rule of thumb: VIF > 10 (i.e., $$R_j^2 > 0.9$$) signals serious multicollinearity.

**Q5.** A White test for heteroskedasticity regresses squared residuals on the regressors, their squares, and cross-products, yielding $$R^2_{\text{aux}} = 0.12$$ with $$n = 100$$ and $$m = 5$$ auxiliary slope regressors. The test statistic and decision are (critical $$\chi^2_5$$ at 5% = 11.07):
- A. 12.0; reject homoskedasticity — use robust standard errors.
- B. 12.0; fail to reject homoskedasticity.
- C. 0.12; fail to reject homoskedasticity.
- D. 5.00; reject homoskedasticity.

**Answer: A**

The question asks you to run the White test: compute the statistic and decide whether the errors are heteroskedastic.

Step 1 — The White test statistic:
$$nR^2_{\text{aux}}$$

Step 2 — Plug in:
$$100 \times 0.12 = 12.0$$

Step 3 — Compare to the chi-square critical value with $$m = 5$$ degrees of freedom, which is 11.07:
$$12.0 > 11.07$$

Since the statistic exceeds the critical value, reject the null of homoskedasticity. The errors are heteroskedastic — switch to White robust standard errors before trusting any t-test. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B: the statistic 12.0 is correct, but exceeding 11.07 means REJECT, not fail to reject. Decision backwards.
- C (0.12): this forgets to multiply by $$n$$ — it is just $$R^2_{\text{aux}}$$.
- D (5.00): this confuses $$m$$ (the degrees of freedom) with the statistic.

Analogy: the White test asks "do the regressors help predict the SIZE of the squared errors?" If they do (statistic above the cutoff), the error spread is not constant — that is heteroskedasticity.

📌 Exam tip: White test statistic = $$n \times R^2_{\text{aux}}$$, compared to $$\chi^2_m$$. Reject for large values → heteroskedasticity present.

**Q6.** Including an IRRELEVANT explanatory variable in a regression:
- A. Biases the other coefficients.
- B. Leaves coefficients unbiased but increases estimator variance.
- C. Makes OLS inconsistent.
- D. Always lowers $$R^2$$.

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the cost of adding a variable that does not belong (the opposite mistake from omitting a relevant one).

Including an irrelevant variable does NOT bias the coefficients — OLS still estimates the true effects correctly on average. But it wastes a degree of freedom and may introduce some collinearity, so the estimator's variance rises and estimates become less precise. It is a pure EFFICIENCY cost, not a bias. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: an irrelevant variable does not bias the other coefficients (unlike OMITTING a relevant one, which does bias).
- C: OLS remains consistent; precision falls but the estimates still converge to the truth.
- D: adding ANY variable can only keep $$R^2$$ the same or raise it — it never lowers plain $$R^2$$. (Adjusted $$R^2$$ might fall, but plain $$R^2$$ does not.)

Analogy: bringing an extra, useless tool to a job doesn't make you do the job WRONG (no bias), but it clutters your bench and makes you a bit less efficient (higher variance).

📌 Exam tip: Irrelevant variable → unbiased but less efficient (higher variance). Omitted relevant variable → biased. Opposite errors, opposite consequences.

**Q7.** Which statement correctly distinguishes the consequences of common regression problems?
- A. Both heteroskedasticity and omitted variable bias bias the coefficients.
- B. Heteroskedasticity and multicollinearity leave coefficients unbiased; omitted variable bias does not.
- C. Multicollinearity biases the coefficients, but heteroskedasticity does not.
- D. All three problems make OLS inconsistent.

**Answer: B**

The question tests the most important distinction in this chapter: which problems bias the coefficients and which only damage precision/standard errors.

The facts:
- Heteroskedasticity → coefficients UNBIASED; only standard errors corrupted.
- Multicollinearity → coefficients UNBIASED; standard errors inflated (imprecise).
- Omitted variable bias → coefficients actually BIASED and inconsistent.

So the correct grouping is B: the first two leave coefficients unbiased; omitted variable bias is the one that biases them.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: heteroskedasticity does NOT bias coefficients, so this is wrong.
- C: multicollinearity does NOT bias coefficients (it inflates variance); the statement is backwards.
- D: only omitted variable bias causes inconsistency; heteroskedasticity and multicollinearity leave OLS consistent.

Analogy: think of three problems with a camera. Two of them (heteroskedasticity, multicollinearity) make the picture BLURRY but still centered on the subject (unbiased, imprecise). One (omitted variable bias) actually points the camera at the WRONG subject (biased).

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the split — heteroskedasticity & multicollinearity = unbiased but imprecise; omitted-variable bias = the deadly one that biases coefficients.

**Q8.** The bias–variance trade-off in model selection implies that a model with too FEW variables tends to suffer from:
- A. High variance (overfitting).
- B. Bias (underfitting).
- C. Perfect multicollinearity.
- D. Heteroskedasticity.

**Answer: B**

The question asks which side of the trade-off a too-small model lands on.

The trade-off:
- Too FEW variables → the model misses real relationships → it is BIASED (underfitting).
- Too MANY variables → the model chases in-sample noise → high VARIANCE (overfitting), poor out-of-sample prediction.

A model with too few variables underfits, so it suffers from bias. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: high variance / overfitting is the problem of too MANY variables, not too few.
- C: perfect multicollinearity is a separate computational issue (exact linear dependence), not the consequence of using too few variables.
- D: heteroskedasticity is about error variance depending on $$X$$ — unrelated to the number of variables.

Analogy: too few variables is like describing a person with just "tall" — you miss important details (bias). Too many variables is like memorizing every freckle — you over-describe THIS person and fail to recognize others (overfitting/variance).

📌 Exam tip: Too few → bias (underfit); too many → variance (overfit). Cross-validation targets out-of-sample prediction to balance the two.

**Q9.** Which model-selection procedure repeatedly fits on part of the data and evaluates fit on a held-out block, directly targeting out-of-sample prediction error?
- A. General-to-specific elimination.
- B. m-fold cross-validation.
- C. The White test.
- D. The dummy variable trap.

**Answer: B**

The question describes a specific procedure: splitting data into blocks, training on most, testing on the rest, and judging by out-of-sample performance.

That is exactly m-fold cross-validation: split the data into $$m$$ blocks; repeatedly fit on $$m - 1$$ blocks and evaluate on the held-out block; choose the model with the best average OUT-OF-SAMPLE fit. It directly measures prediction error rather than in-sample fit. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: general-to-specific starts with all plausible variables and drops the least significant one at a time until all remaining are significant — it is an in-sample t-stat procedure, not an out-of-sample one.
- C: the White test is a heteroskedasticity diagnostic, not a model-selection method.
- D: the dummy variable trap is a multicollinearity error, not a selection procedure.

Analogy: cross-validation is like studying with practice exams you haven't seen the answers to — it tests whether you can GENERALIZE, not just memorize the textbook examples (in-sample fit).

📌 Exam tip: Cross-validation = out-of-sample evaluation by holding out blocks. General-to-specific = drop the least significant variable iteratively (in-sample). BIC penalizes parameters harder than AIC, favoring smaller models.

**Q10.** A regressor $$X_3$$ is regressed on the other four regressors, giving $$R_3^2 = 0.95$$. Approximately how many times larger is the standard error of $$\hat\beta_3$$ compared with the case of orthogonal (uncorrelated) regressors?
- A. About 20 times.
- B. About 4.5 times.
- C. About 0.95 times.
- D. About 10 times.

**Answer: B**

The question links the VIF (which inflates the VARIANCE) to the standard error (which inflates by the SQUARE ROOT of the VIF).

Step 1 — Compute the VIF, which is the variance inflation factor:
$$VIF_3 = \frac{1}{1 - R_3^2} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.95} = \frac{1}{0.05} = 20$$

So the VARIANCE of $$\hat\beta_3$$ is 20 times what it would be with uncorrelated regressors.

Step 2 — The standard error is the square root of the variance, so the standard error inflates by the square root of the VIF:
$$\sqrt{20} \approx 4.47 \approx 4.5$$

The standard error is about 4.5 times larger. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (about 20): that is the inflation of the VARIANCE, not the standard error. The SE is the square root of that.
- C (0.95): this is $$R_3^2$$ itself, not any inflation factor.
- D (about 10): 10 is the VIF threshold for concern, and would correspond to $$R_3^2 = 0.9$$, not 0.95.

Analogy: if an area grows 20-fold, the side length only grows about 4.5-fold (square root of 20). Variance is the "area"; standard error is the "side."

📌 Exam tip: VIF inflates the VARIANCE; the standard error inflates by $$\sqrt{VIF}$$. Here VIF = 20, SE blows up by $$\sqrt{20} \approx 4.5$$.
