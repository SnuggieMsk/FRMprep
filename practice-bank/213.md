**Q1.** A Monte Carlo simulation of a portfolio's annual P&L uses 10,000 trials and produces an across-trial standard deviation of \$20M. The standard error of the mean estimate is closest to:
- A. \$0.20M
- B. \$2.00M
- C. \$0.002M
- D. \$20M

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the Monte Carlo standard error of the estimated mean.

The standard error formula is:
$$SE = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}$$

where $$s$$ is the across-trial standard deviation and $$N$$ is the number of trials. Plug in $$s = \$20\text{M}$$ and $$N = 10{,}000$$:
$$\sqrt{N} = \sqrt{10{,}000} = 100$$
$$SE = \frac{20}{100} = \$0.20\text{M}$$

So the answer is A.

Why the others are wrong:
- B. \$2.00M would come from dividing by $$\sqrt{100} = 10$$ instead of by 100 — using the wrong $$N$$.
- C. \$0.002M divides by 10,000 (i.e., by $$N$$, not $$\sqrt{N}$$).
- D. \$20M is the raw across-trial standard deviation, not divided by $$\sqrt{N}$$ at all.

Think of averaging many noisy measurements: the more trials you average, the tighter your estimate, and the tightening goes with $$\sqrt{N}$$.

📌 Exam tip: Monte Carlo $$SE = s/\sqrt{N}$$. Divide by the SQUARE ROOT of the trial count, not the count itself.

**Q2.** A Monte Carlo estimate currently has a standard error of \$40,000 from 2,500 trials. To cut the standard error to \$10,000, how many trials are needed?
- A. 10,000
- B. 40,000
- C. 5,000
- D. 250,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks how many trials are needed to shrink the standard error by a factor.

Standard error falls as $$1/\sqrt{N}$$. We want the SE to drop from \$40,000 to \$10,000 — that is a reduction by a factor of:
$$\frac{40{,}000}{10{,}000} = 4$$

Because SE scales with $$1/\sqrt{N}$$, cutting SE by a factor of 4 requires multiplying $$N$$ by $$4^2 = 16$$:
$$N_{\text{new}} = 16 \times 2{,}500 = 40{,}000$$

So the answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. 10,000 is only $$4\times$$ the original trials — that would cut SE by $$\sqrt{4} = 2$$, giving \$20,000, not \$10,000.
- C. 5,000 is just double the trials — far too few.
- D. 250,000 is 100× the trials, which would cut SE by 10× (to \$4,000), overshooting the target.

The rule of thumb: to make your estimate $$k$$ times more precise, you need $$k^2$$ times as many trials. Precision is expensive.

📌 Exam tip: SE $$\propto 1/\sqrt{N}$$. To reduce SE by factor $$k$$, multiply trials by $$k^2$$. (10× precision = 100× trials.)

**Q3.** Antithetic variates reduce Monte Carlo variance because the paired estimates are:
- A. Independent of each other.
- B. Positively correlated.
- C. Negatively correlated.
- D. Always exactly equal.

**Answer: C**

The question asks why pairing each random draw with its "mirror" reduces simulation variance.

In antithetic sampling, each draw $$u$$ is paired with $$1-u$$ (or $$z$$ with $$-z$$ for normals). The two resulting estimates tend to move in opposite directions — they are negatively correlated. The variance of their average is:
$$\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{\hat\theta_1 + \hat\theta_2}{2}\right) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1 + \rho)$$

When $$\rho < 0$$, the factor $$(1+\rho)$$ is below 1, so the variance falls below independent sampling. The answer is C.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. If the pairs were merely independent ($$\rho = 0$$), you'd get the ordinary variance $$\sigma^2/2$$ — no extra benefit. The benefit comes from negative correlation.
- B. Positive correlation ($$\rho > 0$$) would make $$(1+\rho) > 1$$ and INCREASE the variance — the opposite of what we want.
- D. If they were always equal, you'd gain no new information at all.

Imagine two friends estimating a height, one always guessing a bit high when the other guesses a bit low: averaging their offsetting errors gives a tighter answer.

📌 Exam tip: Antithetic variates work via NEGATIVE correlation; variance of the average $$= \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1+\rho)$$ falls when $$\rho < 0$$. Bonus: you reuse the random numbers, halving generation cost.

**Q4.** An estimator has a per-draw variance of $$\sigma^2 = 500$$. Using antithetic pairs with correlation $$\rho = -0.4$$, the variance of the average of an antithetic pair is:
- A. 250
- B. 150
- C. 350
- D. 500

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the variance after applying antithetic variates with a given negative correlation.

Use the formula:
$$\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{\hat\theta_1 + \hat\theta_2}{2}\right) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1 + \rho)$$

Plug in $$\sigma^2 = 500$$ and $$\rho = -0.4$$:
$$= \frac{500}{2}(1 + (-0.4)) = 250 \times (0.6) = 150$$

So the answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. 250 is $$\sigma^2/2$$, the variance you'd get from two INDEPENDENT draws ($$\rho = 0$$). It ignores the negative correlation benefit.
- C. 350 would come from using $$\rho = +0.4$$ ($$250 \times 1.4 = 350$$) — wrong sign, giving a (wrong) increase.
- D. 500 is the original per-draw variance, with no averaging applied.

The negative correlation shaves the variance from 250 down to 150 — a 40% reduction beyond plain averaging, at no extra simulation cost.

📌 Exam tip: Apply $$\frac{\sigma^2}{2}(1+\rho)$$ carefully and keep the sign of $$\rho$$. Negative $$\rho$$ shrinks; positive $$\rho$$ inflates.

**Q5.** What is the primary advantage of bootstrapping over a standard Monte Carlo simulation?
- A. It always converges to the true value faster.
- B. It requires no assumption about the underlying return distribution.
- C. It can generate losses worse than any historically observed loss.
- D. It completely eliminates sampling error.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the key benefit of bootstrapping versus Monte Carlo.

Monte Carlo draws from an assumed model (e.g., a normal distribution), which carries model risk if that assumption is wrong. Bootstrapping instead resamples directly from the observed data with replacement — so it makes no distributional assumption. The data "speak for themselves," automatically capturing real fat tails and skew. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. Bootstrapping does not inherently converge faster; both methods improve at the $$\sqrt{N}$$ rate.
- C. This is exactly what bootstrapping CANNOT do — it can never produce a loss worse than the worst observation in the sample. This is one of its weaknesses, not an advantage.
- D. No simulation-based method eliminates sampling error; results from a finite number of resamples always carry some error.

Think of bootstrapping as drawing slips from a hat filled with the actual historical returns, rather than inventing returns from a textbook bell curve that might be wrong.

📌 Exam tip: Bootstrap's strength = no distributional assumption (avoids model misspecification). Its weakness = cannot extrapolate beyond observed extremes.

**Q6.** Fixing the seed of a pseudo-random number generator before a simulation:
- A. Makes the draws truly (physically) random.
- B. Reproduces the exact same sequence of draws on each run.
- C. Eliminates Monte Carlo sampling error.
- D. Is mathematically required for the law of large numbers to hold.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what setting a seed actually does.

Pseudo-random generators are deterministic algorithms; the seed sets their starting state. The same seed always produces the identical sequence of "random" numbers. This gives reproducibility — useful for auditing and debugging — and lets two competing models be tested on exactly the same shocks for a fair comparison. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. Seeding does the opposite of making draws truly random — the sequence is fully deterministic given the seed. The numbers only LOOK random.
- C. The seed does not reduce sampling error; running with a different seed just gives a different (but equally noisy) sample. Error still shrinks only with more trials.
- D. The law of large numbers does not require a fixed seed; it holds as the number of draws grows, regardless of the seed.

Think of a seed like a recipe's starting instructions: follow the same recipe and you get the same dish every time — reproducible, but no longer a surprise.

📌 Exam tip: Seed = reproducibility and fair model comparison on identical shocks. It does NOT reduce error or make draws "truly" random.

**Q7.** Which situation would make the iid (single-observation) bootstrap inappropriate, requiring a block bootstrap instead?
- A. The data are independent across time.
- B. The data exhibit volatility clustering and autocorrelation.
- C. The sample is very large.
- D. The statistic of interest is the sample mean.

**Answer: B**

The question asks when the simple iid bootstrap breaks down.

The iid bootstrap draws single observations one at a time, which scrambles their time order. If the data have time-series dependence — like autocorrelation or volatility clustering (calm periods followed by calm, turbulent followed by turbulent) — this scrambling destroys that structure. To preserve dependence, you draw consecutive blocks of observations (the circular block bootstrap). The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. If data are genuinely independent across time, the iid bootstrap is perfectly fine — there is no dependence to preserve.
- C. A large sample is helpful for the bootstrap, not a problem. Size is not the issue here.
- D. Estimating a simple mean does not by itself require a block bootstrap; the issue is whether the data are dependent, not which statistic you compute.

Think of resampling a melody: pulling random single notes (iid) ruins the tune; pulling short phrases (blocks) keeps the rhythm and structure intact.

📌 Exam tip: Dependent/time-series data (autocorrelation, vol clustering) → use BLOCK bootstrap. iid bootstrap is only valid for independent data.

**Q8.** A risk analyst uses a 500-day return history and a bootstrap to estimate the 99% 1-day VaR. A key limitation of this approach is that the bootstrap:
- A. Assumes returns are normally distributed.
- B. Cannot produce a loss larger than the worst loss in the 500-day window.
- C. Requires at least 1 million resamples to be valid.
- D. Always overstates tail risk.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a limitation of using the bootstrap to estimate an extreme risk quantile.

The bootstrap resamples from the observed data only. Since it can only reuse values that actually occurred, no resample can ever generate a loss worse than the single worst day in the 500-day window. For extreme-quantile risk (like 99% VaR), this is a real weakness: if the future holds a loss larger than anything seen historically, the bootstrap will miss it. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. The bootstrap makes NO distributional assumption — that is its main advantage, the opposite of assuming normality.
- C. There is no fixed minimum like 1 million resamples; a few thousand resamples are typically plenty.
- D. The bootstrap does not "always overstate" tail risk; if anything, it tends to UNDERSTATE extreme tails because it cannot exceed the worst observed loss.

Think of the bootstrap as a photo album: you can only re-arrange pictures you already have — you can never conjure a scene that was never photographed.

📌 Exam tip: The bootstrap can't extrapolate beyond observed extremes — a serious problem for far-tail risk like 99%+ VaR. For unseen extremes, consider parametric or extreme-value methods.

**Q9.** Which of the following best describes the control variates technique?
- A. Pairing each random draw with its mirror image to induce negative correlation.
- B. Simulating a related quantity whose true expected value is known, and using its simulation error to correct the target estimate.
- C. Resampling observed data with replacement.
- D. Increasing the number of trials until the standard error is acceptable.

**Answer: B**

The question asks to identify the control variates method among variance-reduction techniques.

A control variate is a related quantity $$X$$ whose true expected value $$E[X]$$ is known analytically. You simulate it alongside the target, then adjust:
$$\hat\theta_{CV} = \hat\theta + \beta\,(E[X] - \hat{X})$$

Because $$\hat X$$ and the target share the same random noise, the known error in $$\hat X$$ (the gap $$E[X] - \hat X$$) is used to correct the error in the target estimate. It works best when target and control are highly correlated. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. Pairing a draw with its mirror image describes antithetic variates, a different technique.
- C. Resampling observed data with replacement is bootstrapping, not control variates.
- D. Simply adding more trials is brute-force precision improvement, not a variance-reduction technique in the clever sense.

Classic example: to price an arithmetic Asian option, simulate the geometric Asian option (which has a closed-form true price) as the control, and use its known error to sharpen the arithmetic estimate.

📌 Exam tip: Control variate needs a KNOWN $$E[X]$$ and high correlation with the target. Antithetic = mirrored draws; control variate = correcting using a known-answer companion.

**Q10.** Which of the following is a genuine disadvantage of the simulation approach to financial problem solving (compared with an analytic solution)?
- A. Simulation gives more general insight than a closed-form formula.
- B. Simulation results carry sampling error and improve only slowly (at the $$\sqrt{N}$$ rate).
- C. Simulation never depends on any model assumptions.
- D. Simulation always runs faster than computing a formula.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a true drawback of simulation.

Simulation answers are estimates from a finite number of trials, so they always carry sampling error, and that error shrinks only at the slow $$\sqrt{N}$$ rate — meaning big gains in precision require huge increases in trials. The answer is B.

Why the others are wrong:
- A. This is backwards: a closed-form analytic solution gives MORE general insight (it shows how the answer depends on each input). A simulation result is specific to the chosen parameters.
- C. Monte Carlo simulation depends heavily on the assumed data-generating process — model risk is a key weakness, not something it avoids.
- D. Simulation is usually SLOWER and more computationally costly than evaluating a formula, especially for path-dependent or nested problems.

Think of simulation as estimating a room's area by tossing thousands of darts: it works, but it's slow, noisy, and gives less insight than just measuring length × width with a formula.

📌 Exam tip: Prefer analytic solutions where they exist. Simulation's downsides: sampling error (slow $$\sqrt{N}$$ convergence), model risk, computational cost, and limited general insight.
