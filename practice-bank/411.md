**Q1.** A 2-year bond with a 6% semiannual coupon and \$100 face value is priced to yield 5% (YTM). The per-period (semiannual) discount rate is 2.5% and there are 4 periods. Given $$(1.025)^{-4} = 0.905951$$, the price is closest to:
- A. \$98.12
- B. \$100.00
- C. \$101.88
- D. \$103.76

**Answer: C**

The question asks: what is the bond's price when its coupon rate (6%) is higher than its yield (5%)?

Step 1 — set up the pieces. Coupon per period = $$6\% \times 100 / 2 = \$3$$. There are 4 periods. The per-period yield = $$5\% / 2 = 2.5\%$$.

Step 2 — value the coupons (an annuity). The annuity factor is:
$$\frac{1 - (1.025)^{-4}}{0.025} = \frac{1 - 0.905951}{0.025} = \frac{0.094049}{0.025} = 3.76197$$
PV of coupons = $$3 \times 3.76197 = 11.286$$.

Step 3 — value the face amount returned at the end:
$$100 \times 0.905951 = 90.595$$.

Step 4 — add them: $$11.286 + 90.595 = \$101.88$$.

This is a premium (above \$100), which is exactly what we expect because the coupon rate (6%) beats the yield (5%) — the bond pays more than the market demands, so buyers pay extra for it.

- A (\$98.12) is a discount price — that is what you would get if the coupon were below the yield. Trap: it confuses premium and discount.
- B (\$100.00) is par, which only happens when coupon = yield. Here they differ.
- D (\$103.76) over-counts — likely double-counting coupons or mis-keying the calculator.

Analogy: a bond paying a 6% "interest rate" in a 5% world is like a savings account paying more than the bank next door — people will pay a premium to own it.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the quick logic — coupon > yield → premium (price > 100); coupon = yield → par; coupon < yield → discount. You can often eliminate two answers before calculating.

**Q2.** A bond is currently trading at a discount (below par). This tells you that:
- A. The coupon rate is greater than the YTM
- B. The coupon rate is equal to the YTM
- C. The coupon rate is less than the YTM
- D. The bond must be callable

**Answer: C**

The question asks: what does a below-par price reveal about the relationship between coupon and yield?

A discount price (below \$100) happens when the bond's coupon is too small compared with what the market demands (the YTM). To make a low-coupon bond competitive, its price has to fall so the buyer earns the market yield through the discount.

- A is the premium case (coupon > yield gives price > 100) — the opposite.
- B is the par case (coupon = yield gives price = 100) — not a discount.
- D is irrelevant; a discount tells you nothing about call features. Callability is a separate trap.

Analogy: if a bond pays only 3% coupons but everyone else is paying 5%, nobody buys it at full price — the seller must mark it down (discount) to attract buyers.

📌 Exam tip: "Discount = coupon below yield" is a one-second answer. Don't overthink it by looking for hidden features like calls or puts.

**Q3.** An investor buys a coupon bond and holds it to maturity. Her realized compound return will be HIGHER than the purchase YTM if:
- A. Coupons are reinvested at a rate below the YTM
- B. Coupons are reinvested at a rate above the YTM
- C. The bond was purchased at par
- D. Never — the YTM is the maximum possible return

**Answer: B**

The question asks: under what reinvestment condition does the actual return beat the quoted yield?

YTM secretly assumes every coupon you receive is reinvested at the YTM until maturity. If the actual reinvestment rate is higher than the YTM, those coupons grow faster than assumed, and your realized return ends up above the YTM.

- A is the opposite: lower reinvestment rates drag the realized return below YTM.
- C is irrelevant; buying at par does not lock in YTM as the realized return — reinvestment still matters.
- D is false; YTM is just a quoting convention, not a ceiling on returns.

Analogy: YTM is like a recipe that assumes you can keep buying ingredients at today's price. If prices (reinvestment rates) turn out higher, your "yield" from baking and reselling is better than the recipe promised.

📌 Exam tip: YTM = realized return only if coupons are reinvested AT the YTM and the bond is held to maturity. Higher reinvestment rates beat it; lower rates fall short. Zero-coupon bonds are the exception (no coupons to reinvest).

**Q4.** A perpetuity pays \$6 per year forever. Comparable annual yields are 8%. The value of the perpetuity is closest to:
- A. \$48
- B. \$75
- C. \$133
- D. \$60

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what is the present value of a level payment that goes on forever?

The perpetuity formula is:
$$P = \frac{C}{y} = \frac{6}{0.08} = \$75$$

That is the entire calculation — divide the annual cash flow by the yield.

- A (\$48) multiplies $$6 \times 8 = 48$$ — wrong operation (multiplied instead of divided).
- C (\$133) divides by a much smaller rate or inverts something; it does not match $$6/0.08$$.
- D (\$60) divides by 10% instead of 8% — a rate mix-up.

Analogy: if you want \$6 a year from a bank account paying 8%, you need \$75 in the account, because $$75 \times 8\% = 6$$. The perpetuity price is just "how much principal produces this income."

📌 Exam tip: Perpetuity price = payment ÷ yield. Lower yields make perpetuities worth far more (extreme duration), which is why a drop from 8% to 6% raises this from \$75 to \$100.

**Q5.** An investor buys a 6% semiannual-coupon bond at \$100.00, holds it for one year, and sells it for \$100.50. The first \$3 coupon (received at 6 months) is reinvested for the remaining 6 months at 1% per half-year. The gross realized return over the year is closest to:
- A. 6.00%
- B. 6.53%
- C. 6.03%
- D. 0.53%

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what total return did the investor actually earn, including price change and reinvested coupons?

Step 1 — grow the first coupon. The \$3 coupon sits for 6 months earning 1%:
$$3 \times 1.01 = 3.03$$.

Step 2 — add the second coupon received at year-end (not reinvested, just collected): \$3.00.
Total coupon proceeds at year-end = $$3.03 + 3.00 = 6.03$$.

Step 3 — apply the realized return formula:
$$R = \frac{P_{end} + \text{coupons} - P_{start}}{P_{start}} = \frac{100.50 + 6.03 - 100}{100} = \frac{6.53}{100} = 6.53\%$$

- A (6.00%) is just the coupon rate, ignoring the \$0.50 price gain and the \$0.03 of reinvestment income.
- C (6.03%) counts only the coupons (\$6.03) and forgets the \$0.50 price appreciation.
- D (0.53%) counts only the price gain (\$0.50) plus reinvestment but forgets the coupons entirely.

Analogy: your total return is like a full grocery receipt — you must add up the price change AND every coupon AND the little interest the coupons earned, then compare to what you paid.

📌 Exam tip: Realized return = (ending price + all cash received + reinvestment income − starting price) ÷ starting price. Don't drop any piece.

**Q6.** A zero-coupon bond is purchased to yield 5% and held to maturity. Its realized compound return will be:
- A. Above 5%, because there are no coupons to reinvest
- B. Below 5%, because reinvestment risk hurts it
- C. Exactly 5%, regardless of how interest rates move
- D. Impossible to determine without knowing the reinvestment rate

**Answer: C**

The question asks: how does a zero-coupon bond's realized return compare to its YTM?

A zero-coupon bond pays no coupons — there is nothing to reinvest. The reinvestment assumption buried inside YTM simply doesn't apply. So if you buy at a 5% yield and hold to maturity, you are guaranteed to earn exactly 5% compounded.

- A is wrong: with no coupons, there is no way to beat the locked-in yield.
- B is wrong: there is no reinvestment risk precisely because there are no coupons.
- D is wrong: no reinvestment rate is needed — that is the whole point.

Analogy: a zero-coupon bond is like buying a sealed gift card today for \$78 that turns into \$100 on a fixed date. The return is fixed the moment you buy; nothing in between changes it.

📌 Exam tip: Zeros are the one case where YTM = realized return (held to maturity), guaranteed. This is a favorite "exception" question.

**Q7.** A bond's profit-and-loss over a period can be broken into carry (coupon income), rolldown, curve changes, and spread changes. If the yield curve is assumed unchanged over the period, the realized return comes only from:
- A. Curve change plus spread change
- B. Carry plus rolldown
- C. Rolldown only
- D. Spread change only

**Answer: B**

The question asks: if rates and the curve don't move, which P&L pieces survive?

Two components do not require any change in rates:
- Carry = the coupon income you simply collect for holding the bond.
- Rolldown = the price change from the bond "sliding" to a shorter maturity along a curve that is the same shape (a 10-year becoming a 9-year, etc.).

If the curve is unchanged, the curve-change and spread-change components are zero, leaving only carry + rolldown.

- A is exactly the part that disappears when the curve is unchanged.
- C drops carry, the largest piece — you still collect coupons.
- D keeps only the piece that is zero by assumption.

Analogy: even on a day when prices don't move at all, you still earn your "rent" (coupon = carry) and you still age one day closer to maturity (rolldown). Those happen no matter what.

📌 Exam tip: "Curve unchanged" → return = carry + rolldown. This phrasing is a classic GARP cue.

**Q8.** A 10-year annuity pays \$4 at the end of each year. The appropriate annual discount rate is 5%. Given the 10-year, 5% annuity factor is 7.7217, the present value is closest to:
- A. \$40.00
- B. \$30.89
- C. \$80.00
- D. \$77.22

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what is today's value of \$4 received each year for 10 years at 5%?

An annuity's present value is the payment times the annuity factor:
$$PV = C \times \frac{1 - (1+y)^{-n}}{y} = 4 \times 7.7217 = \$30.89$$

- A (\$40.00) just adds up the 10 payments ($$4 \times 10$$) with no discounting — ignores the time value of money.
- C (\$80.00) treats it like a perpetuity ($$4 / 0.05$$) — but the payments stop after 10 years, so it must be worth less.
- D (\$77.22) multiplies the annuity factor by 10 instead of by the \$4 payment.

Analogy: \$4 a year for 10 years is worth less than \$40 total, because dollars arriving later are worth less today — like a promise to pay you allowance for 10 years is worth less than handing you all 10 years at once.

📌 Exam tip: Annuity PV = payment × annuity factor. Note an annuity is always worth LESS than the matching perpetuity (\$30.89 < \$80) because the payments eventually stop.

**Q9.** Two option-free bonds have the same maturity but different coupon rates. When the yield curve is upward-sloping (not flat), their yields to maturity will:
- A. Be identical, since maturity is the only thing that matters
- B. Differ, because YTM is a cash-flow-weighted average of spot rates and the bonds weight those spots differently
- C. Be identical only if both trade at par
- D. Differ only if one bond is callable

**Answer: B**

The question asks: do same-maturity bonds always share the same YTM?

YTM is a kind of "complex average" of the spot rates that apply to each of the bond's cash flows. A high-coupon bond puts more weight on earlier cash flows (which are discounted at shorter-maturity spot rates), while a low-coupon bond puts relatively more weight on the final payment (longer-maturity spot rate). When the curve is not flat, these different weightings produce different YTMs even at the same maturity.

- A is the classic trap: maturity alone does NOT pin down YTM unless the curve is flat.
- C is false; trading at par does not force two different bonds to share a yield.
- D is irrelevant — the effect happens for plain option-free bonds.

Analogy: YTM is like the average "speed" of a trip made of road segments at different speed limits. Two cars covering the same total distance but spending different amounts of time on each segment end up with different average speeds.

📌 Exam tip: YTM is bond-specific. Same maturity ≠ same yield unless the spot curve is flat. This is why YTM is called a "complex average" of spot rates.

**Q10.** A bond pays semiannual coupons and is quoted with a YTM of 5% (a bond-equivalent yield). The effective annual yield is closest to:
- A. 5.00%
- B. 5.06%
- C. 2.50%
- D. 10.00%

**Answer: B**

The question asks: convert a 5% semiannual (bond-equivalent) yield into an effective annual yield.

A 5% bond-equivalent yield means 2.5% per half-year. Compounding two half-years:
$$(1 + 0.025)^2 - 1 = 1.050625 - 1 = 0.050625 = 5.06\%$$

- A (5.00%) is the quoted bond-equivalent yield itself, before compounding — the trap of treating the quote as already effective.
- C (2.50%) is just the per-period rate (half of 5%), not an annual figure.
- D (10.00%) doubles incorrectly — confusing the quoting convention.

Analogy: getting 2.5% twice a year, where the second half-year earns interest on the first half-year's gain, is slightly better than a flat 5% — the tiny extra 0.06% is "interest on interest."

📌 Exam tip: Effective annual yield = $$(1 + y/2)^2 - 1$$ for a semiannual bond. Mixing bond-equivalent and effective yields is a recurring distractor — read which one the question wants.
