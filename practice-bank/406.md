**Q1.** A bank has a $5,000,000 loan to a company. The probability of default (PD) over one year is 3%, and if the company defaults the bank expects to lose 50% of the exposure (LGD = 50%). What is the expected loss (EL)?
- A. $25,000
- B. $75,000
- C. $150,000
- D. $250,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks for expected loss, which is the average loss the bank expects on this loan over one year.

The formula is:

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$$

Plug in the numbers:
- PD = 0.03
- LGD = 0.50
- EAD (exposure at default) = $5,000,000

Step by step:

$$EL = 0.03 \times 0.50 \times 5{,}000{,}000$$

First, $$0.03 \times 0.50 = 0.015$$.

Then, $$0.015 \times 5{,}000{,}000 = 75{,}000$$.

So EL = $75,000. Think of it like this: there is only a 3% chance things go wrong, and even then you only lose half, so the "average" expected loss is small compared to the full loan.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($25,000): This used PD x EAD but forgot... actually it comes from multiplying wrong; it ignores part of the calculation and is too small.
- C ($150,000): This forgot to multiply by LGD (used PD x EAD = 0.03 x 5M = 150,000). Trap: forgetting LGD.
- D ($250,000): This used LGD x EAD without PD (0.50 x 5M = 2.5M is even bigger; 250,000 mixes up factors). Trap: dropping PD.

📌 Exam tip: EL is always the product of all three: PD x LGD x EAD. If any factor is missing, your answer is wrong.

**Q2.** A $25,000,000 exposure has a probability of default of 1.5% and an expected recovery rate of 55%. The expected loss is closest to:
- A. $84,375
- B. $168,750
- C. $206,250
- D. $375,000

**Answer: B**

The question gives a recovery rate, not LGD directly, so first find LGD.

Recovery rate = 55%, so:

$$LGD = 1 - \text{recovery rate} = 1 - 0.55 = 0.45$$

Now apply the formula:

$$EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD = 0.015 \times 0.45 \times 25{,}000{,}000$$

Step by step:
- $$0.015 \times 0.45 = 0.00675$$
- $$0.00675 \times 25{,}000{,}000 = 168{,}750$$

So EL = $168,750.

Analogy: If 55% of the loan comes back even after default, you only truly lose 45% of it. That 45% is your LGD.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($84,375): Used PD = 0.0075 (halved PD by mistake) or similar slip; too small.
- C ($206,250): Used LGD = 0.55 (the recovery rate) instead of 0.45. This is the classic recovery/LGD flip trap.
- D ($375,000): Forgot to subtract recovery; used LGD = 1.0 effectively, or dropped a factor.

📌 Exam tip: Watch the wording. If they give you a recovery rate, LGD = 1 - recovery rate. Never plug the recovery rate straight into the EL formula.

**Q3.** Which statement best describes the difference between expected loss (EL) and unexpected loss (UL) for a credit portfolio?
- A. EL is covered by capital; UL is covered by loan pricing.
- B. EL is the average (mean) loss, covered by pricing/provisions; UL is the variation around that mean, covered by capital.
- C. EL and UL are the same thing measured in different units.
- D. UL is always smaller than EL.

**Answer: B**

The question is about what EL and UL mean and who pays for each.

EL is the mean (average) loss. Because you can predict it on average, you simply build it into the interest rate you charge and set aside loan-loss provisions. It is a normal cost of doing business.

UL is how much actual losses can swing above the average in a bad year. Because it is uncertain, you hold capital as a cushion to absorb it.

Analogy: A pizza shop knows on average it wastes 2 pizzas a day (EL) and prices that into the menu. But on a freak day it might waste 15 (UL) - it keeps savings (capital) to survive that bad day.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Reversed. Pricing covers EL; capital covers UL.
- C: They are genuinely different concepts (mean vs dispersion), not the same thing in different units.
- D: False. In credit, UL is typically much larger than EL because credit losses are skewed (our notes showed UL was about 7.6x EL in one example).

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the pairing - EL goes with pricing/provisions; UL (and credit VaR) goes with capital.

**Q4.** Economic capital for credit risk is best described as:
- A. Expected loss only
- B. The 99.9% worst-case loss minus expected loss
- C. EAD multiplied by LGD
- D. Expected loss plus unexpected loss combined

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the definition of economic capital.

Economic capital is the buffer a bank holds to survive unexpectedly bad years, measured up to a high confidence level (Basel uses 99.9%) over one year, and only for losses ABOVE the expected loss:

$$\text{Economic capital} = \text{Credit VaR} = \text{WCL}_{99.9\%} - \text{EL}$$

We subtract EL because EL is already paid for through pricing and provisions; capital only needs to cover the surprise part.

Analogy: You already budget for your normal monthly bills (EL). Your emergency fund (capital) only needs to cover the shocking extra costs beyond what you already budgeted.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: EL is provisioned, not held as capital.
- C: EAD x LGD is the loss if default happens with full certainty - it ignores probability and confidence levels entirely.
- D: We do NOT add EL; we subtract it. Capital is for unexpected loss only.

📌 Exam tip: The word "minus EL" is the giveaway. Economic capital = worst-case loss minus expected loss.

**Q5.** In the Vasicek single-factor model, if the asset correlation between borrowers is zero (rho = 0), the 99.9% worst-case default rate (WCDR) equals:
- A. Zero
- B. The probability of default (PD)
- C. 99.9%
- D. N(3.090)

**Answer: B**

The question tests what happens to the worst-case default rate when borrowers are completely uncorrelated.

The WCDR formula is:

$$WCDR = N\!\left(\frac{N^{-1}(PD) + \sqrt{\rho}\,N^{-1}(X)}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)$$

Set rho = 0. Then $$\sqrt{\rho} = 0$$ and $$\sqrt{1-\rho} = 1$$. The formula becomes:

$$WCDR = N\!\left(\frac{N^{-1}(PD) + 0}{1}\right) = N\big(N^{-1}(PD)\big) = PD$$

When borrowers share no common risk, the bad luck in a big diversified portfolio averages out (idiosyncratic risk diversifies away), so the worst-case default rate is just the ordinary PD.

Analogy: If everyone's chance of getting sick is independent, then in a huge crowd the fraction sick on any day is steady and equal to the individual probability - no scary clustering.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (Zero): There is still default risk; it doesn't vanish, it just equals PD.
- C (99.9%): That's the confidence level, not a default rate.
- D (N(3.090)): That's about 99.9%, not the answer.

📌 Exam tip: rho = 0 means full diversification, so WCDR collapses to PD. Higher rho fattens the tail and pushes WCDR above PD.

**Q6.** A $100,000,000 well-diversified loan portfolio has PD = 1%, LGD = 60%, and asset correlation rho = 0.1, at the 99.9% confidence level the WCDR = 7.75%. The credit VaR (economic capital) is closest to:
- A. $4.65 million
- B. $4.05 million
- C. $0.60 million
- D. $0.45 million

**Answer: B**

The question asks for credit VaR given the worst-case default rate.

The formula is:

$$\text{Credit VaR} = EAD \times LGD \times (WCDR - PD)$$

Plug in:
- EAD = $100,000,000
- LGD = 0.60
- WCDR = 0.0775
- PD = 0.01

Step by step:
- $$WCDR - PD = 0.0775 - 0.01 = 0.0675$$
- $$100{,}000{,}000 \times 0.60 = 60{,}000{,}000$$
- $$60{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0675 = 4{,}050{,}000$$

So credit VaR = $4.05 million.

We subtract PD because the expected loss part is already provisioned; capital covers only the excess.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($4.65M): Forgot to subtract PD (used WCDR alone: 100M x 0.6 x 0.0775 = 4.65M). This is the classic "don't subtract PD" trap, which overstates capital.
- C ($0.60M): This is the expected loss (100M x 0.6 x 0.01), not credit VaR.
- D ($0.45M): Mixed up factors / used wrong rate.

📌 Exam tip: Credit VaR uses (WCDR - PD). If you forget to subtract PD you'll get a too-large, but plausible-looking, wrong answer - exactly the trap GARP sets.

**Q7.** In the Merton model, the equity of a firm is viewed as equivalent to:
- A. A put option on the firm's assets
- B. A call option on the firm's assets, with strike equal to the face value of debt
- C. Risk-free debt plus a put option
- D. A forward contract on the firm's assets

**Answer: B**

The question tests the core intuition of the Merton model.

In Merton's view, shareholders own the company but owe the debt. At maturity, if the firm's assets $$V_T$$ exceed the debt face value $$D$$, shareholders keep the leftover $$V_T - D$$. If assets are worth less than the debt, shareholders walk away with nothing (limited liability). So their payoff is:

$$\max(V_T - D, 0)$$

That is exactly the payoff of a CALL option on the firm's assets with strike price = D (the debt face value).

Analogy: Owning equity is like having an option to "buy back" the company from the lenders by paying off the debt. You only exercise (pay the debt and keep the firm) if the firm is worth more than what you owe.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: A put on assets describes the lenders' implicit position (risky debt = risk-free debt minus a put), not the equity holders'.
- C: Risk-free debt plus a put doesn't match equity's payoff; it relates to the debt side.
- D: A forward has unlimited downside (linear payoff). Equity has limited downside (you can't lose more than your investment) - that's option-like, not forward-like.

📌 Exam tip: Equity = call on assets (strike = debt). Risky debt = risk-free debt minus a put on assets. Memorize both halves.

**Q8.** Which of the following correctly describes how portfolio expected loss and portfolio unexpected loss aggregate across individual loans?
- A. Both are simple sums of the individual loan values.
- B. EL is a simple sum of individual ELs; UL is NOT a simple sum because it depends on default correlations.
- C. EL depends on correlations; UL is a simple sum.
- D. Neither depends on the individual loans.

**Answer: B**

The question is about adding up EL and UL across many loans.

Expected loss is an average (an expectation). Averages always add up directly, no matter how the loans relate to each other. So portfolio EL = sum of each loan's EL.

Unexpected loss is about variation (standard deviation). Variations do NOT simply add - they combine according to how correlated the losses are:

$$UL_P = \sqrt{\sum_i\sum_j \rho_{ij}\, UL_i\, UL_j}$$

If correlations are low, the portfolio UL is much smaller than the sum of individual ULs (diversification benefit).

Analogy: If you add up everyone's average spending, you just sum it (EL). But the "swinginess" of total spending (UL) depends on whether people's spending moves together - if they spend independently, the ups and downs partly cancel out.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: UL is not a simple sum, so this is wrong.
- C: Reversed - EL is the simple sum, UL is the correlation-dependent one.
- D: Both clearly depend on the individual loans.

📌 Exam tip: Expectations add (EL is linear); standard deviations don't (UL needs correlations). This is why diversification helps with UL but never with EL.

**Q9.** Credit loss distributions are typically described as "highly skewed." This means:
- A. Losses are equally likely to be above or below the mean.
- B. Most years have small losses near the EL, with rare but very large losses in the tail.
- C. Losses follow a perfectly symmetric normal distribution.
- D. The expected loss equals the unexpected loss.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what "skewed" means for credit losses.

A skewed credit loss distribution means the shape is lopsided: in most years losses are small (close to the expected loss), but every so often - usually when many borrowers default together in a downturn - there is a huge loss in the right-hand tail.

Analogy: Think of earthquakes. Most days nothing happens (tiny losses), but rarely a massive quake causes enormous damage. The "average" day looks calm, yet the rare disaster dominates how much insurance you need.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: That describes a symmetric distribution, the opposite of skewed.
- C: A normal distribution is symmetric, not skewed - credit losses are not normal.
- D: EL and UL being equal has nothing to do with skewness; in credit, UL is usually much larger than EL.

📌 Exam tip: Skewness in credit losses is WHY banks need so much capital - the rare fat tail (driven by correlated defaults) is far bigger than the everyday losses.

**Q10.** Which of the following is a recognized weakness of the Merton model for estimating default probability?
- A. It is backward-looking and never updates.
- B. It assumes asset returns are normally distributed, which can understate the probability of extreme (tail) defaults.
- C. It cannot be applied to any publicly traded firm.
- D. It requires no information about the firm's debt.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a genuine weakness of the Merton model.

The Merton model relies on the Black-Scholes-Merton option framework, which assumes asset values move with normally distributed returns. Real defaults cluster and asset values can crash more sharply than a normal distribution predicts, so the normality assumption tends to understate the chance of extreme defaults (the tail). Other weaknesses: it needs traded equity and assumes a simple single-maturity debt structure.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: This is actually a STRENGTH, not a weakness - the model is forward-looking and updates continuously as the stock price moves. So this statement is false.
- C: False. It can be applied to publicly traded firms; in fact it REQUIRES traded equity to back out asset value and volatility. The limitation is the opposite - it struggles with firms that are NOT publicly traded.
- D: False. The model very much needs the firm's debt level (the strike D) to compute distance to default.

📌 Exam tip: Merton's strengths are "forward-looking, updates with the stock price." Its weaknesses are "needs traded equity, simple debt assumption, normality understates the tail." Don't confuse the two.
