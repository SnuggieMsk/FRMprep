**Q1.** A 90-day Treasury bill is quoted at a discount rate of 2.00% with a face value of $100. What is its price?
- A. $98.00
- B. $99.50
- C. $99.80
- D. $98.50

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the price of a T-bill given its discount rate. T-bills use a discount-rate convention on an actual/360 basis.

The formula is:

$$P = F\left(1 - d \cdot \frac{n}{360}\right)$$

where F = face = $100, d = 0.02, n = 90 days.

Step by step:
- $$\frac{n}{360} = \frac{90}{360} = 0.25$$
- $$d \times 0.25 = 0.02 \times 0.25 = 0.005$$
- $$1 - 0.005 = 0.995$$
- $$P = 100 \times 0.995 = 99.50$$

So the price is $99.50.

Analogy: The bank "discounts" the $100 you'll get in 90 days by a small slice (0.5%), so you pay $99.50 today and collect $100 later. Your profit is the 50-cent difference.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($98.00): Used the full year (multiplied 0.02 x 100 = 2) and forgot the 90/360 fraction.
- C ($99.80): Used n/360 incorrectly (e.g., 36/360) or miscomputed the fraction.
- D ($98.50): Used 90/120 or some wrong day fraction.

📌 Exam tip: For a T-bill price, always apply the day-count fraction n/360. The discount is taken off the FACE value, not the price.

**Q2.** Using the T-bill in Q1 (90-day, price $99.50, face $100), what is the bill's true (investment) yield, annualized on an actual/365 basis?
- A. Exactly 2.00%
- B. 2.038%
- C. 1.96%
- D. 2.50%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the true yield, which is the actual return on the money you invest.

The true yield divides the gain by the PRICE PAID (not face) and annualizes using 365 days:

$$y = \frac{F - P}{P} \times \frac{365}{n}$$

Step by step:
- Gain = $$100 - 99.50 = 0.50$$
- $$\frac{0.50}{99.50} = 0.005025$$ (this is the holding-period return)
- $$\frac{365}{90} = 4.0556$$
- $$y = 0.005025 \times 4.0556 = 0.02038 = 2.038\%$$

So the true yield is 2.038%, which is HIGHER than the quoted 2.00% discount rate.

Why the true yield is higher: (1) you divide the gain by the smaller price ($99.50), not the face ($100), and (2) you annualize over 365 days, not 360. Both effects push the true yield above the quoted discount rate.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (2.00%): That's the quoted discount rate, which always understates the true yield.
- C (1.96%): Lower than the discount rate - wrong direction.
- D (2.50%): Overstates the yield; uses wrong inputs.

📌 Exam tip: The quoted T-bill discount rate is NOT the yield - the true yield is always a bit higher because the gain is divided by price (not face) and uses a 365-day year.

**Q3.** The dirty (invoice) price that a bond buyer actually pays equals:
- A. The clean price minus accrued interest
- B. The clean price plus accrued interest
- C. The face value plus accrued interest
- D. The quoted price, always

**Answer: B**

The question asks what makes up the invoice price.

Coupon bonds are QUOTED clean (without accrued interest), but the buyer actually pays the dirty price, which adds the interest that has built up since the last coupon:

$$\text{Dirty} = \text{Clean} + \text{Accrued interest}$$

Why? Whoever holds the bond on the coupon date receives the FULL coupon, even interest earned by the previous owner. So the buyer compensates the seller for the days the seller held the bond by adding accrued interest.

Analogy: If you buy a fruit tree halfway through the season, you pay a bit extra for the fruit that's already grown but not yet picked - that's the accrued interest.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Subtracting is backward - accrued interest is ADDED, not removed.
- C: It's clean PRICE plus accrued, not face value plus accrued.
- D: The quoted (clean) price is not what you pay; you pay the dirty price.

📌 Exam tip: Quoted = clean; paid = dirty = clean + accrued interest. US Treasuries use actual/actual for the accrual fraction.

**Q4.** Bond A is a 6-month bond paying a 4% annual coupon semiannually, priced at 100.98. The single cash flow at t = 0.5 is 102 (coupon of 2 plus principal of 100). What is the 6-month discount factor d(0.5)?
- A. 0.9900
- B. 0.9803
- C. 1.0102
- D. 0.9706

**Answer: A**

The question asks you to extract a discount factor from a bond price. A discount factor d(t) is the price today of $1 delivered at time t.

The bond has just ONE cash flow of 102 at t = 0.5, and its price is 100.98. So:

$$102 \times d(0.5) = 100.98$$

Solve for d(0.5):

$$d(0.5) = \frac{100.98}{102} = 0.9900$$

So d(0.5) = 0.9900. This means $1 received in 6 months is worth about 99 cents today.

Why the others are wrong:
- B (0.9803): This is roughly 100/102 - forgot the bond price is 100.98, not 100.
- C (1.0102): This is 102/100.98 (the fraction flipped upside down). A discount factor must be BELOW 1 for positive rates.
- D (0.9706): That's closer to a 1-year discount factor, not 6-month.

📌 Exam tip: A discount factor = price / cash flow, and it must be less than 1 (and decline as maturity grows). If you get a number above 1, you flipped the ratio.

**Q5.** Given d(0.5) = 0.9900 and d(1.0) = 0.9705, what is the fair price of a 1-year Treasury paying a 6% coupon semiannually (cash flows of 3 at t = 0.5 and 103 at t = 1.0)?
- A. $100.00
- B. $102.93
- C. $106.00
- D. $99.96

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to price a bond using discount factors. Any cash flow stream is priced by multiplying each cash flow by its own discount factor and summing:

$$P = \sum_t CF_t \cdot d(t)$$

The cash flows: 3 at t = 0.5 (half of the 6% coupon on 100) and 103 at t = 1.0 (final coupon of 3 plus principal of 100).

Step by step:
- $$3 \times d(0.5) = 3 \times 0.9900 = 2.97$$
- $$103 \times d(1.0) = 103 \times 0.9705 = 99.9615 \approx 99.96$$
- $$P = 2.97 + 99.96 = 102.93$$

So the fair price is $102.93.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($100.00): Assumes the bond prices at par - but a 6% coupon when rates are lower makes it trade above par.
- C ($106.00): Just summed the cash flows (3 + 103) with no discounting at all.
- D ($99.96): Discounted only the final cash flow (103) and forgot the t = 0.5 coupon.

📌 Exam tip: Price = sum of (each cash flow x its discount factor). Don't forget the intermediate coupons - discount every cash flow on its own date.

**Q6.** A 1-year 6% semiannual-coupon Treasury has a fair price of $102.93 but trades in the market at $102.60. What is the correct arbitrage and the riskless profit per $100 face?
- A. Short the bond and buy STRIPS; profit $0.33
- B. Buy the bond and short the replicating STRIPS package; profit $0.33
- C. Buy the bond and buy STRIPS; profit $0.66
- D. There is no arbitrage; the prices are close enough

**Answer: B**

The question asks how to profit from a bond trading below its fair value.

The bond is CHEAP: it trades at $102.60 but is worth $102.93. The rule is buy low, sell (short) high.

- BUY the cheap bond at $102.60.
- SHORT the replicating STRIPS package, which is worth $102.93, receiving $102.93.

Net cash today:

$$102.93 - 102.60 = 0.33$$

You pocket $0.33 today. In the future, the bond's coupons exactly cover what you owe on the short STRIPS, so the future cash flows net to zero - a riskless profit of $0.33 per $100 face.

Analogy: You find a $20 bill on sale for $19.67. You buy it for $19.67 and immediately sell it for its true $20, keeping the 33-cent difference with no risk.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Reversed legs - you'd buy the bond (it's cheap), not short it. Shorting the cheap thing loses money.
- C: Buying both costs money and creates no offsetting position; that's not arbitrage.
- D: $0.33 is a real, exploitable gap in this idealized (no-friction) setup, so there IS an arbitrage.

📌 Exam tip: Direction discipline - BUY the LOW-priced version, SHORT the HIGH-priced version. Under exam pressure candidates flip the legs; the cheap asset is always the one you buy.

**Q7.** According to the law of one price, if two portfolios produce identical future cash flows, then:
- A. They must trade at the same price, or an arbitrage exists.
- B. The one with the longer maturity must be cheaper.
- C. Their prices can differ by any amount with no consequence.
- D. The higher-coupon one must always be more expensive.

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the meaning of the law of one price.

The law of one price says that two things delivering exactly the same future cash flows must cost the same today. If they don't, you can buy the cheap one, short the expensive one, and lock in a riskless profit with zero net future obligation - so the mispricing should not survive.

Analogy: Two identical bottles of water in the same shop must cost the same. If one were cheaper, everyone would buy the cheap one and resell at the higher price until the gap closed.

Why the others are wrong:
- B: Maturity doesn't decide price here; identical cash flows means identical price regardless of how the portfolios are built.
- C: Prices canNOT differ freely - a difference is precisely an arbitrage opportunity.
- D: Coupon size is irrelevant if the total cash flows are identical; what matters is the cash flows, not the labels.

📌 Exam tip: Law of one price = identical cash flows -> identical price. Any gap is an arbitrage (buy cheap, short expensive).

**Q8.** Treasury STRIPS are best described as:
- A. Coupon-paying Treasury bonds with extra liquidity.
- B. Single-payment zero-coupon claims created by splitting a Treasury's coupons and principal.
- C. Floating-rate Treasury notes.
- D. Bonds that pay only interest and never return principal.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what STRIPS are.

STRIPS are created by splitting a Treasury bond into its individual payments. Each coupon becomes a C-STRIP and the final principal becomes a P-STRIP. Each piece is a zero-coupon claim - it makes exactly ONE payment at one date. Because of this, a STRIP's price IS a discount factor (times 100).

Analogy: A coupon bond is like a booklet of dated gift cards. Stripping it tears out each card so it can be sold separately, and each card pays once on its own date.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: STRIPS are zero-coupon (single payment), not coupon-paying.
- C: They are fixed single payments, not floating-rate notes.
- D: This is false - P-STRIPS return principal, and C-STRIPS pay a (former) coupon amount; each is just a single payment.

📌 Exam tip: STRIPS = zero-coupon single-payment Treasuries. C-STRIPS (from coupons), P-STRIPS (from principal). Their prices equal discount factors x 100. Stripping and reconstitution keep prices consistent.

**Q9.** A 180-day Treasury bill is quoted at a 3.20% discount rate. Its price per $100 of face value is:
- A. $98.40
- B. $96.80
- C. $98.42
- D. $96.40

**Answer: A**

The question asks for a T-bill price from its discount rate.

The formula is:

$$P = F\left(1 - d \cdot \frac{n}{360}\right)$$

with F = 100, d = 0.032, n = 180.

Step by step:
- $$\frac{180}{360} = 0.5$$
- $$0.032 \times 0.5 = 0.016$$
- $$1 - 0.016 = 0.984$$
- $$P = 100 \times 0.984 = 98.40$$

So the price is $98.40.

Why the others are wrong:
- B ($96.80): Used the full 3.20% with no day fraction (0.032 x 100 = 3.20 discount), forgetting 180/360.
- C ($98.42): Used 365 instead of 360 in the day fraction, giving a slightly off number.
- D ($96.40): Doubled the discount or used the wrong day count.

📌 Exam tip: T-bill price uses face x (1 - d x n/360). The 180-day bill has a half-year fraction, so the discount is half the annual rate.

**Q10.** Why do apparent arbitrage opportunities between a bond and its replicating STRIPS sometimes persist in the real world?
- A. Because the law of one price does not apply to Treasuries.
- B. Because of frictions such as bid-ask spreads, financing (repo) costs, and liquidity premia.
- C. Because STRIPS are riskier than the underlying bond.
- D. Because Treasury prices are randomly set and never reflect value.

**Answer: B**

The question asks why small mispricings can survive.

In an idealized world, arbitrage would close any gap instantly. But in the real world there are costs and frictions: bid-ask spreads, the cost of financing positions in the repo market, balance-sheet and capital charges, and liquidity premia (for example, on-the-run Treasuries trade rich to off-the-run ones). If the apparent mispricing is smaller than these round-trip costs, it simply isn't worth exploiting - so it survives.

Analogy: You spot a $20 bill selling for $19.95 in a city across the country. The 5-cent profit isn't worth the airfare, so the deal sits there unexploited.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: The law of one price still applies; frictions just cap how tightly it holds.
- C: STRIPS are also default-free Treasury claims - not riskier than the bond.
- D: Treasury prices do reflect value; they just include liquidity and financing effects.

📌 Exam tip: Real-world frictions (bid-ask, repo financing, capital charges, liquidity premia) mean a mispricing smaller than round-trip costs is NOT exploitable. On-the-run bonds trade rich to off-the-run.
