**Q1.** A pool of mortgages prepays at a constant conditional prepayment rate (CPR) of 6%. The single monthly mortality (SMM) rate is closest to:
- A. 0.50%
- B. 0.51%
- C. 0.60%
- D. 6.00%

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to turn an annual prepayment speed (CPR) into its monthly equivalent (SMM). The catch: you cannot just divide by 12, because prepayment compounds.

The correct conversion is based on survival (the fraction that does NOT prepay):
$$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR})^{1/12}$$

Plug in CPR = 0.06:
$$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - 0.06)^{1/12} = 1 - 0.94^{1/12}$$

Step 1 — compute $$0.94^{1/12}$$ (the 12th root of 0.94):
$$0.94^{1/12} = 0.994857$$

Step 2 — subtract from 1:
$$\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.994857 = 0.005143 = 0.5143\% \approx 0.51\%$$

So B is correct.

Plain words: 94% of the pool survives the year without prepaying. To find the monthly survival, take the 12th root, then the part that prepays each month is 1 minus that.

- A (0.50%) is the trap of dividing 6% by 12 — it ignores compounding and is always offered as bait.
- C (0.60%) is too high; no correct method gives it.
- D (6.00%) just repeats the annual CPR as if it were monthly.

📌 Exam tip: NEVER divide CPR by 12 to get SMM. Use $$\text{SMM} = 1 - (1-\text{CPR})^{1/12}$$. The compounding makes SMM slightly above CPR/12 (0.5143% vs 0.50%).

**Q2.** Under 150 PSA, the CPR assumed for month 12 of a pool's life is:
- A. 2.4%
- B. 3.6%
- C. 6.0%
- D. 9.0%

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the prepayment speed in month 12 under a PSA multiple. First find the 100 PSA speed, then scale it.

The 100 PSA benchmark: CPR starts at 0.2% in month 1 and rises by 0.2% each month until it hits 6% at month 30, then stays flat. So for any month $$t \le 30$$:
$$\text{CPR}_{100} = 0.2\% \times t$$

Step 1 — 100 PSA at month 12:
$$0.2\% \times 12 = 2.4\%$$
(Month 12 is still on the rising "ramp," below the 6% cap, so this is valid.)

Step 2 — scale to 150 PSA (multiply by 1.5):
$$1.5 \times 2.4\% = 3.6\%$$

So B is correct.

Picture a ramp: prepayments climb steadily for the first 30 months, then level off at 6%. At month 12 we're still climbing. 150 PSA just means "1.5 times faster" along the whole ramp.

- A (2.4%) is the 100 PSA speed — correct intermediate step but forgets to multiply by 1.5.
- C (6.0%) is the plateau speed reached at month 30+, not month 12.
- D (9.0%) is the 150 PSA plateau (1.5 × 6%), which applies only after month 30, not at month 12.

📌 Exam tip: For PSA on the ramp (month ≤ 30): $$\text{CPR} = \text{multiple} \times 0.2\% \times \text{month}$$. After month 30 it's just $$\text{multiple} \times 6\%$$.

**Q3.** When market interest rates fall well below a mortgage pool's loan rate, a pass-through investor primarily faces:
- A. Extension risk and positive convexity
- B. Contraction risk and negative convexity
- C. Credit risk from the agency guarantee
- D. No risk, since principal is returned at par

**Answer: B**

The question asks what happens to a mortgage investor when rates drop sharply below the loan rate.

Why B is correct: when rates fall, borrowers rush to refinance, so prepayments speed up and principal comes back EARLY — this is contraction risk. The trouble is you must reinvest that returned cash at the new LOW rates. Also, because borrowers can prepay, the bond's price cannot rise much when rates fall (the upside is capped) — this is NEGATIVE convexity.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — extension risk is the OPPOSITE: it happens when rates RISE and prepayments slow, lengthening the bond. And MBS show negative, not positive, convexity.
- C — agency MBS (Ginnie/Fannie/Freddie) carry essentially no credit risk; the guarantee removes it, leaving prepayment risk.
- D — there is definitely risk: early return of principal at low reinvestment rates hurts you.

Analogy: it's like lending your friend money at a great interest rate, and the moment rates drop they pay you back early — now you can only relend at the new, lousy rate.

📌 Exam tip: Rates DOWN → prepay fast → contraction risk + negative convexity. Rates UP → prepay slow → extension risk. The MBS investor is squeezed both ways.

**Q4.** A $100,000, 15-year level-payment mortgage carries a 6% annual rate with monthly compounding. The fixed monthly payment is closest to ($$1.005^{-180} = 0.40748$$):
- A. $599.55
- B. $843.86
- C. $877.43
- D. $1,000.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the constant monthly payment that fully pays off the loan over 15 years.

The level-payment formula is:
$$X = \frac{L \, r}{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}$$

where $$L = 100{,}000$$, the monthly rate $$r = 6\%/12 = 0.005$$, and $$n = 15 \times 12 = 180$$ payments.

Step 1 — numerator:
$$L \times r = 100{,}000 \times 0.005 = 500$$

Step 2 — denominator using $$(1.005)^{-180} = 0.40748$$:
$$1 - 0.40748 = 0.59252$$

Step 3 — divide:
$$X = \frac{500}{0.59252} = 843.86$$

So B is correct: about $843.86 per month.

Plain words: the payment is set so that 180 equal monthly checks exactly wipe out the loan, covering both interest and principal.

- A ($599.55) is roughly the payment for a 30-YEAR loan (longer term → smaller payment); here it's only 15 years.
- C ($877.43) uses a slightly wrong rate or term and over-shoots.
- D ($1,000.00) just splits $100,000 into chunks ignoring interest and the amortization structure.

📌 Exam tip: Shorter loans have HIGHER monthly payments (less time to spread the principal). If your 15-year payment came out lower than a 30-year one, you swapped the terms.

**Q5.** A mortgage pool's beginning-of-month balance is $100,000,000 and scheduled principal for the month is $150,000. The pool prepays at an SMM of 0.5143%. The dollar prepayment for the month is closest to:
- A. $514,000
- B. $513,529
- C. $771,450
- D. $500,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the dollar amount prepaid. The key is to apply the SMM to the balance AFTER removing this month's scheduled principal, not the raw beginning balance.

The formula rearranged from the SMM definition:
$$\text{Prepayment} = \text{SMM} \times (\text{Beginning balance} - \text{Scheduled principal})$$

Step 1 — balance net of scheduled principal:
$$100{,}000{,}000 - 150{,}000 = 99{,}850{,}000$$

Step 2 — apply the SMM:
$$0.005143 \times 99{,}850{,}000 = 513{,}529$$

So B is correct: about $513,529.

Plain words: scheduled principal is the part everyone pays anyway. Prepayment is EXTRA, voluntary repayment, and it applies only to what's left after the scheduled chunk is taken out.

- A ($514,000) applies the SMM to the full $100,000,000 (forgetting to subtract scheduled principal), giving a slightly too-high figure.
- C ($771,450) uses a wrong SMM or doubles something; not produced correctly.
- D ($500,000) is a rough guess ignoring the precise SMM and the net balance.

📌 Exam tip: Always subtract scheduled principal FIRST, then multiply by SMM. Total principal returned = scheduled principal + prepayment ($150,000 + $513,529 = $663,529 here).

**Q6.** Which statement correctly distinguishes agency from non-agency mortgage-backed securities?
- A. Agency MBS carry significant credit risk; non-agency MBS do not
- B. Agency MBS investors bear essentially only prepayment risk; non-agency investors also bear credit risk
- C. Both are guaranteed by the US Treasury
- D. Non-agency MBS may only securitize conforming loans

**Answer: B**

The question asks how agency and non-agency (private-label) MBS differ in the risks investors face.

Why B is correct: agency MBS are issued or guaranteed by Ginnie Mae (full faith and credit of the US government) or the GSEs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, so the credit risk is essentially removed — investors mainly bear PREPAYMENT risk. Non-agency MBS securitize jumbo, alt-A, or subprime loans with no such guarantee, so investors bear CREDIT risk too (managed with credit enhancement like subordination and overcollateralization).

Why the others are wrong:
- A — backwards: agency MBS have little/no credit risk; non-agency MBS DO carry credit risk.
- C — only Ginnie Mae has full government backing; Fannie/Freddie are GSEs, and non-agency MBS have no Treasury guarantee at all.
- D — it's the reverse: AGENCY pools require conforming loans; non-agency pools handle the non-conforming (jumbo/subprime) loans.

Analogy: an agency MBS is like a loan co-signed by a very reliable guarantor — you mostly worry about being paid back early. A non-agency MBS has no co-signer, so you also worry about not being paid back at all.

📌 Exam tip: Agency = (almost) no credit risk, only prepayment risk, conforming loans. Non-agency = credit risk + prepayment risk, non-conforming loans, needs credit enhancement.

**Q7.** In a CMO, a PAC (planned amortization class) tranche receives a stable, scheduled principal flow. The tranche that absorbs the prepayment variability to protect the PAC is the:
- A. Support (companion) tranche
- B. Sequential-pay tranche
- C. Senior tranche
- D. Interest-only tranche

**Answer: A**

The question asks which tranche "takes the hit" of prepayment swings so the PAC can stay stable.

Why A is correct: a PAC is designed to receive principal on a predictable schedule. To make that possible, the SUPPORT (also called companion) tranche absorbs the extra prepayments when speeds are high and receives less when speeds are low. It cushions the PAC, so it bears more prepayment risk in exchange.

Why the others are wrong:
- B — sequential-pay tranches simply receive principal in ORDER (one after another); they are not the specific shock-absorber paired with a PAC.
- C — "senior" refers to credit priority (who gets paid first if losses occur), not to prepayment protection.
- D — an interest-only tranche receives only interest, not principal; it's a stripped cash flow, not the PAC's companion.

Analogy: the PAC is a VIP with a guaranteed smooth ride; the support tranche is the buffer that takes all the bumps so the VIP doesn't feel them.

📌 Exam tip: PAC = stable scheduled principal; SUPPORT/companion = absorbs the prepayment variability. Remember CMOs only REDISTRIBUTE prepayment risk — they never eliminate it.

**Q8.** In the first month of a $300,000, 30-year level-payment mortgage at a 6% annual rate (0.5% monthly), with a monthly payment of $1,798.65, the portion of that payment that goes to PRINCIPAL is closest to:
- A. $1,500.00
- B. $298.65
- C. $1,798.65
- D. $900.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks how much of the first payment actually reduces the loan balance (principal), versus how much is just interest.

The split each month:
- Interest = monthly rate × beginning balance
- Principal = payment − interest

Step 1 — first-month interest:
$$0.005 \times 300{,}000 = 1{,}500.00$$

Step 2 — first-month principal:
$$1{,}798.65 - 1{,}500.00 = 298.65$$

So B is correct: only $298.65 of the first payment chips away at the loan.

Plain words: early in a mortgage, almost the whole payment is interest because the balance is huge. Only a tiny sliver pays down principal at first; that sliver grows over time.

- A ($1,500.00) is the INTEREST portion, not the principal — the most common mix-up.
- C ($1,798.65) is the TOTAL payment, not just the principal piece.
- D ($900.00) splits the payment roughly in half, which is wrong for month 1 of a 30-year loan.

📌 Exam tip: Early payments are mostly interest; principal share is small at first and grows. Interest = rate × balance; principal = payment − interest. Don't report interest when asked for principal.

**Q9.** A dollar roll transaction involves an MBS holder who:
- A. Buys a TBA for this month and sells it for next month
- B. Sells a TBA for delivery this month and simultaneously buys it back for delivery next month
- C. Exchanges a pass-through for a CMO of equal value
- D. Lends bonds outright with no repurchase agreement

**Answer: B**

The question asks what actually happens in a dollar roll.

Why B is correct: in a dollar roll, the MBS holder SELLS a TBA position for delivery this month and at the same time agrees to BUY it back for delivery next month. The price difference between the two settlement months (the "drop"), plus the coupon/principal given up during the month, determines the implied financing cost. When that implied rate is below the holder's funding rate, the roll is cheap financing ("trades special").

Why the others are wrong:
- A — this reverses the legs; the holder sells the nearby month and buys the deferred month, not the other way around.
- C — swapping a pass-through for a CMO is not a dollar roll; that's a different structuring transaction.
- D — a dollar roll specifically INCLUDES the buy-back (repurchase) leg; an outright loan with no repurchase is something else.

Analogy: it's like selling your car to a dealer today and arranging to buy it back next month — during the month the dealer uses the car (gets the cash flows), and the price gap is effectively what you pay/earn for that arrangement.

📌 Exam tip: Dollar roll = sell now, repurchase later. If the implied financing cost (from the "drop") is below your funding rate, the roll is cheap financing and is said to "trade special."

**Q10.** Weighted average life (WAL) of an MBS is best described as the:
- A. Average time to receipt of principal, weighted by principal amounts
- B. Present-value-weighted average time of all cash flows
- C. Bond's price sensitivity to a change in interest rates
- D. Total number of years until the final scheduled payment

**Answer: A**

The question asks what WAL actually measures.

Why A is correct: WAL is the average time until PRINCIPAL is repaid, weighting each principal repayment by its size. It answers the simple question "on average, when does my money come back?" It is a cash-flow TIMING measure, not a price-sensitivity measure.

Why the others are wrong:
- B — that describes DURATION (which weights the present values of ALL cash flows, interest and principal). WAL ignores discounting and looks only at principal.
- C — price sensitivity to rate changes is duration (and convexity), not WAL.
- D — that's just the stated maturity; WAL is shorter than the final maturity because principal comes back gradually (and faster with prepayments).

Analogy: WAL is like asking "on average, when do I get my deposit back?" — it's about timing of your money returning, not about how the bond's price wiggles when rates move.

📌 Exam tip: WAL = average TIMING of principal return (a cash-flow measure). Duration = price SENSITIVITY to rates (PV-weighted). They are different concepts — don't swap them.
