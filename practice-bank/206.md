**Q1.** A sample of 36 monthly returns has a mean of 1.2% and a sample standard deviation of 3.6%. What is the t-statistic for testing $$H_0: \mu = 0$$?
- A. 0.33
- B. 1.20
- C. 2.00
- D. 12.0

**Answer: C**

The question asks: how many "standard errors" away from zero is our sample mean? That number is the t-statistic.

Step 1 — Write the formula:
$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}$$

Step 2 — Find the standard error (the bottom of the fraction). It is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size:
$$\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{3.6}{\sqrt{36}} = \frac{3.6}{6} = 0.60$$

Step 3 — Plug into the formula. The mean is 1.2%, the value we are testing ($$\mu_0$$) is 0:
$$t = \frac{1.2 - 0}{0.60} = \frac{1.2}{0.60} = 2.00$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.33): this divides 1.2 by 3.6 and forgets to use the standard error — it ignores the sample size entirely.
- B (1.20): this just uses the mean by itself with no division — forgetting the whole point of dividing by the standard error.
- D (12.0): this divides 1.2 by 0.1, which would happen if you used $$3.6/36 = 0.1$$ instead of $$3.6/\sqrt{36} = 0.6$$. The trap is forgetting the square root.

Analogy: the standard error is like the "wobble" in a measurement. The t-statistic asks "how many wobbles away from zero are we?" Two wobbles away is a meaningful distance.

📌 Exam tip: Always take the square root of $$n$$ in the denominator. $$s/\sqrt{n}$$, never $$s/n$$.

**Q2.** A test produces a p-value of 0.03. Which statement is TRUE?
- A. There is a 3% probability that $$H_0$$ is true.
- B. Reject $$H_0$$ at the 5% level but not at the 1% level.
- C. Reject $$H_0$$ at the 1% level but not at the 5% level.
- D. The power of the test is 97%.

**Answer: B**

The question is really asking: at which significance levels does a p-value of 0.03 lead us to reject the null?

The decision rule is simple: reject $$H_0$$ whenever the p-value is smaller than $$\alpha$$ (the significance level).

- At $$\alpha = 5\%$$ (i.e., 0.05): is $$0.03 < 0.05$$? Yes → reject.
- At $$\alpha = 1\%$$ (i.e., 0.01): is $$0.03 < 0.01$$? No → fail to reject.

So we reject at 5% but not at 1%. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: this is the single most common misreading. The p-value is the probability of seeing data this extreme *if the null were true* — it is NOT the probability that the null is true. GARP loves to test this exact trap.
- C: this reverses the logic. A smaller $$\alpha$$ (1%) is a stricter bar, so it is harder to reject, not easier.
- D: power has nothing to do with $$1 - p$$. Power is $$1 - \beta$$ and depends on the true effect size and sample size, not on the p-value.

Analogy: the p-value is like the "surprise level" of your evidence assuming nothing special is going on. A surprise of 0.03 clears the everyday 5% bar but not the strict 1% bar.

📌 Exam tip: Reject when p < α. And remember: the p-value is about the data, not about the probability the null is true.

**Q3.** Lowering the significance level of a test from 5% to 1% while keeping the sample size fixed will:
- A. Decrease both Type I and Type II error probabilities.
- B. Decrease the Type I error probability but increase the Type II error probability.
- C. Increase the power of the test.
- D. Increase the size of the test.

**Answer: B**

The question asks: what happens to the two kinds of errors when we make the test stricter (smaller $$\alpha$$) but do not collect more data?

Key definitions:
- Type I error = rejecting a true null. Its probability equals the size $$\alpha$$.
- Type II error = failing to reject a false null. Its probability is $$\beta$$.
- Power = $$1 - \beta$$.

If we move $$\alpha$$ from 5% down to 1%, we are demanding stronger evidence before rejecting. This directly lowers the Type I error rate (it IS $$\alpha$$). But because we now reject less often, we will also more often miss real effects — so the Type II error rate $$\beta$$ rises. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: you cannot reduce both at once with a fixed sample — there is a trade-off. The only way to shrink both is to collect more data.
- C: power = $$1 - \beta$$; since $$\beta$$ went up, power went down, not up.
- D: the size of the test IS $$\alpha$$. We just lowered it to 1%, so the size decreased, not increased.

Analogy: imagine a strict smoke alarm. Make it less sensitive (smaller $$\alpha$$) and it cries "fire!" less often by mistake (fewer Type I errors), but it will also miss more real fires (more Type II errors).

📌 Exam tip: "The only free lunch is more data." For fixed n, lowering α raises β.

**Q4.** A 95% confidence interval for a regression coefficient is $$(0.4, 2.6)$$. A two-sided 5% test of $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ would:
- A. Fail to reject, since the interval contains only positive values.
- B. Reject, since 0 lies outside the interval.
- C. Be inconclusive without the actual t-statistic.
- D. Reject only at the 1% level.

**Answer: B**

The question tests the duality between confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

The rule: a two-sided test of size $$\alpha$$ rejects $$H_0: \beta = \beta_0$$ exactly when $$\beta_0$$ lies OUTSIDE the $$(1-\alpha)$$ confidence interval. The interval is the complete set of null values that would NOT be rejected.

Here the 95% interval is $$(0.4, 2.6)$$. Is the tested value 0 inside this interval? No — 0 is below 0.4, so it lies outside. Therefore we reject $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ at the 5% level. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: the fact that the interval contains positive values is exactly why 0 is excluded — that means reject, not fail to reject. The conclusion is backwards.
- C: we do not need the t-statistic. The confidence interval already contains all the information; that is the whole point of the duality.
- D: the interval is a 95% interval, which corresponds to the 5% test. It tells us nothing directly about the 1% level (that would need a 99% interval).

Analogy: the confidence interval is a "fence" around the believable values. If 0 is outside the fence, the data says 0 is not a believable value — so reject it.

📌 Exam tip: 95% CI excludes the null value ⇔ reject at 5%. No extra calculation needed.

**Q5.** A strategy's 25 monthly returns average 0.80% with a sample standard deviation of 2.0%. Testing $$H_0: \mu = 0$$ against $$H_1: \mu \ne 0$$ at the 5% level (critical value $$t_{0.025,24} = 2.064$$), the correct decision is:
- A. Reject $$H_0$$; the mean return is significantly positive.
- B. Fail to reject $$H_0$$; no significant evidence of a nonzero mean.
- C. Reject $$H_0$$; the mean return is significantly negative.
- D. The test cannot be performed with only 25 observations.

**Answer: B**

The question asks: is an average return of 0.80% far enough from zero, given the noise, to be statistically convincing in a two-sided test?

Step 1 — Standard error:
$$\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.0}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{2.0}{5} = 0.40$$

Step 2 — Compute the t-statistic:
$$t = \frac{0.80 - 0}{0.40} = \frac{0.80}{0.40} = 2.00$$

Step 3 — Compare to the critical value. For a two-sided 5% test with $$24$$ degrees of freedom, the critical value is 2.064.
$$2.00 < 2.064$$

Since our statistic (2.00) does not exceed the critical value (2.064), we fail to reject. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: it is a close call (2.00 is near 2.064), but 2.00 does not clear the bar, so we cannot claim significance. The trap is rounding "almost there" up to "significant."
- C: the mean is positive (0.80%), so a "significantly negative" conclusion makes no sense even before the math.
- D: 25 observations is perfectly fine — we just use the t-distribution with $$n - 1 = 24$$ degrees of freedom.

Note the contrast: had this been a one-sided test, the critical value would be $$t_{0.05,24} = 1.711$$, and $$2.00 > 1.711$$ would reject. The one- vs. two-tail choice flips the answer here.

📌 Exam tip: Compare the t-statistic to the correct critical value before declaring significance — "almost" does not count.

**Q6.** A risk manager wants to test whether a fund's mean monthly return "differs from" zero. The appropriate setup is:
- A. A one-sided test with $$H_1: \mu > 0$$.
- B. A one-sided test with $$H_1: \mu < 0$$.
- C. A two-sided test with $$H_1: \mu \ne 0$$.
- D. No hypothesis test is needed; just report the mean.

**Answer: C**

The question is about reading the wording to choose between a one-sided and a two-sided test.

The phrase "differs from zero" does not specify a direction — the mean could be above OR below zero and still "differ." Whenever the question cares about deviation in either direction, the alternative is $$H_1: \mu \ne 0$$, which is a two-sided test. The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: $$H_1: \mu > 0$$ is one-sided and would only be correct if the question asked whether the mean "exceeds" or is "greater than" zero.
- B: $$H_1: \mu < 0$$ is one-sided in the opposite direction — appropriate only for wording like "is less than" or "is below" zero.
- D: reporting the mean alone does not tell you whether the difference from zero is statistically meaningful or just random noise. That is exactly what a test decides.

Analogy: "differs from zero" is like asking "is this thermometer reading wrong?" — it could read too high or too low, so you check both directions (two-sided). "Exceeds zero" is like asking "is it hotter than freezing?" — only one direction matters (one-sided).

📌 Exam tip: "Differs / not equal to" → two-sided (1.96 at 5%). "Exceeds / greater / less" → one-sided (1.645 at 5%).

**Q7.** Desk A has 60 days of P&L with mean 1.5 and standard deviation 4. Desk B has 40 days with mean 0.2 and standard deviation 5. Testing whether the two desks have equal mean P&L (two-sided, 5%, critical value 1.96), the z-statistic and decision are closest to:
- A. z = 1.38; fail to reject equal means.
- B. z = 2.10; reject equal means.
- C. z = 0.26; fail to reject equal means.
- D. z = 1.96; reject equal means.

**Answer: A**

The question asks: is the gap between the two desks' average P&L (1.5 vs 0.2) big enough, given the noise, to be statistically real?

Step 1 — The difference of the sample means:
$$\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2 = 1.5 - 0.2 = 1.3$$

Step 2 — The standard error. We add up each desk's variance contribution (variance = standard deviation squared, divided by its sample size):
$$SE = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{4^2}{60} + \frac{5^2}{40}} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{60} + \frac{25}{40}}$$

Compute each piece:
$$\frac{16}{60} = 0.2667, \qquad \frac{25}{40} = 0.6250$$
$$SE = \sqrt{0.2667 + 0.6250} = \sqrt{0.8917} = 0.9443$$

Step 3 — The z-statistic (under $$H_0$$ the true difference is 0):
$$z = \frac{1.3 - 0}{0.9443} = 1.377 \approx 1.38$$

Step 4 — Compare to 1.96. Since $$1.38 < 1.96$$, fail to reject. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B (2.10): you would get this if you forgot to square the standard deviations or mis-added the variances, making the SE too small.
- C (0.26): this comes from dividing 1.3 by 5+4 = 9 or some similar error that hugely overstates the SE.
- D (1.96): this just copies the critical value rather than computing the statistic.

Analogy: the two desks' averages differ by 1.3, but the "wobble" in that difference is about 0.94. Since 1.3 is less than two wobbles away from zero, the gap could easily be luck.

📌 Exam tip: For two means, the SE adds variance contributions: $$\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}$$ — square the SDs, divide by each n, sum, then square-root.

**Q8.** Returns are normally distributed with known $$\sigma = 2$$ and $$n = 16$$, so the standard error is 0.5. A one-sided 5% test of $$H_0: \mu = 0$$ against $$H_1: \mu > 0$$ rejects when $$\bar{X} > 0.8225$$. If the TRUE mean is $$\mu = 1$$, the power of the test is closest to:
- A. 5%
- B. 36%
- C. 64%
- D. 95%

**Answer: C**

The question asks: if the true mean is really 1, what is the chance the test correctly detects it (i.e., rejects $$H_0$$)? That chance is the power.

Power = probability that $$\bar{X}$$ lands in the rejection region (above 0.8225) GIVEN the true mean is 1.

Step 1 — Standardize the rejection threshold using the true mean (1) and the standard error (0.5):
$$Z = \frac{0.8225 - 1}{0.5} = \frac{-0.1775}{0.5} = -0.355$$

Step 2 — Power is the probability that $$Z$$ exceeds $$-0.355$$:
$$\text{Power} = P(Z > -0.355)$$

Because the normal curve is symmetric, $$P(Z > -0.355) = P(Z < 0.355) \approx 0.639$$, i.e., about 64%. The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (5%): that is $$\alpha$$, the Type I error rate — the chance of rejecting when the null is actually true, not the power.
- B (36%): that is $$\beta$$, the Type II error rate ($$1 - 0.64 = 0.36$$) — the chance of MISSING the effect, the opposite of power.
- D (95%): this would be the power only with a much larger sample. With $$n = 16$$ the test is not that powerful.

Analogy: power is how good a metal detector is at finding a coin that is really there. Here it finds the coin about 64% of the time and misses it about 36% of the time.

📌 Exam tip: Power and β are flip sides: power + β = 1. To raise power, increase n, increase the true effect, or accept a larger α.

**Q9.** An analyst screens 20 independent trading signals, all genuinely useless, each tested at the 5% level. The probability that at least one signal appears "significant" by chance is closest to:
- A. 5%
- B. 36%
- C. 64%
- D. 100%

**Answer: C**

The question is about the multiple-testing problem: running many tests inflates the chance of at least one false positive.

Step 1 — For a single test of a true null, the chance of NOT getting a false positive is $$1 - 0.05 = 0.95$$.

Step 2 — For 20 independent tests, the chance that ALL of them correctly stay quiet is:
$$0.95^{20}$$

Compute: $$0.95^{20} = 0.3585$$ (about 36%).

Step 3 — The chance of at least one false positive is the complement:
$$P(\text{at least one rejection}) = 1 - 0.95^{20} = 1 - 0.3585 = 0.6415 \approx 64\%$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (5%): this is the false-positive rate for a SINGLE test. With 20 tests the family-wide rate is much higher — that is the whole point.
- B (36%): this is $$0.95^{20}$$, the probability of ZERO false positives, not at least one. It is the complement of the right answer.
- D (100%): false positives are likely but not certain; the probability is 64%, not a guarantee.

Analogy: buying one lottery ticket gives you a small chance to win. Buy 20 tickets and your chance of winning *at least one* prize climbs a lot — even if each ticket is a long shot. Here "winning" is a false discovery.

📌 Exam tip: Family-wide false-positive rate = $$1 - (1-\alpha)^k$$. Bonferroni fix: test each at $$\alpha/k$$ (here $$0.05/20 = 0.25\%$$).

**Q10.** A VaR model should produce exceptions on 1% of days. Over 500 days a bank observes 9 exceptions ($$\hat{p} = 0.018$$). The two-sided test of $$H_0: p = 0.01$$ gives a p-value of 0.0722. At the 5% level, the correct conclusion is:
- A. Reject $$H_0$$; the model is clearly broken.
- B. Fail to reject $$H_0$$; the exception rate is not statistically distinguishable from 1%.
- C. Reject $$H_0$$; there are too few exceptions.
- D. The p-value is meaningless because only 9 exceptions occurred.

**Answer: B**

The question asks: given a p-value of 0.0722, what do we decide at the 5% significance level?

The decision rule: reject $$H_0$$ only if the p-value is less than $$\alpha$$.
$$\text{Is } 0.0722 < 0.05? \quad \text{No.}$$

Since the p-value (0.0722) is larger than 0.05, we fail to reject. The observed exception rate of 1.8% is higher than the target 1%, but not by a statistically convincing margin. The correct answer is B.

A consistency check via the confidence interval: the 95% CI for $$p$$ is
$$0.018 \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.018 \times 0.982}{500}} = 0.018 \pm 0.0116 = (0.64\%,\ 2.96\%),$$
which contains 1% — so the test and the CI agree (the duality holds).

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: 1.8% looks worse than 1%, but "looks worse" is not the same as "statistically significant." The p-value clears 0.05, so we cannot reject.
- C: there were MORE exceptions (1.8%) than expected (1%), not fewer — the direction is wrong.
- D: the p-value is perfectly valid here; with 500 days and 9 exceptions the test is legitimate.

Analogy: the model misbehaved a little (9 exceptions instead of about 5), but in 500 noisy days that could easily be bad luck rather than a broken model. The evidence is not strong enough to convict.

📌 Exam tip: Compare p-value to α. p = 0.0722 > 0.05 → fail to reject, even when the point estimate "looks" off-target.
