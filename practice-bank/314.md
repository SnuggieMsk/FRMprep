**Q1.** An investor buys a 30-strike call for $3 and sells a 35-strike call for $1 (same expiry). This is a bull call spread. The maximum profit is:
- A. $2
- B. $3
- C. $5
- D. Unlimited

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the maximum profit of a bull call spread.

Step 1 — Net cost (debit):
$$D = 3 - 1 = \$2$$

Step 2 — Maximum payoff occurs when the stock is at or above the higher strike ($K_2 = 35$): the spread pays $K_2 - K_1 = 35 - 30 = \$5$.

Step 3 — Maximum profit = max payoff − debit:
$$5 - 2 = \$3$$

Why B is correct: max profit $= (K_2 - K_1) - D = 5 - 2 = \$3$.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($2) is the net debit (the max LOSS), not the max profit.
- C ($5) is the gross payoff before subtracting the $2 you paid.
- D (Unlimited) describes a plain long call; the short 35-call caps your upside.

Analogy: you buy a cheap upside bet and sell away the part above $35 to lower your cost — your winnings are capped at the $5 spread minus what you paid.

📌 Exam tip: Bull call spread: max profit = $(K_2 - K_1) - D$; max loss = $D$; breakeven = $K_1 + D$.

**Q2.** A box spread with strikes 50 and 55 expires in 1 year; $r = 5\%$ (continuous). Its no-arbitrage price is closest to:
- A. $4.52
- B. $4.76
- C. $5.00
- D. $5.26

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the fair value of a box spread.

A box spread (bull call spread + bear put spread on the same two strikes) always pays exactly $K_2 - K_1$ at expiry, no matter where the stock ends up. So its fair value is the present value of that certain payoff:
$$\text{Price} = (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$$

Compute:
- $K_2 - K_1 = 55 - 50 = 5$
- $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.05 \times 1} = e^{-0.05} = 0.95123$
- Price $= 5 \times 0.95123 = \$4.76$

Why B is correct: the box pays a guaranteed $5, so today it's worth the discounted $5 = $4.76.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($4.52) over-discounts (e.g., using a higher rate or two periods).
- C ($5.00) forgets to discount — that's the future payoff, not today's value.
- D ($5.26) discounts the wrong way (multiplies by $e^{+rT}$ instead of $e^{-rT}$).

Analogy: a box spread is a synthetic zero-coupon bond paying $5 in one year — its price today is just the discounted $5.

📌 Exam tip: Box spread = guaranteed $K_2 - K_1$, so fair value = $(K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$. Works only with European options.

**Q3.** A trader expects a large price move and believes a decline is more likely than a rise. The best-suited strategy is a:
- A. Strap
- B. Strip
- C. Bull put spread
- D. Calendar spread

**Answer: B**

The question asks which strategy fits "big move, downside more likely."

A strip is 1 call + 2 puts (same strike and expiry). It profits from a big move either way, but with double weight on puts it pays more on a DOWN move — perfect when a decline is judged more likely.

Why B is correct: strip = 2 puts + 1 call → bet on a big move tilted to the downside.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (strap = 2 calls + 1 put) tilts to the UPSIDE — the opposite tilt.
- C (bull put spread) is a moderately BULLISH, limited-move strategy, not a big-move bet.
- D (calendar spread) profits when the stock stays NEAR the strike (low movement) — the opposite of expecting a big move.

Analogy: "stri-P" has a P for "Pessimistic" (down). "Stra-P"... think "uP." The extra puts (strip) lean down.

📌 Exam tip: Strip = 2 puts (down-tilted); Strap = 2 calls (up-tilted). Both bet on a big move in either direction.

**Q4.** The profit diagram of a covered call (long stock + short call) most resembles that of a:
- A. Long call
- B. Long put
- C. Short put
- D. Long straddle

**Answer: C**

The question links the covered call to an equivalent single-option profile using put-call parity.

By put-call parity, long stock + short call has the same profit shape as a short put (plus cash). The covered call earns a limited gain (the premium plus a bit of upside to the strike) and loses heavily if the stock falls — just like writing a put.

Why C is correct: parity gives long stock − call = short put + cash, so the profiles match.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (long call) has limited downside and unlimited upside — the opposite shape of a covered call.
- B (long put) profits when the stock falls — a covered call loses when the stock falls.
- D (long straddle) profits from big moves either way; a covered call doesn't.

Analogy: writing a covered call is like being an insurer who collects a premium but is on the hook if things go badly — exactly the payoff of selling a put.

📌 Exam tip: Covered call ≈ short put. Protective put ≈ long call. These come straight from put-call parity.

**Q5.** A bear put spread is built by buying a 50-strike put for $5 and selling a 45-strike put for $2 (same expiry). The breakeven stock price at expiration is:
- A. $42
- B. $45
- C. $47
- D. $50

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the breakeven of a bear put spread.

Net debit:
$$D = 5 - 2 = \$3$$

For a bear put spread, breakeven = higher strike − debit:
$$K_2 - D = 50 - 3 = \$47$$

Check: at $S_T = 47$, the long 50-put pays $50 - 47 = 3$, the short 45-put pays 0, total payoff $= 3$, minus the $3 debit = $0 profit. Confirmed.

Why C is correct: breakeven is where the spread's payoff just covers the $3 you paid, at $47.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($42) is below the lower strike, where you're already at max profit, not breakeven.
- B ($45) is the lower strike, where payoff = $5 (max profit), not breakeven.
- D ($50) is the higher strike, where the payoff is $0 and you're at max loss (−$3), not breakeven.

Analogy: you need the stock to fall just $3 below $50 to recover your $3 cost — breakeven at $47.

📌 Exam tip: Bear put spread: max profit = $(K_2 - K_1) - D$; max loss = $D$; breakeven = $K_2 - D$.

**Q6.** A long straddle is created by buying a 100-strike call for $6 and a 100-strike put for $4. The two breakeven stock prices at expiration are:
- A. $94 and $104
- B. $90 and $110
- C. $96 and $106
- D. $100 only

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the breakevens of a long straddle.

Total premium paid:
$$6 + 4 = \$10$$

A straddle breaks even when the move (up or down from the strike) equals the total premium:
- Lower breakeven: $K - \text{premium} = 100 - 10 = \$90$
- Upper breakeven: $K + \text{premium} = 100 + 10 = \$110$

Why B is correct: breakevens are $K \pm$ total premium = $90 and $110.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($94/$104) uses ±$6 or ±$4 (one premium) instead of the total $10.
- C ($96/$106) uses ±$4 / ±$6 inconsistently — still not the total premium.
- D ($100 only) is the strike, where the straddle has its MAXIMUM LOSS (−$10), not breakeven.

Analogy: you paid $10 for the bet, so the stock must move at least $10 in either direction before you start profiting — break even at $90 and $110.

📌 Exam tip: Long straddle breakevens = $K \pm$ (total premium). Max loss = total premium, at $S_T = K$.

**Q7.** A protective put combines long stock with a long put. Its profit profile most resembles a:
- A. Long call
- B. Short call
- C. Short put
- D. Short straddle

**Answer: A**

The question links the protective put to a single-option equivalent via parity.

By put-call parity, long stock + long put = long call + cash. The protective put has limited downside (the put floors your loss) and keeps unlimited upside (the stock can rise) — exactly the shape of a long call.

Why A is correct: parity gives stock + put = call + cash, so the profit shape matches a long call.

Why the others are wrong:
- B (short call) has capped upside and unlimited downside — the opposite of a protective put.
- C (short put) loses big on the downside; a protective put is PROTECTED on the downside.
- D (short straddle) loses on big moves either way; a protective put gains on big up-moves.

Analogy: buying insurance on your stock (the put) caps your losses but leaves your gains open — just like owning a call.

📌 Exam tip: Protective put ≈ long call (downside protected, upside open). Covered call ≈ short put.

**Q8.** A principal-protected note (PPN) is hardest to create profitably when:
- A. Interest rates are high and volatility is low
- B. Interest rates are low and/or volatility is high
- C. The stock pays no dividends
- D. The note has a very short maturity

**Answer: B**

The question asks the conditions that make PPNs difficult to build.

A PPN = a zero-coupon bond (to return the principal) + a call option (for upside). When interest rates are LOW, the zero-coupon bond costs almost the full principal, leaving little money to buy the option. When volatility is HIGH, the call option is expensive. Either way, there's not enough left over for meaningful upside.

Why B is correct: low rates (expensive zero) and/or high volatility (expensive option) leave too little budget for the upside option.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the opposite — high rates make the zero cheap (more budget for the option), and low volatility makes the option cheap. That's the EASIEST case.
- C: dividends affect option pricing somewhat, but the binding constraints are rates and volatility.
- D: a short maturity doesn't make PPNs hard per se; the rate/volatility budget squeeze is the key issue.

Analogy: your fixed budget must first buy a "safety bond." If that safety eats almost all the money (low rates) or the fun "upside ticket" is pricey (high volatility), you can't afford much upside.

📌 Exam tip: PPN = zero-coupon bond + call. Low rates and high volatility both shrink the option budget, making PPNs hard to create.

**Q9.** Which strategy is essentially a bet that volatility will be LOW and the stock will stay near a central strike $K_2$?
- A. Long straddle
- B. Long strangle
- C. Long butterfly
- D. Strip

**Answer: C**

The question asks which strategy profits when the stock stays put (low volatility).

A long butterfly (buy calls at $K_1$ and $K_3$, sell 2 calls at $K_2$) makes its maximum profit when the stock ends right at the middle strike $K_2$. It is a "short volatility" bet with small, capped risk in either direction.

Why C is correct: the butterfly pays the most if the stock stays near $K_2$ — a low-volatility bet.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (long straddle) profits from a BIG move either way — that's LONG volatility, the opposite.
- B (long strangle) also bets on a big move — long volatility.
- D (strip) bets on a big move (down-tilted) — long volatility.

Analogy: a butterfly is like betting the dart lands dead center on the board — you win most if nothing much moves.

📌 Exam tip: Butterfly = low-volatility bet (stock stays near the middle strike). Straddle/strangle/strip/strap = high-volatility bets.

**Q10.** A zero-cost collar on a stock position is built by:
- A. Buying a call and buying a put at the same strike
- B. Holding the stock, buying a protective put at $K_1$, and selling a call at $K_2$ so the premiums net to zero
- C. Buying two puts and selling one call
- D. Selling the stock and buying a bond

**Answer: B**

The question asks how a zero-cost collar is constructed.

A collar protects a long stock position by buying a downside put (strike $K_1$, the floor) and financing it by selling an upside call (strike $K_2$, the cap). When the strikes are chosen so the call premium received equals the put premium paid, the collar costs nothing up front — hence "zero-cost." Your outcome is locked into the band $[K_1, K_2]$.

Why B is correct: long stock + long put $K_1$ + short call $K_2$, premiums netting to zero, locks profit into a band.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (buy call + buy put, same strike) is a long straddle, not a collar.
- C (2 puts + 1 call) is a strip, a volatility bet, not a protective collar.
- D (sell stock + buy bond) just exits the position; it's not a collar.

Analogy: a collar puts a floor (put) under your losses and a ceiling (call) on your gains, with the ceiling "paying for" the floor — so it's free.

📌 Exam tip: Zero-cost collar = long stock + long put ($K_1$ floor) + short call ($K_2$ cap), premiums net to zero. Locks the outcome into $[K_1, K_2]$.
