**Q1.** The defining feature of reverse stress testing is that it:
- A. Uses inverted (downward-sloping) yield curves as the scenario.
- B. Starts from a failure outcome and works backward to find scenarios that would cause it.
- C. Applies historical scenarios in reverse chronological order.
- D. Stresses only the firm's liabilities.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what makes reverse stress testing different from a normal stress test.

A normal stress test goes forward: pick a scenario, then calculate the loss. Reverse stress testing flips this around. You START with a bad outcome - "what would make us fail or breach our capital minimum?" - and then search BACKWARD for the scenarios that could produce that outcome.

Analogy: Normal stress testing asks "if a storm hits, how much damage?" Reverse stress testing asks "what kind of storm would actually sink our ship?" and then hunts for that storm.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: "Inverted" refers to yield-curve shape, which has nothing to do with the meaning of "reverse" here.
- C: It's not about running history in reverse order; it's about starting from the outcome.
- D: It can involve all risks (assets, liabilities, off-balance-sheet), not only liabilities.

📌 Exam tip: Reverse stress test = outcome-first. Start with "what breaks us?" and work back to the scenario. Regulators require it for recovery and resolution planning.

**Q2.** Compared with VaR, a key advantage of stress testing is that it:
- A. Attaches precise probabilities to extreme events.
- B. Is restricted to a one-day horizon.
- C. Can capture events that are absent from the historical sample.
- D. Requires returns to be normally distributed.

**Answer: C**

The question asks for stress testing's main advantage over VaR.

VaR is built from recent historical data, so it is blind to events that never appeared in that data window (this is exactly why VaR failed in 2007 - the prior period had been calm). Stress testing is not limited by history: you can imagine and test brand-new, unprecedented scenarios. The price you pay is that stress tests usually carry no probability attached.

Analogy: VaR is like predicting tomorrow's weather only from last month's records. Stress testing lets you ask "what if a hurricane we've never seen before hits?" - even if it isn't in the records.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: This is the opposite - stress tests generally do NOT attach probabilities (that's VaR's domain).
- B: VaR is the short-horizon tool (1-10 days for market risk); stress tests often run over quarters or years.
- D: Stress testing doesn't require normality; if anything it's used precisely because real tails aren't normal.

📌 Exam tip: Stress testing's superpower is capturing the unprecedented; its cost is losing probability statements. VaR and stress testing are COMPLEMENTS, not substitutes.

**Q3.** For a hypothetical stress scenario to be "coherent," it must:
- A. Shock only one risk factor at a time.
- B. Have all shocked variables move in an internally consistent way.
- C. Match a past historical episode exactly.
- D. Be approved by the firm's external auditors.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what "coherent" means for a hypothetical scenario.

A coherent scenario is one where all the variables move together in a way that actually makes sense. You can't, for example, shock equities down 40% while leaving credit spreads and volatility unchanged - in a real crash, spreads widen and volatility spikes too. So the shocked variables must be internally consistent with each other.

Analogy: If you imagine a heavy rainstorm, you can't also assume the streets stay perfectly dry. The pieces of the story have to fit together.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: Shocking one variable alone usually makes a scenario INCOHERENT, because related variables would move too.
- C: Hypothetical scenarios are made up (forward-looking); they don't need to match history.
- D: External auditor approval is not what coherence means.

📌 Exam tip: Coherence = variables co-move plausibly (equities down -> spreads wider, volatility higher). A common way to achieve it is to shock core variables and set the rest via estimated conditional relationships.

**Q4.** Under the US CCAR program, the supervisory stress test results primarily constrain:
- A. Banks' trading-desk VaR limits.
- B. Capital distributions (dividends and buybacks) via projected post-stress capital ratios.
- C. The interest rates banks pay on deposits.
- D. The pricing of individual loans.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what CCAR results actually control.

In CCAR, the Federal Reserve projects a bank's revenues, losses, and capital ratios over a 9-quarter horizon under severely adverse scenarios. If a bank's projected post-stress capital ratio is too weak, its ability to pay dividends and do share buybacks is restricted (this links to the stress capital buffer).

Analogy: It's like a budget check before a family books a vacation: if the projection shows you'd run out of savings in a bad year, you're told to cut back on spending (distributions) first.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: CCAR is about overall capital adequacy, not setting individual trading-desk VaR limits.
- C: Deposit rates are not gated by CCAR.
- D: Loan pricing is a business decision, not the direct output CCAR constrains.

📌 Exam tip: CCAR/DFAST -> projected capital ratios under adverse scenarios -> gate dividends and buybacks (stress capital buffer). Capital distributions is the key phrase.

**Q5.** Which of the following statements about the relationship between VaR and stress testing is TRUE?
- A. Stress testing replaces VaR.
- B. VaR and stress testing are complements that answer different questions.
- C. Stress tests always assign probabilities to scenarios.
- D. Historical scenarios alone are sufficient to capture all future risks.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the correct view of how VaR and stress testing relate.

They are complements. VaR answers "how bad could things get over a typical period at a given probability?" using recent data. Stress testing answers "what happens in THIS specific extreme scenario?" without a probability. Each covers the other's blind spot: VaR backtests well but misses anything outside its data; stress testing probes the tail but carries no likelihood.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: False - stress testing does NOT replace VaR; banks use both.
- C: False - stress tests generally do NOT assign probabilities to scenarios. This is a classic GARP trap statement.
- D: False - historical scenarios miss novel risks (crypto, climate, new instruments) that never appeared in the past.

📌 Exam tip: Memorize the false trap statements GARP loves: "stress tests assign probabilities," "stress testing replaces VaR," "historical scenarios are sufficient," "a more severe scenario is always better." All FALSE.

**Q6.** A "stressed VaR" measure is best described as:
- A. A scenario loss with no probability attached.
- B. VaR or ES computed using parameters (volatilities, correlations) calibrated to a crisis period.
- C. A VaR measure that ignores correlations entirely.
- D. A reverse stress test.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what stressed VaR means.

Stressed VaR takes the normal VaR (or ES) machinery but feeds it inputs - volatilities and correlations - calibrated to a crisis window such as 2008, instead of recent calm data. This blends the two worlds: a probabilistic measure (VaR) computed with stressed inputs. Basel 2.5 added stressed VaR, and FRTB's expected shortfall is calibrated to the worst observed period.

Analogy: It's like calculating your monthly budget risk, but using prices from the worst recession year instead of today's calm prices - same calculation, harsher inputs.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: A scenario loss with no probability is a plain stress test, not stressed VaR (which keeps the probability framework).
- C: Stressed VaR still uses correlations - in fact crisis correlations are a key stressed input.
- D: Reverse stress testing is a completely different concept (outcome-first).

📌 Exam tip: Stressed VaR = the VaR/ES formula run on crisis-calibrated parameters. It is a hybrid that puts stressed inputs into a probabilistic measure.

**Q7.** Which of the following is a recognized advantage of HISTORICAL stress scenarios over hypothetical ones?
- A. They are guaranteed to capture future, never-seen risks.
- B. They are internally coherent because the co-movements actually happened.
- C. They require no data.
- D. They always produce larger losses than hypothetical scenarios.

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a genuine advantage of historical scenarios.

Historical scenarios replay real crises (1987, 1998, 2008, 2020). Their big advantage is that the variables moved together in a way that genuinely occurred, so the scenario is automatically coherent and objective - you don't have to guess how equities, spreads, and volatility should co-move; the data already shows you.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: This is actually a WEAKNESS of historical scenarios - they miss new risks (crypto, climate) that weren't in past crises. "The next crisis is never the last one."
- C: They require historical data to replay.
- D: There is no fixed rule that historical losses exceed hypothetical ones; severity depends on the scenario.

📌 Exam tip: Historical scenarios = coherent and objective (it really happened) BUT may miss novel risks. Hypothetical scenarios = forward-looking and tailored BUT subjective and hard to keep coherent.

**Q8.** The "severity versus plausibility" trade-off in scenario design means that a scenario should be:
- A. As extreme as possible regardless of realism.
- B. Severe but plausible - not so mild it gives false comfort, nor so wild that management dismisses it.
- C. Always identical to a past crisis.
- D. Mild enough to always pass the test.

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the severity-plausibility trade-off.

A good scenario must strike a balance. If it is too mild, it gives false comfort - you think you're safe when you aren't. If it is too wild and unrealistic, management dismisses it as nonsense and it distorts capital allocation. The sweet spot is "severe but plausible."

Analogy: A fire drill should be realistic enough to test you, but you don't simulate a meteor strike that wipes out the whole city - nobody would take the lessons seriously.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: "As extreme as possible regardless of realism" leads to dismissal - too wild is a known failure.
- C: It doesn't have to match a past crisis; hypothetical scenarios are also valid.
- D: Designing a scenario just to pass is exactly the governance failure ("scenarios watered down to pass") that regulators warn against.

📌 Exam tip: Severe BUT plausible. Too mild = false comfort; too extreme = ignored. GARP flags "a more severe scenario is always better" as FALSE.

**Q9.** Why are "knock-on" or second-round effects (such as fire sales and funding runs) important to include in stress scenarios?
- A. They are required by accounting standards.
- B. They made 2008 far worse than the first-round losses alone, so ignoring them understates the true impact.
- C. They reduce the severity of the scenario.
- D. They only matter for liquidity, never for capital.

**Answer: B**

The question asks why second-round effects matter.

In 2008, the initial (first-round) losses on subprime assets were bad, but what made the crisis catastrophic were the knock-on effects: forced fire sales pushed prices down further, funding runs dried up cash, and counterparty failures spread the damage. A scenario that captures only first-round losses will badly understate how bad things can really get.

Analogy: One domino falling is a small loss; but if it knocks over the next, and the next, the total damage is far bigger. Good scenarios model the whole chain, not just the first domino.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: It's a risk-management best practice, not an accounting-standard requirement.
- C: They INCREASE severity (make losses bigger), not reduce it.
- D: They matter for both - fire sales and funding runs hit liquidity AND capital.

📌 Exam tip: Good scenarios include second-round/feedback effects (fire sales, funding runs, counterparty failures). Ignoring them understates the true loss.

**Q10.** Regarding the ordering of a 99% Expected Shortfall (ES) and a stress-test loss for the same portfolio, which statement is correct?
- A. The stress loss is always larger than the 99% ES.
- B. The 99% ES is always larger than the stress loss.
- C. There is no fixed ordering - the stress loss can be larger or smaller than the ES because they answer different questions.
- D. They are always exactly equal.

**Answer: C**

The question asks how a 99% ES and a stress loss compare.

These two numbers measure different things. ES is a probability-based average of losses beyond the 99% quantile, drawn from a distribution. A stress loss is the outcome of one specific imagined scenario, with no probability attached. Because they answer different questions, there is no guaranteed ordering - the stress loss might come out bigger OR smaller than the 99% ES depending on the scenario chosen.

Analogy: Comparing "the average of my worst 1% of commute times" (ES) with "how long my commute would be if a specific bridge collapsed" (stress) - one isn't always bigger than the other; it depends on the scenario.

Why the others are wrong:
- A and B: Both claim a fixed ordering, which is false - there is no guaranteed relationship.
- D: They are not equal by construction; they're different measures.

📌 Exam tip: A stress loss can exceed OR fall short of a given ES. No fixed ordering - they answer different questions. This is a favorite GARP trap.
