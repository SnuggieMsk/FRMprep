**Q1.** A 6-month European call on a non-dividend-paying stock trades at $4.00, with $S_0 = \$50$, $K = \$50$, and $r = 5\%$ (continuous). By put-call parity, the European put price is closest to:
- A. $2.16
- B. $2.77
- C. $4.00
- D. $5.23

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to find the put price from the call price using put-call parity.

Put-call parity (European, no dividends):
$$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0$$

Plug in ($T = 0.5$):
- $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} = e^{-0.025} = 0.97531$
- $Ke^{-rT} = 50 \times 0.97531 = 48.7655$
- $p = 4.00 + 48.7655 - 50 = 2.7655 \approx \$2.77$

Why B is correct: it applies parity directly with the discounted strike.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($2.16) likely discounted the strike with the wrong rate or didn't discount at all consistently.
- C ($4.00) just copies the call price — wrong, parity adjusts for the discounted strike vs spot.
- D ($5.23) added $S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ instead of subtracting, flipping a sign.

Analogy: parity is a seesaw — a call plus cash balances a put plus stock. Rearrange to find the missing weight.

📌 Exam tip: Parity holds for EUROPEAN options. Memorize $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$ and just solve for the unknown.

**Q2.** An American call on a non-dividend-paying stock is deep in the money with three months left. A holder who no longer wants the exposure should:
- A. Exercise immediately to capture the intrinsic value
- B. Sell the call in the market
- C. Exercise and immediately short the stock
- D. Hold to expiration no matter what

**Answer: B**

The question tests the rule that an American call on a non-dividend stock should never be exercised early.

The call alive is worth at least $C \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$, which exceeds the exercise value $S_0 - K$ (because $Ke^{-rT} < K$). So selling captures both intrinsic value AND remaining time value, while exercising throws the time value away.

Why B is correct: selling realizes the full market value (intrinsic + time value); exercising only gets intrinsic value.

Why the others are wrong:
- A exercises early and forfeits time value — never optimal for a call on a non-dividend stock.
- C is just exercise (capturing intrinsic only) plus an unnecessary short — still wastes time value.
- D ignores that the holder no longer wants the position; selling is cleaner and captures value now.

Analogy: a coupon worth more if resold than if used — you'd sell it, not "spend" it early.

📌 Exam tip: Never exercise an American call on a non-dividend-paying stock early; sell it instead. $C = c$ in this case.

**Q3.** Which change, all else equal, DECREASES the value of a European put?
- A. A higher strike price
- B. Higher volatility
- C. A higher risk-free rate
- D. Higher expected dividends

**Answer: C**

The question tests the sign of each factor's effect on a European put.

A higher risk-free rate lowers the present value of the strike the put holder would receive on exercise, which hurts the put. (Higher rates help calls, hurt puts.)

Why C is correct: higher $r$ reduces the PV of the strike received, decreasing put value.

Why the others are wrong (all INCREASE a put's value):
- A: a higher strike means the put can sell at a higher price — more valuable.
- B: higher volatility raises the value of all options (more chance of big moves in your favor).
- D: dividends push the stock price down on the ex-date, which RAISES put values.

Analogy: a put is insurance to sell at a fixed price. Higher interest rates make the promised future cash worth less today, so the insurance is worth a bit less.

📌 Exam tip: For puts: $S_0\uparrow$ bad, $K\uparrow$ good, $\sigma\uparrow$ good, $r\uparrow$ bad, dividends$\uparrow$ good. Only higher $r$ decreases the put here.

**Q4.** With $S_0 = \$37$, $K = \$40$, $r = 5\%$ (continuous), and $T = 0.5$, the lower bound on a European put (non-dividend stock) is closest to:
- A. $0.00
- B. $1.01
- C. $2.01
- D. $3.00

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the no-arbitrage lower bound of a European put.

Lower bound:
$$p \ge \max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\ 0)$$

Compute:
- $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} = e^{-0.025} = 0.97531$
- $Ke^{-rT} = 40 \times 0.97531 = 39.0124$
- $Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 39.0124 - 37 = 2.0124$
- $\max(2.0124, 0) = \$2.01$

Why C is correct: discount the strike, subtract spot, and take the max with zero.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($0.00) would apply if the discounted strike were below spot; here it's above, so the bound is positive.
- B ($1.01) under-discounts or uses the wrong rate/time.
- D ($3.00) uses the undiscounted strike: $40 - 37 = 3$, forgetting to discount $K$.

Analogy: the least a put can be worth is the discounted "sell price" minus today's stock price — anything less would be free money for an arbitrageur.

📌 Exam tip: European put lower bound = $\max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0, 0)$. Always discount the strike.

**Q5.** Put-call parity in the form $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$ applies to:
- A. American options only
- B. European options only
- C. Both American and European options
- D. Only options on dividend-paying stocks

**Answer: B**

The question tests the scope of the put-call parity equation.

The exact equality holds only for EUROPEAN options. For American options, early exercise possibilities mean we can only bound the relationship ($C - P$), not pin it down exactly.

Why B is correct: parity as an equation is a European-only result.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the opposite — American options give only an inequality.
- C is wrong because American options don't satisfy the exact equality.
- D is irrelevant — parity has a dividend-adjusted version, but the European requirement is the key restriction.

Analogy: parity is like a perfectly balanced scale that only stays balanced when nobody can remove a weight early — early exercise (American) lets someone tip it.

📌 Exam tip: Parity equation = European only. For American options you get the inequality $S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$.

**Q6.** A 1-year European call on a non-dividend stock trades at $3.00, with $S_0 = \$20$, $K = \$18$, $r = 10\%$ (continuous). Is there an arbitrage, and what is the call's lower bound?
- A. No arbitrage; lower bound is $2.00
- B. Yes; the lower bound is about $3.71, so the call is too cheap
- C. Yes; the lower bound is about $3.71, so the call is too expensive
- D. No arbitrage; lower bound is $0.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks whether the call price violates its lower bound.

Lower bound for a European call (no dividends):
$$c \ge \max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},\ 0)$$

Compute:
- $e^{-rT} = e^{-0.10 \times 1} = e^{-0.10} = 0.90484$
- $Ke^{-rT} = 18 \times 0.90484 = 16.2871$
- $S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 20 - 16.2871 = 3.7129$

The lower bound is about $3.71, but the call trades at only $3.00. Since $3.00 < 3.71$, the bound is violated — the call is TOO CHEAP, so an arbitrage exists (buy the call, short the stock).

Why B is correct: the bound is $3.71, the price $3.00 is below it, so the call is underpriced — arbitrage exists.

Why the others are wrong:
- A uses the undiscounted bound ($20 - 18 = 2$) and misses the violation.
- C gets the bound right but says "too expensive" — it's too cheap (price below bound).
- D claims the bound is zero, ignoring that $S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ is clearly positive here.

Analogy: the call can't cost less than buying the stock now but paying the discounted strike later. If it does, you grab it for the free profit.

📌 Exam tip: If the market price is BELOW the lower bound, the option is too cheap — buy it. Always discount the strike in the bound.

**Q7.** Higher dividends, all else equal, have which effect on European calls and puts?
- A. Raise both calls and puts
- B. Lower both calls and puts
- C. Lower calls and raise puts
- D. Raise calls and lower puts

**Answer: C**

The question tests the dividend effect on option values.

When a stock goes ex-dividend, the stock price drops by roughly the dividend. A lower stock price is bad for calls (they want a high stock price) and good for puts (they want a low stock price). So higher dividends LOWER calls and RAISE puts.

Why C is correct: dividends transfer value out of the stock, hurting calls and helping puts.

Why the others are wrong:
- A and B treat calls and puts the same — but they react oppositely to the stock price.
- D reverses the effect — that would be the effect of a higher stock price or higher rates.

Analogy: a dividend is like the company handing cash out the back door — share price drops, so the "right to buy" (call) is worth less and the "right to sell" (put) is worth more.

📌 Exam tip: Dividends ↓ calls, ↑ puts. In parity, $D$ is added to the call side: $c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$.

**Q8.** For an American option on a non-dividend-paying stock, early exercise of a call is:
- A. Always optimal when deep in the money
- B. Never optimal; the call is worth more alive than exercised
- C. Optimal only when interest rates are negative
- D. Required at expiration

**Answer: B**

The question tests the early-exercise rule for American calls (no dividends).

Early exercise of an American call on a non-dividend stock is never optimal. Exercising pays $K$ now (losing interest on $K$) and throws away the downside insurance the option provides. Because $C \ge c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} > S_0 - K$, the call is always worth more alive than exercised.

Why B is correct: it states the rule and the reason — the call is worth more unexercised.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the trap — "deep ITM, just exercise." Even deep ITM, you should sell rather than exercise early.
- C: negative rates aren't the trigger; the rule is about forgone interest and insurance, and it holds for positive rates.
- D is about expiration mechanics, not early exercise — and even at expiration you'd sell or let auto-exercise handle it.

Analogy: holding the call keeps a safety net under you if the stock crashes. Exercising early cuts the net away for no benefit.

📌 Exam tip: American call on a non-dividend stock → never exercise early, so $C = c$. Dividends are the only thing that can make early call exercise worthwhile (just before ex-date).

**Q9.** Early exercise of an American PUT is most attractive when:
- A. The stock price is low, the risk-free rate is high, and volatility is low
- B. The stock price is high and volatility is high
- C. The risk-free rate is zero
- D. The option is far out of the money

**Answer: A**

The question asks the conditions favoring early exercise of an American put.

Early exercise of a put gives you $K$ in cash now, which earns interest. This is most worthwhile when: the put is deep in the money (low stock price, so little upside left to lose), the risk-free rate is high (interest on $K$ received early is valuable), and volatility is low (little chance the stock recovers, so little time value to give up).

Why A is correct: low $S$, high $r$, low $\sigma$ together maximize the benefit of receiving $K$ early.

Why the others are wrong:
- B is the opposite — a high stock price means the put is OTM (no reason to exercise), and high volatility means lots of time value worth keeping.
- C: a zero rate removes the interest benefit of receiving $K$ early — that discourages early exercise.
- D: an OTM put has zero intrinsic value, so there's nothing to gain by exercising.

Analogy: exercising the put is like cashing a guaranteed check early to start earning interest — most attractive when the price can't realistically improve and interest is high.

📌 Exam tip: American puts CAN be exercised early (unlike non-dividend calls). Favorable conditions: deep ITM, high $r$, low $\sigma$.

**Q10.** All else equal, an increase in the time to expiration $T$ for an American call:
- A. Always decreases its value
- B. Has an ambiguous effect, like European options
- C. Never decreases its value
- D. Only matters if there are dividends

**Answer: C**

The question tests the effect of more time on an American option's value.

A longer-dated American option contains every right of the shorter-dated one, plus more time to exercise. So more time can never make it worth less — its value never decreases with $T$. (For European options the effect can be ambiguous because they can only be exercised at the end, and dividends or deep-ITM puts can complicate it.)

Why C is correct: an American option with more time has strictly more flexibility, so its value never falls with $T$.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is wrong — more time can only help or stay the same for an American option.
- B describes EUROPEAN options, whose time effect is ambiguous; American options are clear-cut.
- D is false — the "never decreases" rule for American options holds regardless of dividends.

Analogy: a longer "anytime" pass includes all the days of a shorter one plus extra days — it can only be at least as useful.

📌 Exam tip: American option value never decreases with more time. European time effect is ambiguous (marked "?" in the factor table).
