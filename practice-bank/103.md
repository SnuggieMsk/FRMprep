**Q1.** Which of the following is a responsibility of the board of directors rather than of management?
- A. Setting individual desk-level VaR limits
- B. Approving the firm-wide risk appetite statement
- C. Designing the format of the daily risk report
- D. Approving individual large loan applications

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to separate board-level duties (oversight, big-picture) from management duties (running things day to day). Approving the firm-wide risk appetite statement is a core board responsibility because appetite is a strategic, firm-level decision.

Think of a board like the owners of a restaurant who decide "we serve seafood and tolerate this much financial risk," while the chef and managers handle the daily cooking and staffing.

- A is wrong: setting specific desk-level VaR limits is a granular management/risk-function task that flows down from appetite.
- C is wrong: designing the daily risk report is an operational task for the risk function, not the board.
- D is wrong: approving individual large loans is a management/credit-committee decision, not a board governance act.

📌 Exam tip: The board sets appetite, approves strategy and key policies, and monitors — it does NOT run the risk function. Granular limits, reports, and individual deals are management tasks.

**Q2.** In the three-lines-of-defense model, who performs independent measurement and challenge of the risk taken by the business lines?
- A. The business lines themselves
- B. The risk management and compliance function
- C. Internal audit
- D. The external auditor

**Answer: B**

The question maps each "line of defense" to its job. The second line — risk management and compliance — sets the framework, measures risk independently, monitors, and challenges the business lines' risk-taking.

Picture a sports team: the players (first line) take the risks on the field; the coaching/analytics staff (second line) independently measure performance and push back; the league referees/auditors (third line) check that the whole system runs as designed.

- A is wrong: the business lines (first line) *own* and generate the risk; they cannot be the independent check on themselves.
- C is wrong: internal audit (third line) assures that the first and second lines work as designed — it audits the system, it doesn't do the day-to-day independent risk measurement.
- D is wrong: the external auditor focuses on the financial statements, not on continuous independent risk measurement and challenge.

📌 Exam tip: 1st line = business owns the risk; 2nd line = risk/compliance measures and challenges; 3rd line = internal audit assures the framework. Watch for traps that give first-line duties to the CRO or make audit a control *designer*.

**Q3.** Which post-crisis governance change is correctly paired with its purpose?
- A. Volcker rule — to increase banks' proprietary trading capacity
- B. Compensation clawbacks — to align pay with long-run, risk-adjusted outcomes
- C. Basel III leverage ratio — to completely replace risk-based capital
- D. Board risk committees — to take over day-to-day risk management

**Answer: B**

The question tests whether you know what each reform was meant to do. Clawback (and deferral/malus) provisions let firms recover or withhold pay when risks taken earlier turn sour, so they align compensation with multi-period, risk-adjusted results.

- A is wrong: the Volcker rule *restricts* proprietary trading, it does not increase it.
- C is wrong: the leverage ratio is a *backstop* alongside risk-based capital, not a replacement for it.
- D is wrong: board risk committees provide *oversight*; they do not run risk management day to day (that is management's job).

📌 Exam tip: Post-crisis fixes — Volcker (limit prop trading), Basel III (more/better capital + leverage ratio + liquidity LCR/NSFR), board risk committees (oversight), CRO elevation (independence), and clawbacks/deferral (align incentives).

**Q4.** The primary responsibility of a firm's audit committee is to:
- A. Set the firm's risk appetite
- B. Manage internal audit's day-to-day testing work
- C. Oversee the integrity of financial reporting and the control environment
- D. Approve new trading products

**Answer: C**

The question asks what the audit committee mainly owns. It is a board committee of independent, financially literate directors whose core job is to oversee the integrity of financial reporting, the external audit relationship, internal audit, and the effectiveness of internal controls.

- A is wrong: setting risk appetite is owned by the board/risk committee, not the audit committee.
- B is wrong: the audit committee *oversees* internal audit (approves its plan, protects its independence) but does not *run* its day-to-day testing.
- D is wrong: approving new trading products is a management/risk function task, not an audit committee role.

📌 Exam tip: Audit committee = financial-reporting integrity, internal controls, and oversight of internal/external audit. Risk committee = risk appetite oversight and forward-looking risk. Know which committee owns which duty.

**Q5.** Best-practice corporate governance suggests that the board should approve:
- A. Business strategy and risk appetite together, because strategy is itself a risk decision
- B. Risk appetite only after strategy has been fully executed
- C. Strategy without regard to risk appetite, to avoid limiting growth
- D. Only the compensation plan, leaving strategy to the CEO alone

**Answer: A**

The question asks how strategy and risk appetite should relate at board level. Best practice is to set them jointly: a growth strategy implies risk consequences (new markets, products, leverage), and the appetite constraint shapes which strategies are even feasible. Treating them separately invites a dangerous disconnect.

It is like planning a road trip and deciding your fuel budget at the same time — choosing the route without considering fuel will strand you.

- B is wrong: setting appetite only *after* executing strategy is backwards — by then the risks are already taken.
- C is wrong: ignoring appetite to chase growth is exactly the disconnect that gets "resolved on the trading floor in favor of growth" — a governance failure.
- D is wrong: the board cannot abdicate strategy entirely to the CEO; strategy and appetite are core board matters.

📌 Exam tip: Strategy IS a risk decision — the board approves strategy and risk appetite jointly. A conservative stated appetite with aggressive growth targets is a red flag.

**Q6.** Why are incentives described as the "transmission belt" of risk governance?
- A. Because limits cannot be cascaded without incentives
- B. Because if pay rewards short-term, risk-blind P&L, employees will push the risk profile beyond appetite regardless of written policy
- C. Because incentives replace the need for a risk function
- D. Because incentives determine the firm's risk capacity

**Answer: B**

The question asks why incentives matter so much in governance. No matter what the policy documents say, people respond to how they are paid. If bonuses reward revenue or short-term profit with no charge for risk, staff will take more risk than the firm's appetite allows. So incentives effectively "transmit" behavior down to the desk.

Think of a delivery company that pays drivers only for speed: no memo about "drive safely" will stop reckless driving until the pay scheme changes.

- A is wrong: limits are cascaded through the limit framework and delegated authorities, not "through incentives."
- C is wrong: incentives complement, they do not replace, the risk function.
- D is wrong: risk capacity is set by capital, liquidity, and franchise — not by the incentive scheme.

📌 Exam tip: Fix incentives with risk-adjusted performance measures (e.g., RAROC), deferred comp, clawbacks, and risk-function input into pay. Misaligned pay overrides policy every time.

**Q7.** A clear risk appetite statement is cascaded down to individual desks primarily through:
- A. The external audit report
- B. The limit framework (limits by business, desk, and risk type)
- C. The annual shareholder meeting
- D. The firm's marketing strategy

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the mechanism that translates a high-level, slow-moving appetite into concrete, granular constraints at the desk. That bridge is the limit framework — cascading limits by business line, desk, and risk type.

Appetite is like a household saying "we'll spend responsibly"; the limit framework is the actual monthly budget broken down into categories that each person must follow.

- A is wrong: the external audit report addresses financial statements, not cascading appetite into desk limits.
- C is wrong: a shareholder meeting is a governance event, not a transmission mechanism for limits.
- D is wrong: marketing strategy is unrelated to risk transmission.

📌 Exam tip: Appetite statement → limit framework is the key translation layer. Other transmission mechanisms include committees (ALCO, credit, op-risk), policies, delegated authorities, and the three lines of defense.

**Q8.** Internal audit (the third line of defense) should report primarily to:
- A. The Chief Executive Officer
- B. The head of the trading desk
- C. The board's audit committee
- D. The Chief Risk Officer

**Answer: C**

The question tests internal audit's reporting line, which exists to protect its independence. Internal audit reports to the board's audit committee so it can objectively assure that the first and second lines work as designed, without being controlled by the people it audits.

- A is wrong: reporting to the CEO would compromise independence, since the CEO runs the business audit is assessing.
- B is wrong: a trading desk head is part of the first line audit reviews — clearly a conflict.
- D is wrong: the CRO leads the second line; if audit reported to the CRO it could not independently assure the second line's own work.

📌 Exam tip: Internal audit (3rd line) reports to the audit committee to stay independent of management and of the risk function it assures. Independence is the whole point.

**Q9.** The role of the board in risk governance is best summarized as:
- A. Day-to-day management of the risk function
- B. Oversight — setting appetite, approving strategy and key policies, ensuring capable management and sound incentives, and monitoring
- C. Personally measuring and reporting the firm's daily VaR
- D. Approving every individual trade above a threshold

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the correct characterization of the board's job. The board provides oversight: it sets risk appetite, approves strategy and major policies, ensures there is a credible CRO and risk organization, oversees incentive design, and monitors — but it does not manage the risk function day to day.

A board is like a ship's owners who decide the destination and safety rules and check on progress; the captain and crew actually sail the ship.

- A is wrong: day-to-day management belongs to management, not the board.
- C is wrong: measuring and reporting daily VaR is an operational risk-function task.
- D is wrong: approving individual trades is a management/delegated-authority function, not board oversight.

📌 Exam tip: Board = oversight, NOT management. Set appetite, approve strategy/policies, ensure capable management and incentives, monitor and challenge ("constructive dialogue").

**Q10.** A risk report shows a clean VaR number, but the underlying trade capture and valuation data feeding it are unreliable. This best illustrates that:
- A. VaR is always wrong and should never be used
- B. A risk number is only as good as the trade capture, valuation, and data plumbing beneath it
- C. The board should personally re-enter all trade data
- D. Internal audit should design the trading system

**Answer: B**

The question is about the interdependence of functional units in risk management. The risk function depends on operations/IT for clean trade capture and on finance for valuations. If that plumbing is faulty, even a sophisticated risk number is unreliable — "garbage in, garbage out."

Think of a fancy fuel gauge wired to a broken sensor: the dial looks precise, but the reading is meaningless because the input is bad.

- A is wrong: the point is not that VaR is inherently useless, but that its quality depends on the data behind it.
- C is wrong: the board does not re-enter trade data; that is far outside its oversight role.
- D is wrong: internal audit must stay independent and assure controls, not *design* the trading system (that would compromise its independence).

📌 Exam tip: Risk management is not an island — it depends on operations/IT (clean trade capture), finance (valuation/P&L), and the back office (independent confirmation). Weak data plumbing undermines any risk number.
