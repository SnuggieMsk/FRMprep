**Q1.** A quote reads EUR/USD = 1.1000. What does this mean?

- A. 1 US dollar costs 1.1000 euros
- B. 1 euro costs 1.1000 US dollars
- C. The euro and dollar are equal in value
- D. 1.1000 euros equal 1.1000 dollars

**Answer: B**

The question tests the base-versus-quote currency convention.

In a quote like EUR/USD = 1.1000, the FIRST currency (EUR) is the base currency, and the SECOND (USD) is the quote currency. The number tells you how many units of the quote currency it takes to buy ONE unit of the base currency. So 1 euro (base) costs 1.1000 dollars (quote). The price is always given in the quote currency.

Why B is correct: one unit of the base (EUR) = 1.1000 units of the quote (USD).

Why A is wrong: it reverses base and quote; that would be the reciprocal (USD/EUR).
Why C is wrong: 1.1000 is not 1.0000, so the currencies are not equal in value.
Why D is wrong: the number is a price, not a statement that the two amounts are equal.

📌 Exam tip: First currency = base (the "one unit"); second = quote (the price). "EUR/USD = 1.10" means one euro costs $1.10.

**Q2.** Spot USD/EUR = 1.2000 (USD per euro). The 6-month USD interest rate is 2% and the EUR rate is 4%, both continuously compounded. The 6-month no-arbitrage forward rate is closest to:

- A. 1.1880
- B. 1.2000
- C. 1.2121
- D. 1.2240

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the covered-interest-parity forward rate.

With the rate quoted as domestic (USD) per unit of foreign (EUR), the formula is:
$$F = S\,e^{(r - r_f)T}$$

Here domestic r = 0.02 (USD), foreign $$r_f$$ = 0.04 (EUR), S = 1.2000, T = 0.5.

Exponent: (0.02 − 0.04) × 0.5 = (−0.02) × 0.5 = −0.01.
$$e^{-0.01} \approx 0.9900$$.
$$F = 1.2000 \times 0.9900 = 1.1880$$.

Why A is correct: 1.2000 × e^(−0.01) = 1.2000 × 0.9900 = 1.1880.

Why B is wrong: 1.2000 is just spot; it ignores the rate differential entirely.
Why C is wrong: 1.2121 ≈ 1.2000 × e^(+0.01) — it flips the sign of the exponent (the inverted-formula distractor).
Why D is wrong: 1.2240 doubles the differential or otherwise mishandles the exponent.

📌 Exam tip: $$F = Se^{(r - r_f)T}$$. The HIGHER-rate currency (EUR at 4%) trades at a forward DISCOUNT, so the forward (1.1880) is below spot (1.2000). A forward above spot signals a sign error.

**Q3.** Under covered interest rate parity, the currency with the HIGHER interest rate:

- A. Trades at a forward premium
- B. Trades at a forward discount
- C. Must appreciate in the spot market
- D. Has no determinate forward relationship

**Answer: B**

The question asks how a high interest rate affects a currency's forward price under CIP.

CIP says you cannot earn a free lunch by switching currencies and locking in the forward. If a currency offers a higher interest rate, that advantage must be given back somewhere — and it is given back through the forward, where the high-rate currency trades at a discount (it is expected to depreciate in forward terms). Otherwise, riskless arbitrage would exist.

Why B is correct: the high-rate currency's rate advantage is offset by a forward discount.

Why A is wrong: a forward PREMIUM would let investors earn both the higher rate AND a currency gain — a free lunch arbitrage would eliminate it.
Why C is wrong: CIP is about the FORWARD rate, not a prediction about the spot market; CIP does not require spot appreciation.
Why D is wrong: CIP gives a precise, arbitrage-enforced forward relationship, not an indeterminate one.

📌 Exam tip: Higher interest rate → forward DISCOUNT under CIP. The rate edge is always handed back via the forward, or arbitrage appears.

**Q4.** USD/EUR = 1.0800 (USD per euro) and CHF/USD = 0.9000 (CHF per dollar). The implied CHF per EUR cross rate is closest to:

- A. 0.8333
- B. 0.9720
- C. 1.2000
- D. 1.9800

**Answer: B**

The question asks for a cross rate built from two USD-based quotes.

We want CHF per EUR. Use the chain (units cancel like fractions):
$$\text{CHF per EUR} = (\text{USD per EUR}) \times (\text{CHF per USD})$$

Plug in:
$$= 1.0800 \times 0.9000 = 0.9720$$

Check the units: (USD/EUR) × (CHF/USD) — the USD cancels, leaving CHF/EUR. Correct.

Why B is correct: 1.0800 × 0.9000 = 0.9720 CHF per EUR.

Why A is wrong: 0.8333 = 0.9000 / 1.0800 — it divides instead of multiplying, mishandling units.
Why C is wrong: 1.2000 = 1.0800 / 0.9000 — wrong operation; units do not cancel correctly.
Why D is wrong: 1.9800 = 1.0800 + 0.9000 — you cannot add exchange rates.

📌 Exam tip: For cross rates, write each quote as a fraction (units) and chain them so the common currency cancels. If USD cancels, you set it up right.

**Q5.** A US multinational owns a factory in Brazil that it has no intention of selling. The exposure of its consolidated balance sheet to the Brazilian real (BRL) is best managed by:

- A. Selling BRL forward each quarter
- B. Buying BRL put options every month
- C. Financing the factory with BRL-denominated debt
- D. Doing nothing, because economic risk cannot be hedged

**Answer: C**

The question asks how to hedge translation (accounting) risk on a foreign asset that won't be sold.

Translation risk arises when foreign assets and liabilities are converted into the home currency for the consolidated financial statements. The sound hedge is matched-currency financing: fund the BRL asset with BRL-denominated debt. Then if the real moves, the gain or loss on the asset is offset by an equal and opposite move on the liability, leaving the balance sheet net exposure roughly unchanged.

Why C is correct: matching the currency of the liability to the asset offsets translation gains and losses naturally.

Why A is wrong: selling BRL forward against an asset you will NOT sell creates real cash gains/losses against a paper exposure — that is speculation, not a clean hedge.
Why B is wrong: buying options against an unsold asset is likewise speculative and costs premium for a paper exposure.
Why D is wrong: this is translation risk (an accounting exposure), which CAN be hedged with matched-currency financing; doing nothing is not the best answer.

📌 Exam tip: Translation risk → hedge with matched-currency FINANCING. Using derivatives against assets you won't sell turns the "hedge" into a speculative bet.

**Q6.** A treasurer must distinguish FX risks. A known, contracted foreign-currency receivable due in 90 days that will change in home-currency value as the exchange rate moves is an example of:

- A. Translation risk
- B. Transaction risk
- C. Economic risk
- D. Settlement risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks which type of FX risk a known future cash flow represents.

Transaction risk is the risk on KNOWN, contracted future foreign-currency cash flows (receivables and payables). A 90-day receivable is a booked amount whose home-currency value depends on the exchange rate at collection — exactly transaction risk. It is hedged with forwards, futures, options, or money-market hedges.

Why B is correct: a contracted receivable with a known amount and date is classic transaction risk.

Why A is wrong: translation risk is an ACCOUNTING exposure from consolidating foreign assets/liabilities, not a single contracted cash flow.
Why C is wrong: economic risk is the long-run competitive effect of FX on future (uncontracted) revenues and costs, not a booked receivable.
Why D is wrong: settlement risk is the risk a counterparty fails to deliver at settlement, a different concept.

📌 Exam tip: Transaction = known contracted cash flows. Translation = accounting/consolidation. Economic = long-run competitive position. The booked receivable is transaction risk.

**Q7.** USD per GBP = 1.2500 and USD per EUR = 1.1000. A dealer quotes EUR per GBP at 1.1500. Compared with the implied cross rate, this means:

- A. The quote matches the implied cross, so no arbitrage exists
- B. GBP is too expensive in EUR terms, creating a triangular arbitrage
- C. EUR is too expensive in GBP terms, but no profit is possible
- D. The quote is irrelevant because cross rates cannot be arbitraged

**Answer: B**

The question asks whether a dealer's cross-rate quote allows triangular arbitrage.

First find the implied cross (EUR per GBP):
$$\frac{\text{USD per GBP}}{\text{USD per EUR}} = \frac{1.2500}{1.1000} = 1.1364$$

The dealer quotes 1.1500 EUR per GBP, which is HIGHER than the implied 1.1364. That means the dealer is paying too many euros for each pound — GBP is overpriced in EUR terms. A trader can sell GBP for EUR at the rich rate while buying GBP cheaply through USD, locking in a riskless profit (triangular arbitrage).

Why B is correct: 1.1500 > 1.1364 means GBP is overpriced in EUR, so triangular arbitrage exists.

Why A is wrong: the quote (1.1500) does NOT equal the implied cross (1.1364), so a gap and arbitrage exist.
Why C is wrong: the misalignment DOES create a profit opportunity; saying none is possible is incorrect.
Why D is wrong: cross-rate misquotes are precisely what triangular arbitrage exploits.

📌 Exam tip: Compute the implied cross from the USD quotes, then compare. Any gap = triangular arbitrage. Sell the overpriced currency at the rich quote.

**Q8.** Which statement about an FX swap is correct?

- A. It is a single contract combining a spot exchange with a reverse forward exchange
- B. It is a one-time exchange of currencies settling T+2
- C. It is an exchange-traded contract with daily settlement
- D. It is a contract to exchange interest payments only, with no principal

**Answer: A**

The question asks for the correct description of an FX swap.

An FX swap is a single contract with two legs: an exchange of currencies now (spot) combined with a reverse exchange at a future date (forward). Economically it is like a collateralized loan of one currency against another, and it is the most heavily traded FX instrument. It is used for rolling hedges and short-term funding in another currency.

Why A is correct: spot leg plus a reverse forward leg in one contract is exactly an FX swap.

Why B is wrong: that describes a plain SPOT transaction (one-time, T+2), not a swap.
Why C is wrong: that describes currency FUTURES (exchange-traded, daily settlement), not an OTC FX swap.
Why D is wrong: exchanging only interest with no principal describes a different instrument; an FX swap exchanges principal currency amounts.

📌 Exam tip: FX swap = spot leg + opposite forward leg in one deal. It is the workhorse for rolling hedges and short-term cross-currency funding.

**Q9.** Purchasing power parity (PPP) states that:

- A. Nominal exchange rates stay fixed regardless of inflation
- B. Nominal exchange rates adjust over time to offset inflation differentials, keeping the real exchange rate roughly constant
- C. Real exchange rates move one-for-one with interest rate differentials
- D. Exchange rates are perfectly predictable in the short run

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the statement of purchasing power parity.

PPP says that over the long run, nominal exchange rates move to offset differences in inflation between two countries. If one country has higher inflation, its currency tends to weaken just enough to keep the REAL exchange rate (the inflation-adjusted rate) roughly constant. It is a useful long-run anchor but a poor short-run predictor.

Why B is correct: nominal rates adjust to offset inflation differentials, keeping the real rate stable.

Why A is wrong: PPP says nominal rates ADJUST for inflation; they are not fixed.
Why C is wrong: that confuses PPP (inflation) with interest-rate parity (interest differentials).
Why D is wrong: PPP is a long-run tendency and is famously a poor short-run predictor.

📌 Exam tip: PPP = inflation differentials drive nominal rates so the REAL rate holds. It is a long-run anchor, weak in the short run. Don't confuse it with interest-rate parity.

**Q10.** The carry trade — borrowing a low-interest-rate currency to invest in a high-interest-rate currency — works because:

- A. Covered interest parity is violated, giving riskless profit
- B. Uncovered interest parity tends to fail empirically: high-rate currencies do not depreciate as much as predicted
- C. The forward rate guarantees the gain
- D. Central banks guarantee the interest differential

**Answer: B**

The question asks why the carry trade is profitable on average.

Uncovered interest parity (UIP) predicts that a high-rate currency will depreciate just enough to wipe out its interest advantage. But empirically this does NOT happen reliably — high-rate currencies tend to depreciate LESS than UIP predicts, or even appreciate. So a trader who borrows the low-rate currency and invests in the high-rate one captures the interest differential. It earns steadily in calm markets but can suffer big losses if the funding currency suddenly strengthens ("up the stairs, down the elevator").

Why B is correct: the carry trade profits from the empirical failure of UIP.

Why A is wrong: covered interest parity (with a forward) holds via arbitrage and offers no riskless profit; the carry trade is uncovered and RISKY.
Why C is wrong: there is no forward locking in the gain — that would be covered, and CIP would erase the profit.
Why D is wrong: central banks do not guarantee the differential; the trade is genuinely risky.

📌 Exam tip: Carry trade = bet that UIP fails. There is no arbitrage and no guarantee — it earns the rate differential but carries crash risk when the funding currency rallies.
