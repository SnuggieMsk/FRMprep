**Q1.** In a single-variable OLS regression, the standard deviation of $$X$$ is 4%, the standard deviation of $$Y$$ is 6%, and the correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$ is 0.5. The OLS slope of $$Y$$ on $$X$$ is:
- A. 0.33
- B. 0.50
- C. 0.75
- D. 1.33

**Answer: C**

The question asks: how much does $$Y$$ change for a one-unit change in $$X$$, given the correlation and the two standard deviations?

Step 1 — Use the slope formula written in terms of correlation:
$$\hat\beta = \rho_{XY}\,\frac{s_Y}{s_X}$$

Step 2 — Plug in the numbers ($$\rho = 0.5$$, $$s_Y = 6$$, $$s_X = 4$$):
$$\hat\beta = 0.5 \times \frac{6}{4} = 0.5 \times 1.5 = 0.75$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.33): this divides $$s_X/s_Y$$ the wrong way around or computes $$0.5 \times 4/6$$. Remember $$s_Y$$ goes on top.
- B (0.50): this is just the correlation $$\rho$$, forgetting to scale by the ratio of standard deviations.
- D (1.33): this is $$s_Y/s_X = 6/4$$ without multiplying by the correlation 0.5.

Analogy: the slope is the correlation "stretched" by how spread out $$Y$$ is compared to $$X$$. Half the correlation, stretched by 1.5, gives 0.75.

📌 Exam tip: $$\hat\beta = \rho\,\frac{s_Y}{s_X}$$. The dependent variable's SD ($$s_Y$$) is always on top.

**Q2.** Stock and market excess returns give $$\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(R_s, R_m) = 0.0024$$ and $$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(R_m) = 0.0016$$. The CAPM beta (the OLS slope of stock on market) is:
- A. 0.67
- B. 1.00
- C. 1.50
- D. 0.0040

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the regression slope (the stock's beta), given the covariance with the market and the market's variance.

Step 1 — The slope formula in covariance/variance form:
$$\hat\beta = \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(R_s, R_m)}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(R_m)}$$

Step 2 — Plug in:
$$\hat\beta = \frac{0.0024}{0.0016} = 1.50$$

The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.67): this flips the fraction ($$0.0016/0.0024$$) — variance on top instead of covariance.
- B (1.00): a "default" beta of 1, ignoring the actual numbers.
- D (0.0040): this adds the two figures ($$0.0024 + 0.0016$$) instead of dividing.

Analogy: beta measures how much the stock "swings" with the market. Covariance is the shared movement; dividing by the market's own variance scales it into a per-unit sensitivity.

📌 Exam tip: Beta = Cov(stock, market) ÷ Var(market). Covariance on top, the X-variable's variance on the bottom.

**Q3.** In a single-variable OLS regression, the $$R^2$$ is 0.49. The correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$ is:
- A. 0.49
- B. $$\pm 0.70$$
- C. 0.24
- D. Cannot be determined

**Answer: B**

The question asks you to back out the correlation from $$R^2$$ in a single-variable regression.

Key fact: in a regression with ONE explanatory variable, $$R^2$$ equals the squared correlation between $$X$$ and $$Y$$:
$$R^2 = \rho_{XY}^2$$

Step 1 — Solve for the correlation by taking the square root:
$$\rho_{XY} = \pm\sqrt{R^2} = \pm\sqrt{0.49} = \pm 0.70$$

The square root has two possible signs, so the correlation is $$\pm 0.70$$. The sign matches the sign of the slope, which we are not told. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.49): this confuses $$R^2$$ with $$\rho$$; $$R^2$$ is the SQUARE of the correlation, not the correlation itself.
- C (0.24): this squares 0.49 again ($$0.49^2 \approx 0.24$$), going the wrong way.
- D: the correlation's MAGNITUDE is perfectly determined ($$0.70$$); only its sign is unknown, so "cannot be determined" overstates the uncertainty.

Analogy: $$R^2$$ is like the area of a square. To get back the side length (the correlation) you take a square root — and a square's side could correspond to either a positive or negative original number.

📌 Exam tip: Single-variable regression: $$\rho = \pm\sqrt{R^2}$$. The magnitude is fixed; the sign follows the slope.

**Q4.** Errors in an OLS regression are heteroskedastic (their variance depends on $$X$$). The consequence is:
- A. The slope estimates are biased.
- B. The slope estimates are unbiased, but the usual standard errors are invalid.
- C. The slope estimates become inconsistent.
- D. The $$R^2$$ is biased downward.

**Answer: B**

The question asks what damage heteroskedasticity does to an OLS regression.

Heteroskedasticity means the spread of the errors changes with $$X$$ (e.g., bigger firms have noisier returns). The key facts:
- OLS coefficients stay UNBIASED and consistent — the point estimates are still fine.
- But the textbook formula for the standard errors assumes constant variance, so those standard errors become wrong (usually too small). That makes the t-tests and F-tests misleading.

So the coefficients survive but the inference does not. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: heteroskedasticity does NOT bias the slope. The deadly assumption for bias is exogeneity ($$E[\epsilon|X]=0$$), not constant variance.
- C: OLS remains consistent under heteroskedasticity; it just is not efficient (no longer BLUE).
- D: $$R^2$$ is not biased by heteroskedasticity; the problem is with the standard errors, not the goodness-of-fit measure.

Analogy: heteroskedasticity is like a bathroom scale that is correct on average but jumps around more for heavy people. Your average reading is still right (unbiased), but your stated "± margin of error" is wrong.

📌 Exam tip: Heteroskedasticity → unbiased coefficients, invalid SEs. Fix with White (robust) standard errors. Omitted-variable bias is the one that biases coefficients.

**Q5.** "Linear regression" requires the model to be linear in the parameters. Which of the following CANNOT be estimated by OLS?
- A. $$Y = \alpha + \beta X^2 + \epsilon$$
- B. $$\ln Y = \alpha + \beta \ln X + \epsilon$$
- C. $$Y = \alpha + X^\beta + \epsilon$$
- D. $$Y = \alpha + \beta X + \epsilon$$

**Answer: C**

The question tests what "linear" really means in linear regression. It means linear in the PARAMETERS ($$\alpha, \beta$$), not necessarily in the variables.

Check each model — can we write it as (parameter) × (some transformed variable) + (parameter)?
- A: $$Y = \alpha + \beta X^2$$. Treat $$X^2$$ as a single variable; then $$\beta$$ multiplies it linearly. Estimable. ✓
- B: $$\ln Y = \alpha + \beta \ln X$$. Transform both sides into logs; then it is a straight line in those logged variables. Estimable. ✓
- C: $$Y = \alpha + X^\beta$$. Here $$\beta$$ sits in the EXPONENT, not as a multiplier. There is no way to transform this into something linear in $$\beta$$. NOT estimable by OLS. ✗
- D: $$Y = \alpha + \beta X$$. The plain straight line. Estimable. ✓

The one that cannot be done is C. The correct answer is C.

Why the wrong options are wrong (i.e., why they ARE estimable):
- A and B are nonlinear in the VARIABLES but linear in the parameters once you transform the data — perfectly fine for OLS.
- D is the textbook linear model — obviously estimable.

Analogy: OLS cares about how the dials ($$\alpha, \beta$$) enter the machine. If a dial multiplies an input, fine. If a dial becomes a power (an exponent), the machine cannot be solved by ordinary least squares.

📌 Exam tip: "Linear" = linear in the parameters. A parameter in the exponent ($$X^\beta$$) breaks OLS; powers/logs of the variables do not.

**Q6.** A regression on 62 observations gives $$\hat\beta = 0.85$$ with $$SE(\hat\beta) = 0.30$$. Testing $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ (two-sided, 5%, critical value 2.000), the t-statistic and decision are:
- A. t = 2.83; reject $$H_0$$ — $$X$$ is significant.
- B. t = 2.83; fail to reject $$H_0$$.
- C. t = 0.85; fail to reject $$H_0$$.
- D. t = 0.30; reject $$H_0$$.

**Answer: A**

The question asks whether the slope is statistically different from zero.

Step 1 — The t-statistic for testing $$\beta = 0$$:
$$t = \frac{\hat\beta - 0}{SE(\hat\beta)} = \frac{0.85}{0.30} = 2.833$$

Step 2 — Compare to the critical value. With $$n - 2 = 60$$ degrees of freedom the two-sided 5% critical value is 2.000.
$$2.833 > 2.000$$

Since the statistic exceeds the critical value, reject $$H_0$$ — $$X$$ is a significant predictor. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B: the t-statistic of 2.83 is correct, but exceeding 2.000 means REJECT, not fail to reject. The decision is backwards.
- C (0.85): this just reports the coefficient as if it were the t-statistic, skipping the division by the SE.
- D (0.30): this reports the SE instead of computing the ratio.

Analogy: the t-statistic asks "how many standard errors is the slope away from zero?" Here it is 2.83 — clearly more than the 2.0 cutoff, so the slope is convincingly nonzero.

📌 Exam tip: t = coefficient ÷ its SE. Degrees of freedom in single-variable regression are $$n - 2$$ (two parameters estimated).

**Q7.** Using the same regression ($$\hat\beta = 0.85$$, $$SE = 0.30$$, critical value 2.000), a risk manager tests whether the hedge ratio equals one: $$H_0: \beta = 1$$. The conclusion is:
- A. Reject; the hedge ratio is significantly different from one.
- B. Fail to reject; the estimate is statistically indistinguishable from one.
- C. The test cannot be done unless $$\beta_0 = 0$$.
- D. Reject; the hedge ratio is significantly greater than one.

**Answer: B**

The question asks whether the estimated slope of 0.85 is far enough from 1 (not from 0) to be significant. The null value here is $$\beta_0 = 1$$.

Step 1 — The general t-statistic uses the hypothesized value in the numerator:
$$t = \frac{\hat\beta - \beta_0}{SE(\hat\beta)} = \frac{0.85 - 1}{0.30} = \frac{-0.15}{0.30} = -0.50$$

Step 2 — Compare its absolute value to the critical value 2.000:
$$|-0.50| = 0.50 < 2.000$$

Since 0.50 does not exceed 2.000, fail to reject. The slope is statistically indistinguishable from a one-for-one hedge. The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: the statistic 0.50 is far below the cutoff, so there is no significant difference from 1.
- C: you can test ANY null value, not just zero — just put $$\beta_0$$ into the numerator.
- D: $$0.85 < 1$$ and the difference is not significant anyway, so "significantly greater than one" is doubly wrong.

Analogy: the estimate (0.85) is close to the target of 1, and the wobble (SE = 0.30) is big enough that 0.85 could easily be a noisy version of a true 1.

📌 Exam tip: To test against a non-zero value, subtract it in the numerator: $$t = (\hat\beta - \beta_0)/SE$$. The hedge-ratio-equals-one test is a classic.

**Q8.** A regression gives $$\hat\beta = 0.85$$ with $$SE = 0.30$$ and critical value 2.000. The 95% confidence interval for $$\beta$$ is closest to:
- A. (0.55, 1.15)
- B. (0.25, 1.45)
- C. (0.85, 1.00)
- D. (0.45, 1.25)

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the range of plausible values for the true slope at 95% confidence.

Step 1 — The confidence interval formula:
$$\hat\beta \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-2}\,SE(\hat\beta)$$

Step 2 — Compute the margin (the "± part"):
$$2.000 \times 0.30 = 0.60$$

Step 3 — Build the interval around the estimate 0.85:
$$0.85 - 0.60 = 0.25 \quad \text{and} \quad 0.85 + 0.60 = 1.45$$

So the interval is $$(0.25,\ 1.45)$$. The correct answer is B.

Notice this interval EXCLUDES 0 (so we reject $$\beta = 0$$, matching Q6) and CONTAINS 1 (so we fail to reject $$\beta = 1$$, matching Q7). The CI is consistent with both tests — that is the duality at work.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A (0.55, 1.15): uses a margin of 0.30 (forgot to multiply by the critical value 2.000).
- C (0.85, 1.00): this is not an interval centered on the estimate at all; it is arbitrary.
- D (0.45, 1.25): uses a margin of 0.40, which does not come from $$2.000 \times 0.30$$.

Analogy: the confidence interval is the estimate (0.85) plus and minus a "buffer." The buffer is the critical value times the wobble: $$2 \times 0.30 = 0.60$$.

📌 Exam tip: CI = estimate ± (critical value × SE). Check it against your tests: it should exclude any null you reject and include any null you fail to reject.

**Q9.** Which statement about OLS estimators under the standard assumptions (exogeneity, random sampling, finite moments) is correct?
- A. OLS is biased but consistent.
- B. OLS is unbiased and consistent, and the slope is approximately normal in large samples.
- C. OLS requires the errors to be normally distributed to be unbiased.
- D. OLS is unbiased only if $$R^2$$ is high.

**Answer: B**

The question asks about the basic statistical properties of OLS estimates when the core assumptions hold.

Under exogeneity ($$E[\epsilon|X]=0$$), random sampling, and finite moments:
- OLS is UNBIASED (on average it hits the true value) and CONSISTENT (it converges to the truth as $$n$$ grows).
- By the Central Limit Theorem, the sampling distribution of $$\hat\beta$$ is approximately normal in large samples — which is what lets us use z/t critical values.

The correct answer is B.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- A: OLS is unbiased (not biased) under these assumptions, so "biased but consistent" is wrong.
- C: normality of the errors is NOT needed for unbiasedness — it only matters for exact small-sample inference. In large samples the CLT takes over.
- D: a high $$R^2$$ has nothing to do with whether the estimator is unbiased. You can have unbiased OLS with a low $$R^2$$ and vice versa.

Analogy: unbiased means the dartboard is centered on the bullseye on average; consistent means the more darts (data) you throw, the tighter they cluster on the bullseye. Neither requires the darts to follow a bell-shaped scatter.

📌 Exam tip: Exogeneity is the key assumption for unbiasedness. Normality of errors is NOT required for OLS to be unbiased/consistent — the CLT delivers approximate normality of $$\hat\beta$$ in large samples.

**Q10.** A regression gives $$\hat\beta = 1.2$$ with $$SE = 0.4$$ and $$n$$ large. At the 5% level, testing $$H_0: \beta = 0$$ (use 1.96 as the cutoff):
- A. Reject $$H_0$$ (t = 3.0).
- B. Fail to reject $$H_0$$ (t = 0.48).
- C. Reject only with a one-sided test.
- D. Not testable without $$R^2$$.

**Answer: A**

The question asks whether the slope is significantly different from zero in a large sample.

Step 1 — Compute the t-statistic:
$$t = \frac{\hat\beta}{SE} = \frac{1.2}{0.4} = 3.0$$

Step 2 — Compare to the large-sample 5% cutoff of 1.96:
$$3.0 > 1.96$$

Since 3.0 comfortably exceeds 1.96, reject $$H_0$$. The correct answer is A.

Why the wrong options are wrong:
- B (0.48): this divides $$0.4/1.2$$ (or $$0.4 \times 1.2$$ rounded), flipping the ratio. The coefficient goes on top.
- C: the statistic 3.0 exceeds even the two-sided cutoff, so it rejects two-sided; you do not need a one-sided test.
- D: the test only needs the coefficient and its standard error; $$R^2$$ is irrelevant to a single-coefficient t-test.

Analogy: the slope (1.2) is three standard errors (0.4 each) away from zero. Anything beyond about two standard errors is "significant" at 5%, so three is a clear reject.

📌 Exam tip: With large n, |t| > 1.96 rejects at 5% and |t| > 2.58 rejects at 1%. If GARP gives you a coefficient and its SE, they want t = β̂ / SE.
