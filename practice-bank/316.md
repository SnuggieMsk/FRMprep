**Q1.** A rate of 8% per annum with semiannual compounding is closest to which continuously compounded rate?
- A. 7.85%
- B. 8.00%
- C. 8.16%
- D. 7.69%

**Answer: A**

The question asks us to take a rate that is "compounded twice a year" and rewrite it as the equivalent rate that is "compounded all the time" (continuously). They describe the same growth, just in different language.

The conversion formula is:
$$r_c = m \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right)$$

Here $$r_m = 0.08$$ (the quoted rate) and $$m = 2$$ (twice a year). Plug in step by step:
$$r_c = 2 \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{0.08}{2}\right) = 2\ln(1.04)$$
$$\ln(1.04) = 0.039221$$
$$r_c = 2 \times 0.039221 = 0.078442 = 7.84\% \approx 7.85\%$$

So A is correct.

Think of it like this: if you split a candy bar into more and more pieces, each piece gets smaller, but the total candy is the same. More frequent compounding means a smaller quoted rate gives the same total growth. So the continuous rate must be a little LOWER than 8%.

- B (8.00%) is the trap of doing nothing — assuming the number is unchanged when you switch conventions. The number must change.
- C (8.16%) goes the wrong direction; 8.16% is actually the effective annual rate $$(1.04)^2 - 1$$, which is higher than 8%, not the continuous rate.
- D (7.69%) over-shoots downward — it is not produced by the correct formula.

📌 Exam tip: When converting to MORE frequent compounding (annual → continuous), the quoted rate goes DOWN. If your answer went up, you used the formula backwards.

**Q2.** The 1-year and 2-year continuously compounded zero rates are 4.0% and 5.0%. The continuously compounded forward rate for year 2 (the period from year 1 to year 2) is closest to:
- A. 4.5%
- B. 5.0%
- C. 6.0%
- D. 9.0%

**Answer: C**

The question asks: given today's spot rates for 1 year and 2 years, what is the rate "locked in" for the second year alone?

The continuous-compounding forward rate formula is:
$$R_F = \frac{R_2 T_2 - R_1 T_1}{T_2 - T_1}$$

Plug in $$R_1 = 0.04, T_1 = 1, R_2 = 0.05, T_2 = 2$$:
$$R_F = \frac{0.05 \times 2 - 0.04 \times 1}{2 - 1} = \frac{0.10 - 0.04}{1} = \frac{0.06}{1} = 0.06 = 6.0\%$$

So C is correct.

Everyday picture: the 2-year rate (5%) is an average of the first-year rate (4%) and the second-year rate. If the average is 5% and year 1 was only 4%, then year 2 must be higher than 5% to pull the average up — here, 6%.

- A (4.5%) is the simple average of 4% and 5% — but forward rates are not a plain average; they weight by time.
- B (5.0%) just repeats the 2-year spot rate. The forward must be above the spot when the curve slopes up.
- D (9.0%) comes from adding $$0.05 \times 2 + 0.04 \times 1$$ instead of subtracting — a sign mistake in the numerator.

📌 Exam tip: On an upward-sloping curve, the forward rate is always ABOVE both spot rates. If your forward came out below the 2-year spot, recheck the arithmetic.

**Q3.** For pricing derivatives, the modern best proxy for the risk-free rate is:
- A. The Treasury bill yield, since the government cannot default
- B. OIS / SOFR-based rates, because Treasury yields are pushed artificially low
- C. The prime lending rate set by commercial banks
- D. Legacy LIBOR, because it captures bank credit risk

**Answer: B**

The question asks which interest rate derivatives traders treat as "risk-free" when valuing contracts.

Why B is correct: OIS (overnight indexed swap) rates and SOFR-based rates are built from actual, secured, very-low-risk overnight transactions. They are the market standard proxy for the risk-free rate in derivatives work.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — Treasury yields look risk-free, but they are dragged DOWN by tax advantages and heavy regulatory/liquidity demand for Treasuries. That makes them too low to use as the discount rate for derivatives.
- C — the prime rate is a bank lending rate that includes a profit margin and credit risk; it is not risk-free.
- D — legacy LIBOR contains bank credit risk and was manipulated; it has been phased out and replaced by SOFR-type rates.

Analogy: a Treasury yield is like a sale price that is artificially marked down because everyone is forced to buy it — it does not reflect the true "fair" cost of money.

📌 Exam tip: Remember the one-liner: "Risk-free for derivatives = OIS/SOFR, NOT Treasuries (Treasuries are too low)."

**Q4.** Under the liquidity preference theory of the term structure, forward rates:
- A. Exactly equal expected future spot rates
- B. Lie below expected future spot rates
- C. Lie above expected future spot rates
- D. Have no relationship to expected future spot rates

**Answer: C**

The question asks what the liquidity preference theory says about how forward rates compare to what people actually expect future short rates to be.

Why C is correct: lenders prefer to lend short term (they want their money back soon), and borrowers prefer to borrow long term (they want certainty). To convince lenders to commit for longer, borrowers must pay a premium. This premium pushes long-term and forward rates ABOVE the rates people genuinely expect — i.e., forward rates are biased upward. This is the standard explanation for why yield curves usually slope upward.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — that is the PURE EXPECTATIONS theory, where forwards equal expected future spots with no premium.
- B — this reverses the premium; liquidity preference adds a premium on top, so forwards are higher, not lower.
- D — that describes MARKET SEGMENTATION, where each maturity is its own independent market.

Analogy: locking your money in a long-term fixed deposit usually pays more than a savings account — the bank pays you extra to give up easy access. That extra is the liquidity premium.

📌 Exam tip: Match the theory to the keyword — "premium for lending long" = liquidity preference; "forwards = expected spots" = pure expectations; "independent supply/demand per maturity" = market segmentation.

**Q5.** A 1-year zero-coupon bond with face value $100 is priced at $96.08. The 1-year continuously compounded zero rate is closest to:
- A. 3.92%
- B. 4.00%
- C. 4.08%
- D. 4.16%

**Answer: B**

The question gives the price today and the single future payment of a pure discount bond, and asks for the continuously compounded rate that links them.

For continuous compounding, $$\text{Price} = \text{Face} \times e^{-R \times T}$$. Rearranging for the rate over $$T = 1$$ year:
$$96.08 = 100 e^{-R \times 1}$$
$$e^{-R} = \frac{96.08}{100} = 0.9608$$
$$e^{R} = \frac{100}{96.08} = 1.04080$$
$$R = \ln(1.04080) = 0.04000 = 4.00\%$$

So B is correct.

Plain words: the bond grows from $96.08 to $100 in one year. The "natural log" button turns that growth ratio into the continuous rate.

- A (3.92%) comes from $$\ln(0.9608) = -0.04$$ then dropping the minus sign incorrectly, or from $$(100-96.08)/100$$ — a simple percentage discount, not a compounded rate.
- C (4.08%) uses $$(100-96.08)/96.08 = 4.08\%$$, the simple (non-compounded) return on price — not the continuous rate.
- D (4.16%) is too high; no correct method gives it.

📌 Exam tip: For a zero-coupon bond with continuous compounding, the rate is just $$R = \frac{1}{T}\ln(\text{Face}/\text{Price})$$. Divide the natural log by the number of years.

**Q6.** Bond A is a 1-year zero priced at 96.08 (face 100), implying a 1-year zero rate of 4.00% (continuous). Bond B is a 2-year bond with a 5% annual coupon priced at 100.77 (face 100). Using bootstrapping, the 2-year continuously compounded zero rate is closest to ($$e^{-0.04}=0.9608$$):
- A. 4.00%
- B. 4.50%
- C. 5.00%
- D. 5.50%

**Answer: B**

The question asks us to "bootstrap" — use the already-known 1-year rate to back out the unknown 2-year rate from a 2-year coupon bond's price.

Bond B pays a 5 coupon at year 1 and 105 (coupon + face) at year 2. Its price equals the sum of those cash flows discounted at the matching zero rates:
$$100.77 = 5e^{-0.04 \times 1} + 105 e^{-R_2 \times 2}$$

Step 1 — discount the year-1 coupon using the known rate:
$$5 \times 0.9608 = 4.8040$$

Step 2 — isolate the year-2 term:
$$105 e^{-2R_2} = 100.77 - 4.8040 = 95.9660$$
$$e^{-2R_2} = \frac{95.9660}{105} = 0.91396$$

Step 3 — solve for $$R_2$$:
$$-2R_2 = \ln(0.91396) = -0.08998$$
$$R_2 = \frac{0.08998}{2} = 0.04499 \approx 4.50\%$$

So B is correct.

Picture it like climbing a ladder: you can only reach the second rung (2-year rate) by standing on the first rung (1-year rate) you already know.

- A (4.00%) just repeats the 1-year rate — but the curve here rises.
- C (5.00%) wrongly assumes the zero rate equals the coupon rate; a bond priced near par does not make its zero rate equal the coupon.
- D (5.50%) is too high and not produced by the correct steps.

📌 Exam tip: Bootstrapping always works from shortest to longest maturity, discounting each known earlier cash flow first, then solving for the single remaining unknown rate.

**Q7.** Using 1-year and 2-year continuous zero rates of 4% and 5%, an investor enters a forward rate agreement (FRA) to RECEIVE 6.5% (annually compounded) on $100 million for year 2. The forward rate for year 2 is 6.18% (annually compounded). The value of the FRA is closest to ($$e^{-0.10}=0.9048$$):
- A. +$320,000
- B. +$289,536
- C. −$289,536
- D. +$650,000

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the value today of an agreement that lets you receive a fixed 6.5% when the going forward rate is only 6.18%. Receiving more than the going rate is good, so the value should be positive.

The value to the receive-fixed party is:
$$V = L \times (R_K - R_F) \times (T_2 - T_1) \times e^{-R_2 T_2}$$

Plug in $$L = 100{,}000{,}000$$, $$R_K = 0.065$$, $$R_F = 0.0618$$, period $$= 1$$ year, discount factor $$e^{-0.05 \times 2} = e^{-0.10} = 0.9048$$:

Step 1 — rate difference:
$$0.065 - 0.0618 = 0.0032$$

Step 2 — multiply by notional and the 1-year period:
$$100{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0032 \times 1 = 320{,}000$$

Step 3 — discount back to today:
$$320{,}000 \times 0.9048 = 289{,}536$$

So B is correct: $289,536.

Analogy: you locked in a price to BUY lemonade at 6.5 cents when the market lets you buy at only 6.18 cents — wait, here you RECEIVE 6.5% versus a market of 6.18%, so it's like being paid above the going rate: a gain.

- A (+$320,000) forgets to discount the future gain back to today (skips Step 3).
- C (−$289,536) has the wrong sign; receiving ABOVE the forward rate is a gain, not a loss.
- D (+$650,000) ignores that we should multiply by the rate DIFFERENCE, not the full 6.5% rate.

📌 Exam tip: An FRA's value is (locked rate − forward rate) × notional × period, then DISCOUNTED. Forgetting the discount factor is the most common slip.

**Q8.** Which statement about compounding frequency is correct, holding the effective annual return fixed?
- A. More frequent compounding requires a higher quoted rate
- B. More frequent compounding requires a lower quoted rate
- C. Compounding frequency does not affect the quoted rate
- D. Continuous compounding gives the highest quoted rate of all

**Answer: B**

The question asks how the quoted (nominal) rate must change when you compound more often but keep the actual yearly growth the same.

Why B is correct: if compounding happens more often, interest earns interest sooner, which boosts the effective return. To keep the effective return UNCHANGED, the quoted rate must be set LOWER. So the continuous rate (compounding "infinitely often") is the lowest quoted rate for a given effective return.

Quick check using numbers: a 12% effective annual return needs a quoted rate of 12% annually, but only about 11.39% continuously ($$\ln(1.12) = 0.1133$$). The continuous quote is lower.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — backwards; more frequent compounding needs a lower, not higher, quote.
- C — frequency definitely matters; that's the whole point of conversions.
- D — continuous gives the LOWEST quoted rate for a fixed effective return, not the highest.

Analogy: dividing a pizza into more slices doesn't give more pizza — each slice (quoted rate) just gets thinner while the whole pizza (effective return) stays the same.

📌 Exam tip: Order of quoted rates for the same effective return: annual > semiannual > quarterly > monthly > continuous (continuous is always the smallest number).

**Q9.** A 2-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon (face $100). The 1-year and 2-year continuously compounded zero rates are 4% and 4.5%. The bond's price is closest to ($$e^{-0.04}=0.9608$$, $$e^{-0.09}=0.9139$$):
- A. $98.64
- B. $102.64
- C. $106.00
- D. $100.00

**Answer: B**

The question asks for the price of a coupon bond when each cash flow is discounted at its own zero rate (not a single yield).

The bond pays a 6 coupon at year 1 and 106 (coupon + face) at year 2. Discount each at its matching zero rate:
$$P = 6 e^{-0.04 \times 1} + 106 e^{-0.045 \times 2}$$

Note $$0.045 \times 2 = 0.09$$, so the second discount factor is $$e^{-0.09} = 0.9139$$.

Step 1 — year-1 coupon:
$$6 \times 0.9608 = 5.7648$$

Step 2 — year-2 cash flow:
$$106 \times 0.9139 = 96.8734$$

Step 3 — add:
$$5.7648 + 96.8734 = 102.6382 \approx \$102.64$$

So B is correct.

Picture a bond as a small bundle of separate IOUs; each IOU is priced with the interest rate that matches its own due date, then we add them up.

- A ($98.64) uses the wrong discount factors or discounts the 106 at the 1-year rate.
- C ($106.00) just sums the raw cash flows with no discounting at all.
- D ($100.00) assumes the bond trades at par, ignoring that the 6% coupon beats the ~4–4.5% rates, so it should trade at a premium.

📌 Exam tip: Because the coupon (6%) is higher than the discount rates (4–4.5%), the bond MUST trade above par. A coupon-above-yield bond at a price below 100 should immediately look wrong.

**Q10.** A repo (sale-and-repurchase agreement) rate is best described as:
- A. An unsecured interbank lending rate with embedded bank credit risk
- B. The yield on long-term government bonds
- C. A secured borrowing rate, collateralized by securities, with very low credit risk
- D. The fixed rate in a long-dated interest rate swap

**Answer: C**

The question asks what kind of interest rate a repo rate is.

Why C is correct: in a repo, one party SELLS a security and agrees to BUY it back shortly after at a slightly higher price. The security acts as collateral, so the lender is protected if the borrower fails. That collateral makes repo a secured loan with very low credit risk, which is why repo rates are low and stable.

Why the others are wrong:
- A — that describes legacy LIBOR (unsecured interbank lending). Repo is secured, the opposite.
- B — that is the Treasury bond yield, a long-term government rate, not an overnight secured funding rate.
- D — that is a swap rate, unrelated to repo financing.

Analogy: a repo is like a pawn shop loan — you hand over your watch (the security) as collateral and buy it back later for a bit more. Because the shop holds your watch, the loan is low-risk for them.

📌 Exam tip: Keyword match for rate types — "secured/collateralized by securities" = repo; "unsecured interbank" = LIBOR; "overnight indexed swap average" = OIS; "actual Treasury repo transactions" = SOFR.
