**Q1.** A gap call option has payoff strike $K_1 = \$55$ and trigger strike $K_2 = \$50$. At expiration the stock is $S_T = \$53$. The payoff is:
- A. $0
- B. $3
- C. −$2
- D. −$5

**Answer: C**

The question tests the gap option payoff, which can be negative.

A gap call pays $S_T - K_1$ if $S_T > K_2$ (the trigger), and zero otherwise.

Step 1 — Is the trigger met? $S_T = 53 > K_2 = 50$? Yes, so the option pays out.

Step 2 — Payoff = $S_T - K_1 = 53 - 55 = -\$2$.

Because the payoff strike ($55$) is higher than the trigger ($50$), in the range $50 < S_T < 55$ the payoff is negative — the holder actually loses money at expiry.

Why C is correct: the trigger is crossed, and the payoff $53 - 55 = -2$ is NOT floored at zero.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($0) wrongly assumes the payoff can't be negative — but gap payoffs are not floored at zero.
- B ($3) computes $53 - 50$ (using the trigger as the strike) — wrong; the payoff uses $K_1 = 55$.
- D (−$5) is the maximum loss ($50 - 55$), which occurs at $S_T = 50$, not at $53$.

Analogy: a gap option is like a door that opens at one number ($K_2$) but charges you a fee based on a different number ($K_1$). Once the door opens, you must pay even if the math is against you.

📌 Exam tip: Gap payoff is automatic once the trigger $K_2$ is crossed and uses $K_1$ — it is NOT floored at zero, so it can be negative.

**Q2.** A down-and-out call is worth $4.20, and the vanilla call (same strike and maturity) is worth $6.50. The down-and-in call with the same barrier is worth:
- A. $2.30
- B. $4.20
- C. $6.50
- D. $10.70

**Answer: A**

The question tests barrier parity.

For the same strike, maturity, and barrier:
$$c_{\text{in}} + c_{\text{out}} = c_{\text{vanilla}}$$

Solve for the knock-in:
$$c_{\text{in}} = c_{\text{vanilla}} - c_{\text{out}} = 6.50 - 4.20 = \$2.30$$

Why A is correct: a knock-in plus a knock-out (same terms) equals a vanilla option, so the in-option is the difference.

Why the others are wrong:
- B ($4.20) just repeats the knock-out price.
- C ($6.50) repeats the vanilla price.
- D ($10.70) ADDS the two given prices instead of subtracting — that breaks the parity identity.

Analogy: think of the vanilla call as a whole pie. The knock-out is one slice; the knock-in is the rest of the pie. Together they're the whole.

📌 Exam tip: Barrier parity: in + out = vanilla (same $K$, $T$, $H$). If a question gives two of the three, the third is free marks.

**Q3.** Compared with an otherwise identical vanilla call, an average-price (Asian) call is:
- A. Dearer, because it is path-dependent
- B. Cheaper, because averaging reduces the effective volatility
- C. Identical in value by no-arbitrage
- D. Dearer, because the average is harder to manipulate

**Answer: B**

The question asks how an Asian (average-price) option compares in cost to a vanilla.

An average-price option's payoff depends on the AVERAGE price over the period, not the single final price. An average is smoother (less volatile) than the final price. Since lower volatility means lower option value, the average-price option is CHEAPER than the vanilla.

Why B is correct: averaging lowers effective volatility, making the option cheaper.

Why the others are wrong:
- A: path-dependence alone doesn't make it dearer — here the averaging effect lowers volatility and the price.
- C: they are not identical; the payoff structures differ.
- D: manipulation-resistance is a useful FEATURE, but it does not push the price UP — that's not the cost driver.

Analogy: averaging a bunch of numbers smooths out the extremes, like the average temperature for a month being far less jumpy than any single hot or cold day.

📌 Exam tip: Asian average-price options are CHEAPER than vanilla (lower effective volatility) and resist manipulation.

**Q4.** A floating lookback call option pays:
- A. $\max(S_{\max} - K, 0)$
- B. $S_{\max} - S_T$
- C. $S_T - S_{\min}$
- D. $\max(K - S_{\min}, 0)$

**Answer: C**

The question asks for the floating lookback call payoff.

A floating lookback call lets you effectively "buy at the lowest price" over the option's life. Its payoff is the final price minus the minimum price observed:
$$S_T - S_{\min}$$

Why C is correct: floating lookback call = final price − the lowest price = buying at the low.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($\max(S_{\max} - K, 0)$) is a FIXED lookback call (uses a set strike $K$ and the maximum).
- B ($S_{\max} - S_T$) is a floating lookback PUT (sell at the high).
- D ($\max(K - S_{\min}, 0)$) is a FIXED lookback put.

Analogy: a floating lookback call is like getting to rewind time and buy the stock at its cheapest moment, then value it at the end.

📌 Exam tip: FLOATING lookback uses the extreme as the strike (call: $S_T - S_{\min}$; put: $S_{\max} - S_T$). FIXED lookback uses a set $K$ with the extreme price.

**Q5.** Over an option's life, a stock is observed at 50, 45, 62, 58, and finishes at $S_T = 55$. The floating lookback call payoff is:
- A. $5
- B. $7
- C. $10
- D. $12

**Answer: C**

The question asks us to compute a floating lookback call payoff from a price path.

Step 1 — Identify the minimum observed price: from {50, 45, 62, 58, 55}, $S_{\min} = 45$.

Step 2 — Floating lookback call payoff = $S_T - S_{\min}$:
$$55 - 45 = \$10$$

Why C is correct: you "bought at the low" of $45 and the final price is $55, so payoff $= 10$.

Why the others are wrong:
- A ($5) is the vanilla call payoff with $K = 50$ ($55 - 50$), not a lookback.
- B ($7) is the floating lookback PUT ($S_{\max} - S_T = 62 - 55$).
- D ($12) is the FIXED lookback call with $K = 50$ ($S_{\max} - K = 62 - 50$).

Analogy: like buying the stock at its cheapest dip ($45) and marking it at the finish ($55) — a $10 gain.

📌 Exam tip: For floating lookback call, scan the path for the MINIMUM, then subtract it from $S_T$. Don't confuse it with the fixed version (uses $S_{\max}$ and $K$).

**Q6.** Which of these exotic options is path-dependent?
- A. Gap option
- B. Chooser option
- C. Barrier option
- D. Compound option

**Answer: C**

The question asks which exotic depends on the path the price takes, not just the final price.

A barrier option's value depends on whether the stock hits a barrier $H$ at any point during its life (knock-in comes alive, knock-out dies). Because the whole price path matters, it is path-dependent.

Why C is correct: barriers depend on whether the price touches $H$ along the way — path-dependent.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (gap option) depends only on the final price $S_T$ relative to the trigger — not path-dependent.
- B (chooser option) depends on a decision at $T_1$ and the final outcome, but its payoff structure isn't driven by the full path the way a barrier is — it's classified as not path-dependent.
- D (compound option) is an option on an option; its value isn't path-dependent in the barrier/lookback sense.

Analogy: a barrier option is like a game where touching a trip-wire at any moment changes everything — so the whole journey matters, not just where you end.

📌 Exam tip: Path-dependent exotics include barriers, lookbacks, and Asians. Gap, chooser, compound, and forward-start are NOT path-dependent.

**Q7.** A variance swap is generally preferred by dealers over a volatility swap because:
- A. It has no exposure to realized volatility at all
- B. Variance can be replicated from a static strip of vanilla calls and puts, making it easier to value and hedge
- C. It pays off only on the direction of the stock price
- D. It requires no underlying market

**Answer: B**

The question asks why dealers favor variance swaps.

A variance swap pays on realized VARIANCE ($\sigma^2$), and variance has a convenient property: it can be replicated by a fixed (static) portfolio of vanilla calls and puts across many strikes. That makes it much easier to price and hedge than a volatility swap (which pays on $\sigma$ and is harder to replicate). So dealers prefer variance swaps.

Why B is correct: static replication of variance from vanilla options makes valuation and hedging straightforward.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is false — a variance swap is precisely exposure TO realized variance (volatility squared).
- C is false — both swaps are pure volatility/variance plays with NO direction exposure.
- D is false — they rely on a market of vanilla options to hedge.

Analogy: variance is like a recipe you can assemble from standard ingredients (vanilla options) on the shelf, while volatility is a custom dish that's harder to recreate.

📌 Exam tip: Variance swaps are easier to hedge (static replication from vanilla options) → dealers prefer them. Both swaps give pure volatility exposure, no price direction.

**Q8.** Which statement about basket options is correct?
- A. A basket option is always more expensive than buying separate options on each asset
- B. A basket option is cheaper than a basket of individual options because asset correlations below 1 reduce the basket's volatility
- C. Basket options require all assets to be perfectly correlated
- D. Basket options have no diversification effect

**Answer: B**

The question asks how a basket option compares to owning options on each asset separately.

A basket option pays off on the value of a PORTFOLIO of assets. When the assets aren't perfectly correlated (correlation < 1), their ups and downs partly offset, so the basket's overall volatility is LOWER than the sum of the individual volatilities. Lower volatility means a cheaper option. So a basket option costs less than buying a separate option on each asset.

Why B is correct: imperfect correlation reduces basket volatility, making the basket option cheaper than the sum of individual options.

Why the others are wrong:
- A is the opposite — it's cheaper, not more expensive.
- C is false — basket options work precisely BECAUSE correlations are below 1 (otherwise no diversification benefit).
- D is false — the diversification effect is exactly what makes them cheaper.

Analogy: a mixed fruit basket is steadier in price than betting on each fruit separately — when apples dip, oranges might rise, smoothing things out.

📌 Exam tip: Basket option < sum of individual options, thanks to diversification (correlation < 1 lowers basket volatility).

**Q9.** A chooser option allows the holder, at time $T_1$, to:
- A. Change the strike price of the option
- B. Decide whether the option will be a call or a put (both with the same strike, expiring at $T_2$)
- C. Cancel the option for a full refund
- D. Double the size of the position

**Answer: B**

The question tests the definition of a chooser option.

A chooser option lets the holder decide at a future date $T_1$ whether the option becomes a call or a put (both struck at $K$ and expiring at $T_2$). At $T_1$ the holder picks whichever is worth more, so its value is $\max(c, p)$. Because it bundles a call and a put, it resembles (but is cheaper than) a straddle.

Why B is correct: the holder chooses call-or-put at $T_1$ — that's the defining feature.

Why the others are wrong:
- A (change the strike) describes a different feature, not a chooser.
- C (cancel for a refund) is not what a chooser does.
- D (double the size) is unrelated to the chooser's choice between call and put.

Analogy: a chooser is like a ticket that lets you decide later whether to bet on the price going up or down, once you've seen which way the wind is blowing.

📌 Exam tip: Chooser = pick call or put at $T_1$; value $= \max(c, p)$. Cheaper than a straddle because you commit to one direction.

**Q10.** Static option replication is used to hedge exotic options because:
- A. It requires constant, costly rebalancing like delta hedging
- B. It finds a fixed portfolio of vanilla options that matches the exotic on a chosen boundary, bought once and unwound only if the boundary is reached
- C. It eliminates all market risk with no positions
- D. It works only for vanilla options, never exotics

**Answer: B**

The question asks what static replication is and why it helps with exotics.

Static replication builds a FIXED portfolio of vanilla options that matches the exotic's value along a chosen boundary (for example, along a barrier and at maturity). If two portfolios agree on the whole boundary, they agree inside it too. The hedge is set up ONCE and only unwound if the boundary is reached — unlike dynamic delta hedging, which needs constant, costly rebalancing (and breaks down near barriers).

Why B is correct: it describes the fixed, boundary-matching portfolio bought once — the essence of static replication.

Why the others are wrong:
- A describes DYNAMIC delta hedging, which is exactly what static replication avoids.
- C is false — static replication uses real option positions; it doesn't conjure a hedge from nothing.
- D is false — static replication is specifically valuable for hedging EXOTICS like barriers.

Analogy: instead of constantly adjusting your umbrella all day (dynamic hedging), you pick the right rain gear once based on the forecast boundary and only change it if conditions hit that boundary.

📌 Exam tip: Static replication = a fixed vanilla-option portfolio matching the exotic on a boundary, set up once. It avoids the costly, fragile rebalancing of dynamic delta hedging near barriers.
